Amory Blaine inherited from his mother every trait, except the stray inexpressible few, that made him worth while. He came into the world with the sort of restless brilliance that attaches itself to nothing and everything at once, and his mother, Beatrice O'Hara -- a woman of extraordinary poise and quite devastating opinions -- saw to it that his education would be measured not by what he learned but by the number of things and people he could be contemptuous of. She had herself absorbed the sort of education that will be quite impossible ever again, a grand tour of European capitals and country estates, the drawing rooms of minor royalty and the salons of the intelligentsia, and she carried from each a conviction of her own immense superiority that she passed along to her son as naturally as the color of her eyes.

They traveled together, Beatrice and Amory, through the great soft afternoons of his childhood, drifting from lake resort to mountain spa, from the heavy mahogany comfort of the Blaine house in Lake Geneva to hotel suites on the Eastern seaboard, and everywhere Beatrice held court with the trembling intensity of a woman who suspected, correctly, that her beauty was beginning to fade and that her wit had better compensate. She spoke to her son as though he were an equal, which is to say she spoke to him as though he were a particularly promising audience, and by the time he was eleven Amory had developed a sort of aristocratic egotism that would have been insufferable in anyone less charming. He believed himself capable of infinite expansion for good or evil, and if the reader finds this an outrageous presumption in a child, I can only say that Amory found it perfectly natural, and that this presumption would carry him a surprisingly long way before it collapsed.

His father, Stephen Blaine, was an ineffectual, inarticulate man who drifted through the periphery of their lives like a piece of expensive furniture that no one had bothered to move. He had inherited money and a certain social position and had done nothing whatsoever to augment either, and when he died -- which he did eventually, with the quiet good taste that had characterized his life -- Amory felt a pang of something that was not quite grief, more a vague unease at the discovery that a person could vanish from the world without leaving much of a hole.

It was Monsignor Thayer Darcy who filled the role of father in any meaningful sense, though he occupied it with considerably more flair than Stephen Blaine had ever managed. Darcy was a Catholic priest of enormous personal magnetism, a man who had once moved through the highest circles of worldly society before finding God -- or rather, before God found him and dragged him, protesting elegantly, into the priesthood. He had been a great friend of Beatrice's, perhaps more than a friend in those gilded days before his conversion, and he recognized in young Amory the same restless intelligence that had made his own youth such a magnificent and terrifying thing. Their conversations, when they came, were of a kind that Amory had with no one else -- ranging from the nature of evil to the latest novels, from the architecture of cathedrals to the architecture of a well-lived life -- and Darcy became the fixed star by which Amory navigated, the one constant point in a sky of drifting constellations.

At thirteen Amory was sent to live with an aunt and uncle in Minneapolis, and for two years he learned the bitter lesson that the qualities which had made him extraordinary among Beatrice's friends made him merely peculiar among the boys of Minneapolis. He had read too much and experienced too little of the right kind of thing, and his attempts at popularity were marked by a straining self-consciousness that set him apart as surely as his mother's tutoring had. He was handsome, yes, and clever, certainly, but there was a quality of trying too hard about him that the other boys detected with the merciless precision of adolescence.

It was at St. Regis preparatory school in Connecticut that Amory first learned to package his ambitions in a form the world would accept. He arrived determined to be a success, and he achieved it -- not through any particular academic distinction, for he was lazy about his schoolwork in the way that clever boys often are, but through football and a growing talent for making himself the center of any room he entered. He discovered that the trick was not to appear to want things too desperately, that the influential man was the noncommittal man, and that a carefully cultivated air of indifference could open doors that raw eagerness never would. By the time he left St. Regis, he had become, in his own estimation at least, exactly the sort of young man who ought to go to Princeton, which is to say exactly the sort of young man Princeton had been built to receive.

And Princeton -- ah, Princeton was everything Amory had imagined and a great deal more that he hadn't. He arrived on a September afternoon when the wealth of sunshine lay across the long green swards and danced on the leaded window-panes and swam around the tops of spires and towers, and the great dreaming beauty of the place seized him with a force he had not anticipated. Here were the Gothic towers of Holder and Cleveland climbing with clear blue aspiration against skies that seemed made expressly for the purpose of framing them, and here were the elm-lined paths and the courtyards heavy with ivy and the sense, impossible to articulate but undeniable, that one had arrived at the center of something that mattered. Amory felt his heart quicken with the certainty that this was where he was meant to be, that these spires and gargoyles and shadowed quadrangles had been waiting for him specifically, which was of course exactly what every freshman felt and exactly what Princeton intended them to feel.

He set about the business of conquering the place with a systematic enthusiasm that would have impressed a military strategist. The social hierarchy at Princeton operated according to rules as rigid and as unwritten as the code of any medieval court, and Amory studied it with the same intensity he refused to bring to his coursework. There were the eating clubs, those great arbiters of social destiny that could make or destroy a man's four years, and there were the publications and the organizations and the interlocking webs of friendship and rivalry that determined who mattered and who merely existed. Amory wanted, with a wanting that went deeper than vanity, to matter.

He found his people: Kerry Holiday, reckless and generous, with whom he shared midnight conversations and a taste for the dramatic; Thomas Parke D'Invilliers, thin and bookish, a poet of genuine talent who introduced Amory to Swinburne and Brooke and the entire trembling apparatus of modern verse; and Burne Holiday, Kerry's brother, a serious-minded young man who would later trouble Amory's comfortable assumptions about what constituted a meaningful life. Together they formed the core of a circle that radiated outward through the eating clubs and the Triangle Club and the various publications until Amory found himself, by the spring of his sophomore year, exactly where he had always intended to be: at the glittering center of things.

It was during this triumphant period that he met Isabelle Borge, and the meeting carried with it all the electric inevitability of a collision that both parties have been moving toward since birth. She was visiting friends during winter break, this girl from Minneapolis with her dark beauty and her devastating self-assurance, and Amory was presented to her across a room full of other people who immediately ceased to exist. They recognized each other -- not in the mundane sense of prior acquaintance, but in the deeper sense of two vanities meeting and finding their mirror image. She was exactly as beautiful as he had hoped, and he was exactly as charming as she required, and they fell into each other with the consuming intensity of youth.

Their courtship unfolded through that Princeton spring like a slow explosion, all heated glances and whispered conversations and the delicious agony of physical proximity. Amory moved from party to party with Isabelle on his arm and the conviction in his heart that this was happiness, that this shimmering present tense was what all the striving had been for. The moonlight revels, the singing on the steps of Cottage Club, the long drives through countryside going blue with evening -- he wanted to fix each moment in amber, to preserve it against the dimming that he sensed, even then, was coming.

For it did come, the dimming. Their relationship, which had blazed so brightly in the proximity of Princeton weekends, could not sustain itself across the long silences between visits. Letters were exchanged, then fewer letters, then letters with a perfunctory quality that neither quite acknowledged. When they met again, something had shifted -- she seemed sharper to him, more calculating, and he appeared to her as less substantial than the figure she had constructed in his absence. Their final encounter ended in mutual recrimination, a quarrel that was really about the discovery that they had each been in love not with the other person but with the idea of being in love, which is a very different thing and much harder to sustain.

His academic fortunes declined in direct proportion to his social ascent, and by the end of his sophomore year Amory found himself in the peculiar position of being one of the most popular men on campus and one of the least distinguished in the classroom. It was a tension that bothered him in his more reflective moments, but Amory's reflective moments tended to be brief and comfortable, interrupted by the next party or the next conversation or the next opportunity to be admired. His father's death during this period registered as a distant concern, primarily financial -- the Blaine fortune, never as robust as Beatrice's manner had suggested, was dwindling with a quiet insistence that would eventually prove catastrophic.

It was Burne Holiday who first cracked the lacquered surface of Amory's self-satisfaction. Burne had always been the quieter of the two Holiday brothers, more watchful, less inclined toward the charming recklessness that made Kerry such good company, and in their junior year he underwent a transformation that Amory observed with a mixture of admiration and alarm. Burne began to question everything -- the eating clubs, the social hierarchy, the entire elaborate apparatus of privilege that Princeton had constructed to sort its young men into categories of worth. He renounced his club membership, took up pacifism with a convert's fervor, and spoke with an earnestness that made the other students uneasy, as earnestness always does in places where cleverness is the preferred currency. Amory could not follow Burne into his radicalism -- he was too fond of the world's good opinion for that -- but something in Burne's example lodged in him like a splinter, the uncomfortable suggestion that a life spent pursuing popularity might not be a life spent pursuing anything at all.

There was also Clara Page, his widowed cousin in Philadelphia, whom Amory visited during this unsettled period and promptly fell half in love with. Clara was the kind of woman who restored one's faith in the possibility of genuine goodness -- lovely, serene, raising two small children alone with a grace that made her poverty seem almost elegant. She possessed a quality that Amory, for all his cleverness, could not quite name: a centeredness, a calm self-knowledge that had nothing to do with egotism and everything to do with having suffered and having come through the suffering intact. He proposed to her, or rather he circled the idea of proposing with enough transparent longing that Clara was forced to address it directly, and she refused him with a kindness that only made him love her more. She told him, with the gentle precision of a woman who has learned to see clearly, that he did not really love her -- that he loved the idea of her, the way a man in a storm loves the idea of a harbor. And she was right, which Amory recognized even as it stung, because Clara was always right about the things that mattered most.

There were stranger things, too, during these Princeton years -- things that Amory could never quite explain and that he carried with him afterward like evidence of a world operating beneath the surface of the one he could see. On more than one occasion he encountered what he could only describe as the presence of evil, a figure glimpsed in doorways or felt pressing against the edges of a room, something with pale features and an air of terrible familiarity that filled him with a dread more profound than anything in his daylight experience could account for. He saw it -- or felt it, for the distinction between seeing and feeling grew uncertain at these moments -- after an evening of excess, a devil's face materializing in the shadows with the calm authority of something that had been waiting for him. These episodes shook Amory in ways he could not articulate to his Princeton friends, who would have found such talk embarrassing, but he wrote of them to Monsignor Darcy, and Darcy, to his credit, did not dismiss them. The Monsignor understood -- had perhaps experienced something similar in his own youth -- that there were forces in the world that operated beyond the reach of reason, and that a young man's encounter with evil was as real and as consequential as his encounter with love.

The war came, as wars do, at exactly the wrong moment. America entered the great conflagration in 1917, and the Princeton campus, which had seemed so eternal in its beauty and its rituals, was suddenly emptied of its young men as efficiently as if someone had pulled a plug. Kerry Holiday enlisted with the cheerful recklessness that characterized everything he did, and Amory followed -- not from patriotism exactly, nor from any clearly articulated sense of duty, but because staying behind while others went seemed impossible for a young man who had built his entire identity on being at the center of whatever was happening.

The war itself occupied a strange lacuna in Amory's life, a period he would later remember in fragments: the tedium of training camps, the absurdity of military routine, the occasional terrifying proximity of death, and above all the sense that the world he had known -- the world of Princeton spires and debutante balls and long golden afternoons -- had been irrevocably altered. His mother died during this interval, Beatrice who had shaped him and spoiled him and made him believe he was extraordinary, and her death left him more alone than he had ever been and considerably poorer than he had imagined. The Blaine finances, it transpired, had been managed with a carelessness that matched Beatrice's approach to everything else, and what remained was barely enough to sustain the appearance of the life Amory had always assumed was his birthright.

He returned from France in the spring of 1919, discharged into a world that bore little resemblance to the one he had left, and took up residence in New York with his friends Tom D'Invilliers and Alec Connage. The city in those first postwar months had a fevered quality, a sense of desperate gaiety that Amory found simultaneously exhilarating and disturbing. Everyone was drinking more than they should and dancing longer than was wise and speaking with a brittle cleverness that covered, he suspected, a great deal of emptiness. He took a position at an advertising agency -- the phrase itself felt like a small death to a young man who had imagined himself destined for literature or politics or at the very least something that didn't involve writing copy about laundry soap -- and settled into the business of being young and poor in Manhattan.

And then there was Rosalind.

Rosalind Connage arrived in his life like a force of nature, which is not an exaggeration but a precise description of the effect she produced. She was Alec's sister, and Amory first encountered her in the Connage family home on a spring evening when the windows were open and the curtains were moving in a warm breeze and Rosalind came down the stairs in a dress that caught the light and did something complicated to his breathing. She had glorious yellow hair and an eternal kissable mouth, small and slightly sensual and utterly disturbing, and a way of looking at a man that suggested she found him simultaneously fascinating and faintly ridiculous. She was, in short, everything.

Their courtship was conducted at the velocity of two people who recognized, beneath the banter and the cleverness, that they had found something rare. Amory had been in love before, or had thought he had, but Rosalind made his feelings for Isabelle seem like a sketch compared to an oil painting. She was witty and self-possessed and ruthlessly honest, and she had a gift for romantic conversation that turned every exchange into a kind of verbal dance. She told him she had kissed dozens of men and supposed she would kiss dozens more, and he found even this declaration enchanting, because when Rosalind said it, it sounded not like promiscuity but like a statement of imperial prerogative.

They fell in love completely, the way one can only fall in love at twenty-three, with a wholeness and an abandon that left no room for caution. For a few weeks in that spring of 1919, Amory was happier than he had ever been or would ever be again, and he knew it even as it was happening, which is both the gift and the curse of a certain kind of intelligence. He would walk with Rosalind through Central Park in the long May evenings and feel the future opening before them like a landscape seen from a great height, beautiful and vast and full of possibility.

But Rosalind was also practical in the way that very beautiful women can afford to be, which is to say she understood with perfect clarity what beauty could purchase and what poverty could not. She loved Amory -- of this he was certain, and of this she was too -- but she loved also the life she had been born into, the life of fine things and comfortable rooms and the assumption that money would always be there to smooth the rough places. And Amory could not give her that. He was working at an advertising agency for a salary that would not have covered Rosalind's monthly florist bills, and the Blaine fortune was gone, irretrievably gone, and all that remained was his charm and his intelligence and his capacity for love, which are excellent things but which do not pay the rent.

She told him on a June evening, with the gentleness of someone performing an amputation, that she was going to marry Dawson Ryder. Ryder was wealthy and dependable and perfectly nice, and he could give her the life she needed, and she was sorry, she was so terribly sorry, but there it was. Amory listened and felt something break inside him with an almost audible snap, some load-bearing structure that had supported his entire conception of how the world worked, and when he walked out into the street afterward he understood for the first time what people meant when they said their heart was broken, because it was not a metaphor at all but a precise description of a physical sensation located somewhere behind his sternum.

What followed was a period of dissolution that Amory would later recall with a mixture of shame and a strange distant tenderness, the way one regards the suffering of a younger self who didn't know any better. He drank -- not with the convivial enthusiasm of the Princeton years but with the grim determination of a man trying to obliterate something. He drank in speakeasies and hotel bars and in his small apartment on the East Side, and the alcohol did what it was supposed to do, which was to blur the sharp edges of his loss into something manageable, though it did this less and less effectively as the weeks wore on. His friends watched with the helpless concern of people who know that grief must run its course, and Amory let it run, sinking deeper into a lethargy that was equal parts heartbreak and self-pity.

He emerged from this darkness slowly, the way one climbs out of a deep pool, and it was during a visit to Maryland that summer that he met Eleanor Savage. He heard her before he saw her -- a voice singing in a haystack as a thunderstorm gathered overhead, which is the kind of introduction that only happens in novels and in the lives of people who are destined to make a dramatic impression on each other. Eleanor was beautiful, of course, because all the women in Amory's life were beautiful, but she was beautiful in a way that was different from Isabelle's polished charm or Rosalind's devastating glamour. She was wild, this girl, with a recklessness about her that suggested she had looked at the world's conventions and found them not merely boring but actually contemptible.

They spent a summer together that had the quality of a fever dream, all poetry readings and moonlit rides and conversations about God and death and the fundamental absurdity of human existence. Eleanor was an atheist with the passionate intensity that only the very young bring to their disbelief, and she challenged Amory's Catholic faith -- the faith that Monsignor Darcy had so carefully nurtured -- with an intellectual ferocity that he found both thrilling and unsettling. They were alike, he and Eleanor, in their egotism and their romanticism and their conviction that they were somehow set apart from the common run of humanity, and this likeness drew them together with the force of mutual recognition.

But there was a darkness in Eleanor that went deeper than intellectual rebellion, a self-destructive streak that revealed itself on their last night together with a violence that Amory had not anticipated. They quarreled -- about marriage, about faith, about the future -- and Eleanor, in a fit of rage or despair or some combustion of the two, rode her horse toward a cliff's edge at full gallop, as though she intended to hurl herself and the animal into the void. She stopped at the last possible moment, the horse rearing and screaming against the night sky, and Amory stood watching with his heart in his throat and the sudden cold understanding that Eleanor was not merely wild but genuinely dangerous, to herself and to anyone who loved her. They parted the next morning with the exhausted tenderness of two people who have survived something terrible together, and Amory knew, walking away from her, that whatever he had felt for Eleanor was real but that it was not love -- not the kind of love that sustains, that builds something. It was something more like recognition, and recognition, however intoxicating, is not enough.

The autumn brought him to Atlantic City, where an incident occurred that would mark the final stage of Amory's education in the difference between the person he was and the person he wished to be. He encountered his friend Alec Connage at a hotel, and Alec had brought with him a young woman who was not his wife, and through a series of circumstances that had the awful inevitability of a closing trap, the situation threatened to erupt into scandal. A house detective appeared, that most dreaded figure of hotel melodrama, and Amory -- in a moment of selfless impulse that surprised even himself -- took the blame. He claimed the woman was with him, not with Alec, and he did this not from any expectation of gratitude or any calculation of advantage but simply because Alec had a family who would be destroyed by the revelation and Amory had no one. It was, perhaps, the first genuinely unselfish act of his life, and it cost him what remained of his social reputation, which by that point was not a great deal but was all he had.

And then Monsignor Darcy died.

The news reached Amory like a blow struck in the dark, unexpected and devastating, and it carried with it a grief far deeper than anything he had felt at the death of his own father. Darcy had been his compass, his conscience, the one person who had understood both the grandeur and the absurdity of Amory's ambitions, and without him the world seemed to lose its organizing principle. Amory read the letter announcing Darcy's death and felt something close over him -- not despair exactly, but a kind of terrible clarity, the recognition that every fixed point in his life had now been removed and that he was, for the first time, truly and completely alone.

His finances, too, had reached their terminal point. The last remnants of the Blaine fortune had evaporated through a combination of bad investments and legal complications, and Amory found himself in the extraordinary position of being a Princeton man with no money, no prospects, and no one in the world who was obligated to care whether he lived or died. It was a position that clarified things wonderfully.

He decided to walk to Princeton -- not for any practical reason, but because movement seemed preferable to stillness and because Princeton was the last place where he had been wholly happy, or at least wholly convinced that happiness was possible. Along the way, he was picked up by the car of a wealthy man who turned out to be the father of Jesse Ferrenby, a friend who had died in the war. They rode together through the New Jersey countryside, and Amory, emboldened by his poverty and his grief and the liberating sensation of having nothing left to lose, launched into a passionate defense of socialism that would have horrified the Amory Blaine of five years earlier. He argued for the redistribution of wealth, for the dismantling of the very class structure that had formed him, and the older man listened with the patient skepticism of someone who has heard young men hold forth on the injustices of the world before and knows that most of them will eventually inherit money and change their minds.

But Amory was sincere, or believed himself to be, which in a young man of twenty-three amounts to the same thing. The war had destroyed something in him -- not his ambition, exactly, but the comfortable assumption that the social order which had produced him was natural and right and permanent. He had seen too many of his friends die for reasons that no one could adequately explain, and he had loved a woman who had chosen money over love, and he had watched his own fortune dissolve like sugar in rain, and these experiences had left him with a conviction that the world was organized badly and that someone ought to do something about it, even if that someone was an unemployed ex-advertising man with a talent for self-dramatization.

He arrived at Princeton as evening was falling, and the campus received him with the same Gothic beauty that had seized his heart as a freshman, the spires and towers rising against a sky going violet with dusk. But he saw it differently now, this place that had meant everything to him. He saw it as a monument to a world that was passing, a beautiful anachronism sustained by money and tradition and the stubborn belief that certain kinds of young men were more important than other kinds of young men. He loved it still -- he would always love it -- but he loved it now with the complicated, slightly rueful love that one feels for something one has outgrown.

He walked the familiar paths and felt the ghosts of his former selves walking beside him: the eager freshman, the triumphant sophomore, the young man who had believed that social conquest was the highest achievement life had to offer. Those selves seemed very distant now, separated from the present Amory by the war and by Rosalind and by Monsignor Darcy's death and by the long, slow process of discovering that the world was larger and more indifferent and more complicated than he had ever imagined. He thought of all the things he had wanted -- popularity, love, money, a sense of purpose -- and of how few of them he had managed to hold onto, and he felt not bitterness exactly but a kind of exhausted wonder at the gap between expectation and reality.

The great question, of course, was what came next, and Amory found that he had no answer. The egotism that had propelled him through Princeton and through the war and through the wreckage of his love affairs was still there, still burning with its old intensity, but it had been refined by suffering into something less grandiose and more honest. He was no longer the romantic egotist who believed himself destined for greatness in some vaguely defined but certainly magnificent form. He was something more modest and more durable: a young man who had been stripped of his illusions and his money and his certainties and who was left with nothing but himself.

And perhaps that was enough. Perhaps the whole long painful education of his twenties -- the failed romances, the lost friends, the vanished fortune, the death of his mentor -- had been pointing toward this single, austere conclusion: that the self was the only thing one truly possessed, and that knowing it honestly, without flattery or evasion, was the beginning of wisdom if not the whole of it. He did not know what he would do with this knowledge. He did not know if it would lead him to the great achievements he had once imagined or merely to a more dignified form of failure. He knew only that it was his, this hard-won self-awareness, and that it was real in a way that his Princeton triumphs and his advertising career and even his love for Rosalind had not been real.

He stood on the Princeton campus in the gathering dark and stretched his arms toward the crystalline, radiant sky. He did not look like a figure of triumph -- a young man alone, without money or position or the woman he loved, standing in the shadow of towers that no longer represented the pinnacle of his aspiration. But there was something in his bearing that suggested he had arrived, finally, at a place that mattered, even if he could not yet say where it was or what it meant. He had come through the fire of youth and emerged not unscathed but alive, not wise perhaps but aware, and awareness, he was beginning to understand, was the necessary precondition for everything else.

It was not much, this knowledge. He would have preferred love, or money, or the certainty that God existed, or at the very least a clear sense of what he was supposed to do on Monday morning. But it was his, and it was real, and in a world that had shown itself to be careless with the things he valued, the possession of something real and unassailable seemed to Amory Blaine a thing worth having.

He knew himself -- he was certain of that now, after all the years of posturing and pretension, after Isabelle and Rosalind and Eleanor, after Princeton and the war and Monsignor Darcy's grave. He knew himself, and the knowledge sat in him with the weight and the clarity of a stone dropped into still water. But that was all. That was all he knew, and the admission carried in it both the humility of a man who has abandoned his illusions and the stubborn pride of one who refuses to replace them with someone else's. He was Amory Blaine, and he knew himself, and the rest -- the vast, terrifying, beautiful rest of it -- remained to be discovered.

The towers of Princeton rose behind him, dark against the stars, and ahead of him stretched the road that led away from everything he had been and toward everything he might become, and Amory walked forward into the darkness with the curious, unquenchable conviction that he was, despite everything, just beginning.
