The night was bitter cold, and the snow fell soft and silent on the Fist of the First Men, where the brothers of the Night's Watch huddled in their cloaks and cursed the gods who had brought them to this forsaken place. Chett had murder in his heart. He and a dozen like-minded men meant to slit the Lord Commander's throat and desert before the dawn, flee south to some place where the cold could not reach them and the dead did not walk. He dreamed of killing Samwell Tarly most of all, the fat craven who had stolen his place at Maester Aemon's side. But the snow changed everything. Snow meant tracks, and tracks meant pursuit, and so the plot unraveled even as it was conceived. And then the horns sounded. One blast for rangers returning. Two for wildlings. Three... three blasts had not been heard in living memory, not in a thousand years. Three meant Others. The dead were coming, and all of Chett's petty hatreds meant less than nothing in the face of what emerged from that frozen darkness.

The battle at the Fist was a slaughter. The wights came in their hundreds, their thousands, dead men with blue eyes and cold black hands, and the Night's Watch broke before them like a dam before a flood. The survivors fled south through the haunted forest in a ragged column, starving and frostbitten and half-mad with terror. It was at Craster's Keep that the worst of it happened -- old Craster, that vile man who wed his daughters and gave his sons to the cold, offered the Watch a grudging shelter. But hungry men are dangerous men, and when Craster taunted them one time too many, a knife found his belly. Lord Commander Jeor Mormont, the Old Bear himself, tried to restore order and died for it, murdered by his own sworn brothers in the smoke and chaos of that wretched hall. The mutiny left the Watch shattered, and it fell to Samwell Tarly, of all people -- fat, frightened, bookish Sam -- to lead the wildling girl Gilly and her newborn babe south through the snow. Sam, who could scarcely hold a sword without trembling, had already done what no man had done in eight thousand years. On the flight from the Fist, when a White Walker had come for them with its pale sword gleaming like starlight, Sam had stabbed it with a blade of dragonglass, and the creature had melted away like so much mist. They called him Sam the Slayer after that, though he wore the name like a jape he did not understand.

Far to the south and east, where the Narrow Sea gave way to the sweltering heat of Slaver's Bay, Daenerys Targaryen was building an army. She had come to Astapor seeking the Unsullied, those famed eunuch soldiers who knew neither fear nor mercy, trained from boyhood in the most brutal fashion imaginable. The slavers of Astapor were proud of their wares and cruel in their commerce, and they looked upon this silver-haired girl with her three small dragons and saw only gold. They offered her eight thousand Unsullied in exchange for the largest of her dragons, the black beast called Drogon. Daenerys agreed, and the Good Masters of Astapor thought themselves very clever indeed. They were wrong. When the exchange was made and the slave masters handed her the whip that symbolized command, Daenerys spoke a single word in High Valyrian -- *dracarys* -- and Drogon bathed the slaver in black fire. The Unsullied turned their spears on their former masters at her command, and Astapor burned. She freed every slave in the city and told the Unsullied they could leave if they wished, that she would have no slaves in her service. They stayed. They chose her, and she loved them for it.

From Astapor she marched on Yunkai, the Yellow City, where the Wise Masters trembled behind their walls and hired sellsword companies to defend them. One such company, the Second Sons, sent a captain named Daario Naharis to treat with her, and Daario, taken by her beauty and her fire, killed his fellow captains and brought their heads to Daenerys as a gift. Yunkai fell without the need for dragons, and the freed slaves poured from the city gates crying *mhysa*, mother, reaching for Daenerys as though she were a goddess come to earth. She meant to go further still, to Meereen, the greatest of the slaver cities, where the Great Masters had nailed one hundred and sixty-three slave children to posts along the road as a warning. Daenerys counted every one.

Yet even as she freed the enslaved and burned the masters, treachery festered in her own camp. Ser Barristan Selmy, the legendary knight of the Kingsguard, had come to her in disguise, calling himself Arstan Whitebeard. And Ser Jorah Mormont, her most trusted advisor, the man who had stood beside her since the beginning, had once sold her secrets to the Spider, Varys, back in King's Landing. When the truth came out, Daenerys was forced to judge them both. Jorah she banished, for his betrayal cut deepest -- he had been closest to her heart. Barristan she forgave, for his service to her father and his honest shame. Meereen fell through cunning rather than siege; her men crept through the sewers and freed the slaves within, who rose against their masters. And when it was done, Daenerys looked upon the city she had taken and made a choice that surprised even herself. She would not march west to Westeros. She would stay. She would rule. She would learn what it meant to be a queen, here in Meereen, with the blood of a hundred and sixty-three masters drying on the plaza where she had ordered them nailed to posts in answer for the children.

In the riverlands, the war had made a wasteland of what had once been fertile country. Arya Stark wandered those ravaged lands like a ghost, a girl of ten who had seen more death than most soldiers see in a lifetime. She fell in with the Brotherhood Without Banners, outlaws who claimed to fight for the smallfolk in the name of King Robert, though Robert was long dead. Their leader was Lord Beric Dondarrion, a man who had died six times and been brought back each time by the red priest Thoros of Myr. Arya watched Beric fight the Hound, Sandor Clegane, in a trial by combat -- watched the lightning lord's flaming sword and the Hound's desperate fury -- and when the Hound split Beric's skull and killed him, she watched Thoros kneel and pray, and Beric rose again with the wound still fresh upon him. The Brotherhood meant to ransom Arya to her brother Robb, but before they could, the Hound stole her away.

Sandor Clegane was a hard and bitter man, scarred in body and soul, and he had no love for anyone save perhaps the memory of a world that had never existed. But he kept Arya alive when others would have left her in a ditch, and she kept him sharp with her tongue when silence might have served her better. He meant to take her to the Twins, where her brother Robb and her mother Catelyn were attending a wedding. They arrived too late, and too early, and what they found at that castle by the river was the end of everything Arya had hoped for.

But first, the matters that had led to that bloody night must be told. Robb Stark, the Young Wolf, King in the North, had won every battle and was losing the war. His fatal error had been made before the story began -- he had broken his pledge to marry a daughter of Lord Walder Frey, wedding instead Jeyne Westerling, a minor lord's daughter from the west. It was an act born of honor, or of passion, or of youthful folly, and it cost him everything. Lord Walder Frey was not a man who forgave such slights. He was old and petty and venomous, a man who counted every slight and hoarded every grudge, and he had been nursing this one like a canker.

Robb needed the Freys. He needed their bridge and their swords, and so he went to the Twins to make amends. His uncle Edmure Tully would wed one of Lord Walder's daughters in his place -- a handsome offering, for Edmure was Lord of Riverrun, and any daughter who wed him would be a great lady. The Freys accepted, and the wedding went forward. The hall was hung with banners, the wine flowed freely, and the music played. Edmure's bride proved fair, and for a time it seemed as though the breach might truly be mended.

It was a lie. All of it, from the first smile to the last cup of wine. When the doors were barred and the musicians in the gallery drew crossbows instead of bows, when the Frey men-at-arms turned on Robb's bannermen with naked steel, the true nature of Walder Frey's hospitality was revealed. They called it the Red Wedding, after, for the blood that ran like rivers across the floor of that accursed hall. Robb's bannermen were butchered as they feasted, drunk and unarmed. Smalljon Umber died trying to shield his king with a table. Dacey Mormont fell with a crossbow bolt in her stomach. Outside, the camps were attacked, and Robb's direwolf Grey Wind, chained in a kennel, was shot with crossbows. Catelyn Stark watched her son die. She watched Roose Bolton drive a dagger into Robb's heart, saying *Jaime Lannister sends his regards*. In her madness and grief, Catelyn clawed her own face until the blood ran down her cheeks, and then they cut her throat and threw her body in the river, a mockery of the Tully funeral custom she had honored all her life.

Outside, Arya saw the slaughter begin. She saw Grey Wind's body, saw the men with Frey and Bolton sigils cutting down Stark soldiers, and she wanted to run inside, to find her mother, to do something, anything. The Hound stopped her. He hit her with the flat of his axe and carried her away into the darkness. It was not kindness, exactly, but it was the thing that saved her life.

Roose Bolton had betrayed his king for a promise -- Tywin Lannister had offered him the position of Warden of the North, and Bolton, ever the cold and calculating man, had accepted. The Freys had been promised protection and reward. The old alliance of the North was broken, shattered in a single night of blood and treachery, and the architects of the massacre would call it justice, or necessity, or the fortunes of war.

In King's Landing, the Lannisters reigned supreme, or so it seemed. Tywin Lannister, the most powerful man in Westeros, had returned to the capital as Hand of the King and set about consolidating his family's grip on the Iron Throne. Joffrey was king in name, but it was Tywin who ruled, and everyone who mattered knew it. The old lion had plans for everyone. He forced his son Tyrion to marry Sansa Stark, so that the Lannisters might claim Winterfell through her. Sansa, who had endured so much cruelty at Joffrey's hands, found herself wed to the one Lannister who had shown her something approaching kindness -- but she could not love him, and Tyrion, for all his wit and wounded pride, could not make her. He refused to consummate the marriage, telling her he would not force himself upon her, and they lived together in a cold and awkward truce.

Joffrey, meanwhile, was to wed Margaery Tyrell, binding the powerful Reach to the Iron Throne. The Tyrells had come to King's Landing with their armies and their gold and their ambitions, and Margaery was everything Joffrey's first betrothed had not been -- clever, charming, beloved by the smallfolk. Her grandmother, Olenna Tyrell, the Queen of Thorns, was the true power behind House Tyrell, a sharp-tongued old woman who saw the world with merciless clarity.

The wedding feast was a grand affair, as lavish as only Lannister gold and Tyrell ambition could make it. Seventy-seven courses, singers and jugglers and a troupe of jousting dwarfs that Joffrey had commissioned specifically to humiliate his uncle Tyrion. The boy king was cruel even on his wedding day, pouring wine over Tyrion's head and forcing him to serve as his cupbearer. And then Joffrey raised his cup and drank, and the wine caught in his throat. His face went purple. He clawed at his neck, gasping, choking, and the blood came from his nose and his eyes, and he died there on the floor of the great hall, in his mother's arms, pointing an accusing finger at his uncle. Cersei screamed for the guards to seize Tyrion. She called it murder, called Tyrion a kinslayer and a monster, and Tyrion, standing there with the poisoned chalice in his hands and the dwarf joust still ringing in his ears, could do nothing but stare at the dead boy who had made his life a hell.

They called it the Purple Wedding. The poison had been smuggled into the feast in a stone from Sansa's hairnet -- a hairnet that Dontos Hollard, the drunken fool, had given her at the behest of Petyr Baelish. It was Olenna Tyrell who had plucked the poisoned stone from Sansa's hair and dropped it into Joffrey's wine, though Sansa did not know it at the time. Littlefinger had orchestrated the whole affair from afar, using the Tyrells as his instruments and Sansa as his unwitting accomplice.

In the chaos that followed Joffrey's death, Dontos spirited Sansa away from the feast and brought her to a ship in the harbor, where Littlefinger waited. Petyr Baelish, who had risen from the minor lordship of a few stony acres to become one of the most dangerous men in the Seven Kingdoms, told Sansa the truth of what had happened -- or as much truth as a man like Littlefinger ever told. He had Dontos killed with a crossbow bolt, for dead men tell no tales, and he took Sansa to the Eyrie, the mountain fortress of the Vale of Arryn, where her aunt Lysa waited.

Lysa Arryn was half-mad with jealousy and obsession. She had loved Littlefinger since they were children together at Riverrun, and she had done terrible things for that love. It was Lysa who had poisoned her own husband, Jon Arryn, the Hand of the King, at Littlefinger's bidding. It was Lysa who had written the letter to Catelyn naming the Lannisters as murderers, the letter that had set the entire war in motion. All of it -- the war, the death, the suffering of half a realm -- could be traced back to Petyr Baelish's whispered instructions and Lysa Arryn's desperate, deluded love. Littlefinger married Lysa and bided his time. When Lysa, in a fit of jealous rage, tried to push Sansa through the Moon Door, Littlefinger intervened. He told Lysa he had only ever loved one woman -- her sister Cat. And then he pushed her through the Moon Door himself, and she fell screaming into the sky.

Tyrion's trial was a farce from the start. Cersei wanted him dead, and she assembled a parade of witnesses to testify against him, each one lying or twisting the truth to paint Tyrion as a murderer and a traitor. The worst of it was Shae. Shae, who had shared his bed and whispered sweetness in the dark, who had called him her lion and made him believe, for a time, that someone might love him despite everything -- Shae took the stand and lied. She told the court that Tyrion and Sansa had plotted together to murder the king. She called him her *giant of Lannister*, those words that had been their private jest, and turned them into a weapon before the whole court. Tyrion's heart broke in that moment, though he would not show it. He turned to the assembled lords and ladies and told them what he truly thought of them, told them he wished he had poisoned Joffrey, told them he wished he could poison them all. He demanded trial by combat.

Prince Oberyn Martell of Dorne offered to be Tyrion's champion. Oberyn had his own reasons -- he had come to King's Landing seeking justice for his sister Elia, who had been raped and murdered by Ser Gregor Clegane during the sack of the city at the end of Robert's Rebellion. Gregor Clegane, the Mountain That Rides, was Cersei's chosen champion, and Oberyn wanted nothing more than to face the man who had killed his sister and her children. The duel was fought in the yard before the whole court. Oberyn was quick and graceful, dancing around the Mountain with his poisoned spear, opening cuts on the huge knight's arms and legs. He demanded a confession. *You raped her. You murdered her. You killed her children.* Again and again he said it, driving the spear into Clegane's flesh, and it seemed that Oberyn would win, that justice would be done. But Oberyn wanted too much. He wanted the confession more than the victory, and when he pressed too close, the Mountain caught him. Gregor Clegane crushed Oberyn's skull with his bare hands, even as Oberyn's poisoned spear had done its slow and terrible work. Both men fell. Oberyn died in the yard, and Gregor Clegane would die screaming in the days that followed, though it took him a long time about it. Tyrion was condemned to death.

It was Jaime who saved him. Jaime Lannister, the Kingslayer, who had lost his sword hand and found something like a conscience in its place. Jaime's journey through this tale was its own kind of transformation. Catelyn Stark had freed him from his cell at Riverrun and sent him south with Brienne of Tarth, a huge and homely woman warrior sworn to Catelyn's service, in exchange for a promise to return her daughters. Jaime and Brienne were captured by the Brave Companions, a sellsword company in the employ of Roose Bolton, commanded by the lisping Vargo Hoat. It was Hoat who ordered Jaime's hand cut off -- his sword hand, the hand that had made him the finest swordsman in the Seven Kingdoms. They took it with a single stroke of an arakh and hung it around his neck on a cord, and Jaime Lannister, who had defined himself by his skill with a blade since he was a boy of fifteen, was unmade.

What grew in the place of that lost hand was something unexpected. Stripped of his strength, his beauty, his identity as a warrior, Jaime was forced to confront who he truly was. In the baths of Harrenhal, feverish and half-delirious, he told Brienne the truth of why he had killed the Mad King -- not for ambition or treachery, but to save the half a million people of King's Landing from the wildfire Aerys had hidden beneath the city. It was the secret he had carried for seventeen years, the truth behind the name Kingslayer, and he gave it to this woman he had mocked and insulted because she was the only person who might understand. When Hoat threw Brienne into a bear pit for sport, Jaime went back for her. He leapt into the pit unarmed and one-handed and stood between her and the bear until Bolton's men could pull them out. It was not the act of the man he had been.

Jaime returned to King's Landing a changed man and found it much the same. He freed Tyrion from his cell on the night before the execution, and as they parted, Jaime told his brother a truth that Tywin had buried years ago -- that Tysha, Tyrion's first wife, the crofter's daughter he had loved, had not been a whore as Tywin had claimed. She had been exactly what she seemed, an innocent girl who loved Tyrion, and Tywin had forced his guardsmen to rape her and made Tyrion watch. The revelation shattered something in Tyrion that had been cracking for a very long time.

Before fleeing the city, Tyrion climbed to the Tower of the Hand. He found Shae in his father's bed, naked beneath the gold coverlet, and a crossbow mounted on the wall. Shae tried to speak, tried to call him her giant of Lannister one final time, but Tyrion wrapped the gold chain of the Hand's office around her throat and strangled her. Then he took the crossbow and found his father on the privy. Tywin Lannister, who had orchestrated the Red Wedding, who had destroyed House Reyne and House Tarbeck, who had ruled the Seven Kingdoms from behind the Iron Throne for decades, died on the privy with a crossbow bolt in his belly. Tyrion told him that Tysha had not been a whore. Tywin said the word *whore* one last time, and Tyrion put a second bolt through his heart. Varys smuggled Tyrion out of the city in a cask, bound for the Free Cities and exile.

North of the Wall, Jon Snow was living a lie. He had gone over to the wildlings on the orders of Qhorin Halfhand, killing the ranger to prove his loyalty to Mance Rayder, the King-Beyond-the-Wall. Mance was no monster, as it turned out, but a former brother of the Night's Watch who had traded his black cloak for freedom. He was charismatic and clever, a man who had united a hundred warring clans and tribes under a single banner through sheer force of will. His army was vast -- a hundred thousand wildlings, with giants and mammoths among them -- but they were not soldiers. They were a people fleeing, running from the cold and the dead things that walked in the cold.

Jon walked among them and learned their ways. He climbed the Wall itself with a band of wildling raiders, an ascent of seven hundred feet of sheer ice that nearly killed him. And he fell in love with Ygritte, a flame-haired spearwife who teased him and challenged him and warmed his bed in the cold nights beyond the Wall. *You know nothing, Jon Snow*, she told him, again and again, and there was more truth in it than either of them knew. When the time came to choose, Jon could not kill an innocent man to prove his loyalty to the wildlings, and he fled. Ygritte put three arrows in him before he escaped, and he rode for Castle Black bleeding from three wounds and carrying a heart heavier than the Wall itself.

The defense of the Wall was Jon Snow's finest hour and his hardest trial. With the Lord Commander dead and the Watch in disarray, Jon took command by necessity and sheer force of will. The wildlings attacked from both sides -- Mance's great host from the north, and a smaller band of raiders who had climbed the Wall from the south. Jon fought on the Wall and in the tunnels beneath it. He held the gate when the giant Mag the Mighty came with his mammoth to batter it down, and when the giant broke through, it was Donal Noye, the one-armed blacksmith, who held the inner gate and died killing Mag in the darkness.

And Ygritte died. She fell in the battle with an arrow in her chest -- Jon did not know whose arrow, and it did not matter. He held her as she died, and she told him they should have stayed in that cave, the cave where they had made love in the warm water with the firelight playing on the walls. *You know nothing, Jon Snow*, she said, and then she was gone.

Stannis Baratheon arrived at the Wall like a hammer blow from the gods. His army swept down on the wildling host from the north, scattering Mance's undisciplined horde and capturing the King-Beyond-the-Wall himself. Stannis had come at the urging of Davos Seaworth, his Hand, who had been moved by a letter from the Night's Watch begging for aid. Davos, the onion knight, the former smuggler who had lost his fingertips and gained a lordship, had been on his own journey through the pages of this tale. He had been plucked from a barren rock in Blackwater Bay where he had washed up after the great battle, half-dead and sun-scorched. He returned to Dragonstone to find Stannis in the grip of Melisandre, the red priestess who burned men alive as offerings to her god R'hllor.

Melisandre had seen victory in her flames, and she had power, there was no denying it. She had birthed a shadow that killed Renly Baratheon. She had burned the leeches filled with the bastard king's blood and spoken the names of Stannis's rivals -- Robb Stark, Balon Greyjoy, Joffrey Baratheon -- and one by one they died, though whether by her magic or mere coincidence, no man could say. Now she wanted to burn Edric Storm, King Robert's bastard son, to wake a dragon from stone. Davos could not allow it. He smuggled the boy away in the dead of night, and when Stannis would have executed him for it, Davos showed him the letter from the Wall. The true war is not between kings, Davos argued. The true war is in the north, against the cold and the dead. And Stannis, whatever else he was -- stubborn, humorless, rigid, just -- Stannis listened. He sailed north, and the Wall was saved.

In the aftermath, Stannis offered Jon Snow something he had wanted his entire life -- legitimacy. He would make Jon a Stark, Lord of Winterfell, if Jon would bend the knee and swear fealty. It was the dream Jon had carried since boyhood, the name he had never been allowed to bear, the home that had never quite been his. He nearly accepted. But the old gods had their own plans, and Jon could not burn the godswood at Winterfell to please Melisandre's red god. He refused Stannis. And when Samwell Tarly, working behind the scenes with quiet cunning that surprised everyone, arranged the election, Jon Snow was chosen as the 998th Lord Commander of the Night's Watch. He was a boy of seventeen, and the Wall was his to hold against the darkness.

Bran Stark's journey was a quieter thing, but no less strange. The crippled boy who had once dreamed of being a knight traveled north beyond the Wall, guided by the green dreams that the Reed children -- Meera and Jojen -- helped him understand. His powers as a skinchanger grew stronger with each passing day. He could slip into the mind of his direwolf Summer as easily as pulling on a cloak, and there were hints of deeper powers still, visions through the eyes of weirwood trees, glimpses of the past and future tangled together like roots. At the Nightfort, the oldest and most haunted castle on the Wall, Bran and his companions encountered Samwell Tarly and Gilly, fleeing south from Craster's Keep. And they met Coldhands, a mysterious figure who seemed to be a dead man walking, yet not a wight -- his hands were black with old blood and cold as ice, but his eyes were not the blue of the Others. Coldhands guided them through the Black Gate, a hidden passage beneath the Wall, and into the frozen lands beyond. Bran was going north, always north, following the call of the three-eyed crow that haunted his dreams.

And so the wheel turned, and the great lords and ladies of Westeros played their game of thrones, and the smallfolk suffered and died, as they always had and always would. Tywin Lannister, the great architect of so much suffering, lay dead on the privy, and the realm he had built was already beginning to crack. The Stark children were scattered to the winds -- Jon at the Wall, Sansa in the Eyrie, Arya on a ship to Braavos, Bran beyond the Wall, Rickon vanished, and Robb cold in his grave. Daenerys sat a stolen throne in Meereen, learning that conquest was the easy part and ruling was the thing that broke you. Stannis held the Wall and dreamed of the Iron Throne. And somewhere in the riverlands, in the dark places where outlaws gathered and the dead walked, there was a woman who had been pulled from the river three days after the Red Wedding, her flesh grey and rotting, her throat cut so deep she could not speak. Beric Dondarrion had given her the kiss of life, the last kiss, trading his own flame for hers, and he had died the final death so that she might rise. They called her Lady Stoneheart, and she had no mercy in her, not anymore. Mercy had been cut out of her with Robb's murder and her own. She remembered, though. She remembered the Freys. She remembered the Boltons. She remembered every face and every name.

In the epilogue, Merrett Frey rode to a crossroads with gold to ransom a captured Frey boy, and the Brotherhood Without Banners was waiting for him. They had a new leader now, one who did not speak, for her throat had been cut too deep for words. But she remembered. She pointed her finger, and they hanged Merrett Frey from a tree, and the wind blew cold through the riverlands, and winter was coming.

It was always coming.

The war of the five kings was all but over. Three of the five were dead -- Robb Stark, Joffrey Baratheon, Balon Greyjoy -- and a fourth, Renly, had died before the tale began in earnest. Only Stannis remained, and his war had shifted north, to the Wall and the lands beyond, where the true enemy stirred. But the game of thrones was not done. It would never be done, not so long as men craved power and feared death and loved the wrong people for the wrong reasons. The realm was broken, and the pieces were still falling, and the storm of swords that had swept across Westeros had left wounds that would take a generation to heal, if they ever healed at all.

For this was the truth that George R.R. Martin's tale told, again and again, in blood and fire and ice: that the wars men fight for crowns and glory are nothing beside the wars they fight within themselves, that honor is a sword without a hilt and love a poison as deadly as any strangler, and that in the game of thrones you win or you die, but even the winners pay a price so steep that victory and defeat become, in the end, the same cold thing.
