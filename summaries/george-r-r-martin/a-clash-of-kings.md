The comet came in the night, red as blood and bright as flame, its long tail slashing across the black sky above Dragonstone like a wound that would not close. Old Maester Cressen watched it from his balcony and told himself it was no omen, though in all his years of service he had never seen a comet half so fierce nor so fell in color. A white raven had come from the Citadel that very day, heralding the end of the longest summer in living memory, and now this red messenger burned overhead as if the heavens themselves were bleeding. On Dragonstone, the red priestess Melisandre of Asshai proclaimed the comet a sign from her god, R'hllor, the Lord of Light, and at her urging Stannis Baratheon had set the old wooden gods of the castle ablaze in sacrifice, the carved faces of the Seven writhing in green and orange flame on the beach while his queen, the gaunt and fervent Selyse, watched with hungry approval. Cressen had served Stannis since the boy was young, had loved him as the son the gods never gave him, and he could see with terrible clarity how this red woman had sunk her claws into the lord he served. In desperation he resolved to do what must be done. He placed a crystal of the strangler into a cup of wine and offered it to Melisandre, thinking to share the cup and die with her if he must. She drank. He drank. The old maester crumpled to the floor, the poison burning through him like wildfire, and when his sight dimmed and his heart stuttered and stopped, Melisandre stood above him, untouched, the ruby at her throat pulsing with a light red as the comet overhead.

Across the narrow sea, the comet blazed above the red waste where Daenerys Targaryen led her ragged khalasar through a land of ash and bone. The Dothraki called it shierak qiya, the bleeding star, and they feared it, but Daenerys took it for a sign that pointed the way she must ride. She was the last scion of House Targaryen, mother of three dragons no larger than cats, and she had nothing else in all the world save a dwindling band of followers half mad with thirst and hunger. She sent riders in three directions and waited, and when her bloodrider Jhogo returned with men from the great trading city of Qarth, she dared to hope. The Qartheen were a pale and graceful people who draped themselves in silks and spoke in honeyed tones, and three of their number came to treat with her: Xaro Xhoan Daxos, a merchant prince whose wealth was legend; Pyat Pree, a warlock with blue-stained lips; and Quaithe of the Shadow, a masked woman who spoke in riddles. They had come for the dragons, every one of them, though each couched their desire in different words. Daenerys accepted Xaro's hospitality and entered Qarth through its great gates, her dragons perched upon her shoulders, and the city received her with feasts and marvels, though beneath the silk and spice she sensed something rotten, something false.

In King's Landing, the boy king Joffrey Baratheon sat the Iron Throne, though it was his mother Cersei who truly ruled, or believed she did. The realm bled. Robb Stark, the Young Wolf, had been crowned King in the North and was winning battle after battle in the riverlands, smashing Lannister armies and capturing Jaime Lannister himself. The smallfolk whispered that Joffrey was no true king, that he was born of incest between Cersei and her twin brother Jaime, and Stannis Baratheon was writing letters to every lord in the Seven Kingdoms proclaiming as much. The realm had not seen such chaos since the fall of the Targaryens, and into this viper's nest came Tyrion Lannister.

Lord Tywin had dispatched his youngest son to serve as Hand of the King in his stead, and Tyrion arrived at court with the sellsword Bronn at his side and a mountain clansman's axe at his hip. He was a dwarf, mocked and despised even by his own kin, yet he had a mind as sharp as Valyrian steel and a tongue to match. Cersei received him with barely concealed fury, for she had expected their father and gotten instead the little brother she loathed. No matter. Tyrion set about the business of ruling with a will. He found King's Landing rife with Cersei's incompetence and Joffrey's cruelty, the city's defenses a shambles, the treasury empty, and enemies closing from every side. He tested the loyalty of the small council by feeding each member a different tale and waiting to see which reached the queen's ears. It was Grand Maester Pycelle who proved the informer, and Tyrion had him seized and thrown into a black cell. He forged an alliance with House Martell of Dorne by offering them a royal marriage, dispatched Littlefinger to treat with the Tyrells, and commissioned the pyromancers of the Alchemists' Guild to produce vast quantities of wildfire. Most cunning of all, he set smiths to forging a great iron chain, link by massive link, to stretch across the mouth of the Blackwater Rush. The chain would be his masterstroke, though none yet knew it.

All the while Sansa Stark endured. She was a hostage in all but name, a pretty bird in a gilded cage, and Joffrey delighted in her torment. When the boy king held his nameday tourney, the knight Ser Dontos Hollard arrived drunk and half undressed, and Joffrey would have had him killed had Sansa not intervened with careful, courteous words, suggesting the fool would serve better as a jester. It saved the man's life, and in gratitude Dontos sought her out in the godswood and swore to be her Florian, promising to spirit her from the city when the time was right. Sansa kissed him on the cheek and called him her true knight, and in the darkness of the godswood she dared to dream of home. But the Hound found her on her way back to her chambers, drunk and bitter, and he told her truths she did not want to hear. Sandor Clegane was no knight, he said, and the world was built by killers, and the sooner she learned that, the longer she would live. He was brutal in his honesty, yet there was something in him that would not let Joffrey's men beat her without flinching, some remnant of the boy he had been before his brother pressed his face into the brazier and melted half the world away.

South of King's Landing, a different sort of war was brewing. Renly Baratheon had crowned himself at Highgarden with the wealth of the Reach behind him, wed Margaery Tyrell to seal the alliance, and gathered the largest army in Westeros, a hundred thousand strong, a glittering host of knights in enameled armor who jousted and feasted and played at war as if it were a tourney. Renly was young and handsome and generous, everything his dour brother Stannis was not, and men flocked to his rainbow banners. Catelyn Stark came to him at Bitterbridge as Robb's envoy, seeking alliance against the Lannisters, and she found a king who would rather hold tourneys than fight battles, a court of summer knights who had never known winter. She liked Renly well enough, but she could see the folly in his claim. He was the younger brother. By all the laws of gods and men, the crown belonged to Stannis.

And Stannis meant to have it. He sailed from Dragonstone to Storm's End, where Renly's garrison held the castle, and there the two brothers met beneath the walls and traded words as sharp as swords. Stannis offered Renly a place on his council. Renly laughed and offered Stannis a peach. Neither would bend, and so they parted, and that night, in the darkness of Renly's tent, something unspeakable happened. Catelyn was there with Brienne of Tarth, a woman warrior sworn to Renly's service, when the candles guttered and the shadows moved and a thing of darkness slid through the silk walls of the pavilion like smoke through a keyhole. It had Stannis's face, or something like it, and it reached for Renly with hands of shadow and opened his throat in a gout of blood. Renly died in Brienne's arms, the life going out of his eyes like a candle snuffed, and the king's guards burst in to find Brienne holding the bloody king and Catelyn standing witness. They fled together, Brienne swearing her sword to Catelyn and Catelyn accepting, because there was no time for anything else and the world had gone mad.

What Catelyn did not know, and what Ser Davos Seaworth the Onion Knight had seen with his own eyes, was how the shadow was born. Stannis had ordered Davos to row Melisandre beneath the walls of Storm's End in the dead of night, through a sea cave where the water ran black and cold. There in the darkness the red woman had pulled up her robes and given birth to a thing of shadow, a darkness in the shape of a man, and Davos had watched it slide away into the night with a horror that would never leave him. He was a smuggler's son raised to lordship, a practical man who believed in what he could see and touch, and what he had seen in that cave shook the foundations of his world. When Storm's End fell and Renly's bannermen bent the knee to Stannis, Davos spoke against Melisandre with every ounce of courage he possessed. He and Ser Cortnay Penrose convinced Stannis to leave the red woman behind when his fleet sailed for King's Landing, though Davos feared it might not be enough to save whatever remained of the man Stannis had been.

Far to the north, beyond the Wall, the Night's Watch was stirring. Lord Commander Jeor Mormont, the Old Bear, had led three hundred brothers into the haunted forest to discover why the wildlings had vanished from their villages and what the ranger Benjen Stark had found before he disappeared. They came to Craster's Keep, a squalid holdfast where a wildling named Craster wed his own daughters and gave his sons to the cold, and there Jon Snow learned things that chilled him worse than any wind off the Frostfangs. The wildlings had not vanished. They were gathering, all of them, every clan and tribe and village, rallying to a man named Mance Rayder who had once been a brother of the Watch. The King-Beyond-the-Wall, they called him, and he was massing an army the likes of which the north had not seen in living memory.

From Craster's Keep, the Watch marched to the Fist of the First Men, an ancient ringfort atop a hill, and there Mormont waited for his scouts to return. Jon was sent with the legendary ranger Qhorin Halfhand into the Skirling Pass to learn Mance's numbers and intentions. They were a small party, five men against the wilderness, and the mountains swallowed them whole. Jon captured a wildling girl named Ygritte, fierce and quick-tongued, kissed by fire with her red hair, and when Qhorin ordered him to kill her, Jon found he could not do it. He let her go and paid for his mercy when the wildlings found their trail. One by one Jon's companions fell or were sent away, until only he and Qhorin remained, cornered and captured by a band of wildlings led by a man called Rattleshirt, the Lord of Bones. In those last desperate moments, Qhorin Halfhand gave Jon his final order: yield to them, join them, do whatever must be done to learn Mance Rayder's plans and carry them back to the Wall. And when Rattleshirt demanded proof of Jon's sincerity, Qhorin attacked him, and Jon fought back, and Ghost tore at the Halfhand's leg, and Longclaw opened the great ranger's throat in a spray of red. Jon stood over the body of the bravest man he had ever known and felt nothing but the cold, and when the wildlings took him to their camp, he looked upon the vast host of Mance Rayder, thirty thousand strong with giants and mammoths among them, and understood what was coming for the Wall.

Meanwhile Robb Stark's war ground on in the riverlands, and in his absence the north lay exposed. Robb had sent Theon Greyjoy home to the Iron Islands to treat with his father, Lord Balon, hoping to win the ironborn fleet to his cause. It was a grievous miscalculation. Balon Greyjoy was a hard man, iron-crowned and iron-willed, and he had no love for the Starks who had taken his last surviving son as a hostage. He meant to carve his own kingdom from the chaos of the south, and when Theon arrived at Pyke expecting a hero's welcome, he found instead a father who despised him for becoming too much the green-lander, a sister who mocked him, and a plan that gave him nothing but scraps while his sister Asha received the glory. Theon burned with resentment. He was Balon's son and heir, a prince of the Iron Islands, yet they treated him like a stranger. And so, desperate to prove his worth, Theon hatched a scheme as bold as it was mad. While Asha struck the Stony Shore and drew the north's remaining defenders west, Theon sailed a skeleton crew to the lightly held castle of Winterfell and took it by surprise.

Young Bran Stark, who ruled in Robb's name from his father's seat, woke to find ironborn in his home and Theon Greyjoy grinning down at him. Bran was only a boy, crippled and frightened, but he had the blood of the Starks and he would not beg. He yielded Winterfell to spare the lives of his people, and Theon took the great seat of the north as his own, though it fit him no better than a stolen cloak. The castle seethed with hostility. Every servant, every guard, every cook and stable boy looked at Theon with eyes full of hate, and the prince of the Iron Islands began to learn what it meant to hold a place by fear alone.

Bran had not been idle in the weeks before the taking. Jojen Reed, the strange green-eyed boy from the crannogs, had been teaching him to understand his dreams, and Bran had begun to accept what he had long denied: that he was a warg, a skinchanger, that the wolf dreams he shared with Summer were real. When the time came, Bran, Rickon, Hodor, Osha the wildling woman, and the Reed siblings, Jojen and Meera, escaped Winterfell in the night with the direwolves at their side. They hid in the crypts beneath the castle, in the darkness among the tombs of dead kings, and when Theon's hunters lost the trail, the turncloak prince chose a darker path. On the counsel of a foul-smelling servant called Reek, Theon killed two miller's boys of an age with Bran and Rickon, dipped their heads in tar, and displayed the charred remains on the walls of Winterfell, proclaiming the Stark boys dead. The north wept, and Theon Greyjoy learned that some things, once done, can never be undone.

Arya Stark's path was darker still. She had escaped King's Landing disguised as a boy bound for the Wall, traveling north with Yoren of the Night's Watch and a ragged company of recruits, orphans, and criminals. Among the latter were three men locked in a wagon, dangerous beyond measure: Rorge, Biter, and a man from Braavos called Jaqen H'ghar, who spoke of himself in the third person and smiled a smile that could mean anything. When gold cloaks from King's Landing came hunting for Gendry, a blacksmith's apprentice who was in truth one of Robert Baratheon's bastard sons, Yoren refused to yield him up and paid for it with his life. The attack scattered the recruits and left Arya and a handful of survivors, Gendry, Hot Pie, and Lommy Greenhands among them, stumbling through a war-ravaged countryside where every village was burned and every road thick with corpses.

They were captured by Ser Amory Lorch and taken to Harrenhal, the cursed castle by the God's Eye, where Lord Tywin Lannister had made his seat of war. Arya became a servant, a mouse scurrying through the largest castle in Westeros, her true name hidden behind a dozen false ones. She was no one, a ghost among ghosts, and every night she whispered a litany of names, the names of those she meant to kill: Joffrey, Cersei, Ilyn Payne, the Hound, Ser Amory, the Tickler, and more, a prayer of vengeance that grew longer with each passing day. It was in Harrenhal that Jaqen H'ghar found her. She had saved his life during the attack, pulling him and his companions from the burning wagon, and by the customs of his people he owed her three deaths in return. Three lives she had saved. Three lives she could take. Arya named the Tickler first, a torturer who extracted confessions with a rat and a heated bucket, and the man was found dead with a broken neck. She named Ser Amory Lorch second, and he died with a poisoned dart in his neck. For her third name, Arya named Jaqen himself, and the Braavosi only smiled and asked her to unname him. She would, she said, if he helped her free the northern prisoners Lord Bolton had brought to Harrenhal. Jaqen agreed, and in a single night of blood they opened the gates and put the Lannister garrison to the sword, and Roose Bolton, Lord of the Dreadfort, took Harrenhal for the north. When it was done, Jaqen H'ghar pressed an iron coin into Arya's palm and told her that if she ever wished to find him again, she need only give the coin to any man from Braavos and speak the words: valar morghulis. All men must die. Then he passed his hand across his face, and when he lowered it, he wore a different face entirely, and Arya watched him walk away into the night, a stranger.

Arya did not linger. She escaped Harrenhal with Gendry and Hot Pie, slipping out through a postern gate with a map she had stolen from Roose Bolton's chambers, and struck out through the war-torn riverlands toward Riverrun, where she hoped to find her mother and her brother. The road was long and dark and full of terrors, but Arya Stark had learned to be hard, and the girl who had once dreamed of being a lady now dreamed only of the names.

In Qarth, Daenerys found that the wealth of the east was as treacherous as the poverty of the red waste. Xaro Xhoan Daxos offered her ships and gold if she would marry him, and when she refused, his hospitality cooled. Pyat Pree urged her to visit the House of the Undying Ones, where the warlocks of Qarth kept their ancient seat of power. Daenerys had nowhere else to turn. She needed ships to cross the narrow sea, armies to reclaim her father's throne, and the warlocks promised her the truth of all things. She drank the shade of the evening from a crystal glass, sweet and bitter on her tongue, and entered the House of the Undying alone, for so the rules demanded. The dwelling was ancient and vast, a labyrinth of corridors and doors, and the visions came upon her like fever dreams. She saw a great hall of the dead, a feast of corpses where a man with a wolf's head wore an iron crown. She saw her brother Rhaegar and his wife and child, and heard him say the babe's name was Aegon, the prince that was promised, and that his was the song of ice and fire. She saw a house with a red door and a lemon tree, the home of her childhood, and she wept. She passed through doors that opened onto wonders and horrors alike, always remembering to take the door to her right and the stairs that went up, as Pyat Pree had instructed, until at last she found the Undying themselves, ancient things more shadow than flesh, a blue-lipped heart beating in the darkness above them. They whispered prophecies to her. Three fires must you light, they said, one for life and one for death and one to love. Three mounts must you ride, one to bed and one to dread and one to love. Three treasons will you know, once for blood and once for gold and once for love. And then they tried to devour her. They reached for her with cold tendrils of darkness, draining her warmth, her strength, her very life, and Daenerys screamed, and Drogon answered. The black dragon tore through the ancient heart with teeth and claws, and fire bloomed in the House of the Undying, and Daenerys stumbled free into the sunlight with the building burning behind her. Pyat Pree came at her with a knife, but her bloodriders cut him down, and the warlocks of Qarth were broken.

She left Qarth soon after, for the city had turned against her. An assassin came for her in the harbor, a Sorrowful Man with a manticore in a jeweled box, and she was saved only by the intervention of a fat old knight named Arstan Whitebeard and a eunuch warrior called Strong Belwas, who said they had been sent by a friend in Pentos, Illyrio Mopatis, with three ships to carry her home. Daenerys had learned suspicion in the red waste, had tasted betrayal in Qarth, but she had three dragons and the blood of Old Valyria, and she would not be afraid. She boarded the ship and set sail west, toward the Bay of Slavers and whatever destiny awaited her.

All these threads of war and wonder came to their blood-red culmination in the Battle of the Blackwater. Stannis Baratheon sailed from Storm's End with the greatest fleet the narrow sea had ever known, two hundred ships bearing an army that outnumbered the city's defenders five to one. In King's Landing, Tyrion prepared for the onslaught with the grim efficiency of a man who knew that defeat meant death. He had the wildfire, thousands of jars of the foul green substance that burned so hot it could melt stone and set the very river ablaze. He had the chain, a great boom of iron that his smiths had labored over for months. And he had his wits, which were worth more than both.

The night Stannis came, the Blackwater Rush burned green. Tyrion sent a single ship, a derelict packed to the gunwales with wildfire, drifting downriver toward the enemy fleet, and when it exploded, it took a dozen ships with it, and then the current carried the burning hulks into the rest of the fleet, and the river became a highway of green flame. The chain rose dripping from the water, sealing the mouth of the rush, and Stannis's ships could neither advance nor retreat. Men burned alive in their armor. The screams carried across the water to the city walls where Sansa Stark knelt in Maegor's Holdfast with the queen and the other highborn women, praying to gods who did not answer. Cersei drank and mocked and spoke of what would happen if the city fell, the rape and the murder, and Sansa understood with a child's clarity that the queen meant to have Ser Ilyn Payne take their heads before she let them be taken alive.

On the walls and on the water, Tyrion fought as he had never fought. When the Hound, Sandor Clegane, broke and fled from the wildfire, the flames licking at something deep inside him that was more terrible than any enemy, Tyrion rallied the men himself, leading a sortie through the tunnels beneath the city to strike at Stannis's forces on the northern bank. He was a dwarf on a warhorse, an absurd figure, and yet the men followed him because he was there, because he was fighting when greater men had run. They drove the attackers back, for a time, but Stannis's numbers were too great, and the line was faltering when Tyrion felt the blade. Ser Mandon Moore of the Kingsguard, one of his own protectors, turned on him in the chaos and opened his face from brow to jaw, a slash that would have killed him had his squire Podrick Payne not driven a spear through Ser Mandon's back. Tyrion fell, his blood mingling with the mud and the ashes, and the darkness took him.

He did not see the end of the battle. He did not see the host that came thundering down the roseroad with the sun at their backs, the combined strength of House Tyrell and House Lannister, led by Lord Tywin himself and the Knight of Flowers, Ser Loras Tyrell, who wore the armor of the dead Renly and made the enemy believe a ghost had come to take vengeance. The vanguard smashed into Stannis's rear like a hammer on an anvil, and the battle became a rout. Stannis was driven from the field with a fraction of his force, his fleet in ashes, his dream of the Iron Throne shattered on the walls of King's Landing. It was Tywin Lannister who strode into the throne room in the aftermath and claimed the victory, Tywin Lannister who was named Hand of the King and Savior of the City, and Tyrion, who had done more than any man to hold the walls, lay in a dark chamber with half his face cut away, forgotten and alone.

In the ruin of battle, alliances shifted like sand. The Tyrells, newly bound to the Lannisters, proposed that Joffrey set aside Sansa Stark and take Margaery Tyrell as his queen instead. The High Septon was persuaded to dissolve the betrothal, and Joffrey, who had never wanted Sansa except as a thing to hurt, agreed with a show of reluctance that fooled no one. Sansa wept, but they were tears of relief, for she believed herself free at last. Littlefinger, ever the schemer, whispered that she was not free at all, that the Lannisters would never let the key to Winterfell walk away, but she did not yet understand. It was Dontos who gave her hope, pressing a silver hairnet into her hands and telling her that her escape would come on Joffrey's wedding night, and that she must be sure to wear it. The fool's eyes were earnest, and Sansa wanted to believe, because believing was all she had.

The Hound came to her one last time, during the battle, when the wildfire had driven him half mad with old terrors. He offered to take her from the city, to carry her north to Winterfell, and for a moment Sansa almost went. But she looked at his burned face and his wine-dark eyes and the blood on his white cloak, and she was afraid, and she told him she would not go. Sandor Clegane left King's Landing alone, riding into a night lit green by wildfire, and Sansa never saw him again, though she would think of him in the days to come and wonder if she had made the right choice.

In the north, Winterfell burned. Ramsay Snow, the Bastard of Bolton, revealed himself from behind the guise of the servant Reek, betrayed Theon Greyjoy, and put the castle to the torch. The great fortress of the Starks, eight thousand years old, was reduced to smoke and ruin, its people scattered or slaughtered, its towers crumbling into ash. When the fires died, Bran and his companions emerged from the crypts where they had hidden among the bones of kings. The world they found was changed beyond recognition. The walls still stood, for Winterfell was built of granite and would not fall easily, but the wood was gone, the roofs collapsed, the great hall open to the sky. Maester Luwin lay dying beneath the heart tree, an arrow in his belly, and he told them they must go, must split apart, Bran and Rickon each taking a different road so that the Starks would not be ended in a single stroke. Osha would take Rickon and the wild direwolf Shaggydog one way. Bran, Hodor, Meera, Jojen, and the direwolf Summer would go another, north, beyond the Wall, where Jojen's green dreams told them Bran's destiny lay. They said their farewells in the godswood, beneath the red leaves of the weirwood, and Bran wept because he was only a boy and his home was ashes and his father was dead and his brothers scattered and the world was cold and dark and full of terrors.

Yet the comet still burned overhead, and men saw in it what they wished to see. To Melisandre it was the herald of a new age, the coming of Azor Ahai reborn. To the Dothraki it was a dark portent, a bleeding star. To the smallfolk of Westeros it was the sign of Lannister crimson, or Tully red, or the red of dragonfire, depending on whom you asked. Five kings had gone to war, and two were dead, and the realm bled from a hundred wounds, and winter was coming, as the Starks had always promised. In the south the lions roared triumphant. In the north the wolves were scattered. On Dragonstone the fires of R'hllor burned low, and Stannis brooded on his failure, and Melisandre whispered of prophecies yet unfulfilled. Beyond the Wall, the dead stirred in the cold, and the wildings marched, and Jon Snow walked among his enemies with a dead man's blood on his hands and a mission he did not know how to fulfill. And in the east, three dragons screamed above the summer sea, and the girl who carried them sailed toward a world that had forgotten fire.

The game of thrones went on, as it always had, as it always would, for when you play the game of thrones, you win or you die, and the dead have no say in the matter. The kings clashed and the banners flew and the swords sang their red song, and in the end, as in the beginning, it was the common folk who paid the price, the farmers in their fields and the fishermen on their boats and the children in their cradles, the ones who never asked for war and never wanted crowns and never understood why the great lords could not simply leave them be. They burned and they bled and they died in their thousands, and the lords called it glory, and the singers called it valor, and the crows and the worms called it supper, and winter was coming for them all.
