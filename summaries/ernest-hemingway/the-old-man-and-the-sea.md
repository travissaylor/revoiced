By evening the empty boat had become something the boy could hardly bear to see. Santiago had taken it out for eighty-four days without a catch. He brought it back each day and put away the gear and there was always the next morning to prepare for. The younger fishermen laughed at him. Some of the older men watched him come in and were quiet. They knew how long a man could work without being paid by the sea. His clothes and his sail were patched, and his hands carried the marks of old work. The marks were healed. There had been nothing lately to open them. Only his eyes seemed untouched by the waiting. They were clear and blue and he looked out of them without asking anyone to excuse him.

Manolin had worked with him through the first forty days. Then the boy's parents had put him aboard another boat because they believed Santiago's luck was finished. That boat caught fish. A boy had to do what his father told him, and fish sold at the market were an argument his father understood. But every evening Manolin returned to help the old man carry his things. He had been five when Santiago first took him fishing. He remembered the violence of a fish brought into the boat, the noise of its tail, and the old man keeping him safe while bringing the fish under control. Much of what he knew had come through those hands. He could leave the boat without leaving the man who had taught him.

They sat at the Terrace and talked with the easy care of people who know what must not be pressed. The boy wanted to fish with him again. Santiago told him to stay where things were going well. He had no wish to take the boy from his father or make his own misfortune into an obligation. The boy could help with bait, however. They settled that between them with the seriousness of men arranging a business that still deserved good materials. Santiago meant to go well offshore in the morning. September was a month for the large fish, and a man should be ready when one came. Eighty-five might be a lucky number. They spoke of lottery tickets and the way luck could change. Under their talk lay the boat, ready to go out once more.

At the shack there was a bed, a chair, a table, and the little place where a charcoal fire could be made. The walls were built of palm material. Religious pictures remained there, but Santiago had put away the photograph of his wife because looking at it made the room too empty. He spoke of food he did not have. The boy answered as if the food were there. They also spoke of a net that had been sold long before. These small arrangements saved the old man from explaining his poverty and saved the boy from making him explain it. Manolin would get food. That was the thing to do. A man could discuss the next day's fishing more reasonably after eating, even if he had forgotten how hungry he was.

While Santiago slept in the chair, the boy brought supper from the Terrace. Martin, the owner, had supplied it, and the old man wanted to thank him with something from a good fish. There had been other kindnesses. He remembered them as debts he hoped to settle, though the boy was thinking about more immediate things. There should be soap and water for him, a clean shirt, shoes, and something warm before the weather changed. For now there were beans and rice and other food in the containers, and they ate while talking about baseball. Santiago trusted the Yankees and especially Joe DiMaggio. A great player could suffer and continue to play well. The old man liked remembering that DiMaggio's father had been a fisherman. That seemed a useful thing for a son to know.

They talked of other players and managers, of victories and the danger of being too certain about a team. The boy trusted Santiago as the old man trusted the great player. Santiago accepted the boy's faith without making much of it. His strength was less than it had been, but there were things a man learned after strength alone no longer settled his work. He would wake the boy before daylight. After Manolin left, he folded his trousers to use beneath his head and lay down under the blanket. Sleep took him away from the empty days. He dreamed of Africa, where he had sailed when he was young, and of the lions on the shore. In the evening of the dream they moved and played on the sand. He loved watching them. He did not need to be young again to see them clearly.

Before dawn he went to wake Manolin. The boy rose from sleep and came with him, and they carried the gear down in the dark. Other fishermen were moving toward their boats. They drank coffee, and the boy brought the bait and sardines. Santiago would have little else during the day except the water he carried. Coffee was enough for the moment. There was work to do while the air was cool and the land still held its shape against the sky. They wished each other luck. Then the old man rowed out, hearing other oars for a time before the boats spread apart. The boy's help had ended at the shore. Ahead, the current and his own arms would take him where he had decided to go.

He passed beyond the places where he had been fishing and rowed toward deeper water. In the dark he heard flying fish break the surface. He felt tenderness for the small birds that worked so hard to find food over a sea that could destroy them easily. The sea was familiar to him in all its changes. Some of the younger fishermen treated it as an opponent or a place to earn money with their motorboats. He thought of it as female, capable of giving and withholding, moved by things beyond a fisherman's wishes. He could love it without expecting it to be kind. The current carried life beneath him that he could not see, and he read what rose from it: a bird turning, a disturbance in the water, the color where depth began.

He set his baits at different depths and kept the lines straight. Other men might let theirs drift and trust that the bait was where they wanted it. He wanted to know. Luck could come whenever it chose, but he could place the hooks properly before it arrived. He had sardines to make the baits attractive, and he watched the sticks that would signal a pull. When the sun came up he avoided its early glare as well as he could. His eyes had worked against that glare for many years. The water brightened and the land diminished behind him. He followed a bird that was working above small fish. A tuna struck, and he brought it aboard. It was food for later. He kept moving, attentive to the larger chance underneath the morning's small success.

There were creatures he disliked and others he admired without wishing to be one of them. The drifting Portuguese men-of-war looked beautiful and could leave a man hurt and helpless. Turtles ate them, and this pleased him. He remembered the work of taking turtles and the endurance of their hearts. He knew what turtle eggs and shark-liver oil could do for strength, though neither memory supplied a meal now. These were ordinary thoughts belonging to an ordinary working morning. He had begun talking aloud when he fished alone. There was no one to be disturbed by it. The boy would have understood the things he said. Out here a man could address a bird or his own hands and then listen again to the silence without feeling that he had done anything strange.

Toward noon one of the deep lines moved. Santiago took it gently between his fingers. The touch below was cautious, and he understood that a fish was handling the bait far down in the cold water. He waited. He wanted the fish to eat well and take the hook with it. There was weight, then a pause, then the movement came again. He gave line with care, speaking encouragement that could never reach its intended listener. At last the weight became certain. He pulled to set the hook and tried to bring the fish upward. Nothing yielded. The line stayed hard, and the boat began moving. Somewhere below him the fish was swimming steadily, taking the skiff along as though it were another small resistance in the water.

He could not fasten the line to the boat. A sudden movement would break it. He had to make his body the yielding part between the hook and the wood. He braced himself and held the strain across his shoulders, keeping the reserve coils ready. The fish took him farther from shore. He had not seen it, but the pull told him enough to be careful. It did not rush about or waste itself. It went on as if it knew exactly what it meant to do. He wished Manolin were there. With the boy, there would have been another pair of hands and someone to speak to without answering himself. Alone, he arranged what could be arranged and let the fish tow him. The contest had begun before either could look at the other.

Through the afternoon the shore fell away until he could no longer depend on seeing it. He knew he could find his way home by the light over Havana and the direction of the wind and current. For now there was only the line to attend to. The pressure hurt, but it was a pressure he could study. He shifted the sack across his back to protect the place where the line lay. He drank a little water. He watched its angle, hoping the fish might rise and fill its air sacs so it could not go deep and die there. If it sank beyond his power to lift it, all his patience would end in a weight on the bottom. It kept swimming instead. He admired this and would have liked to make it stop.

As the light changed, the old man thought of the fish's loneliness. He had once hooked a female marlin and watched the male remain near her while she fought. The male stayed even when the female was brought into the boat. That memory had a sadness he had never used up. Killing a fish could be necessary and still be sad. This one had taken his bait because it was feeding, and now they were joined by something neither could abandon without losing. Santiago had chosen to come so far. The fish had chosen the deep water before he arrived. Their choices had met in a hook. He respected the creature holding him, and his respect increased his need to prove that he could do the work he had come to do.

Night required him to simplify everything. Another fish took one of the other baits, and he cut that line rather than let it endanger the one he held. He sacrificed the remaining gear he could not manage and joined the spare coils to his reserve. The knots had to be made without giving the great fish an opening. Each little task was awkward under the load. There was no spare person in the boat to hold, reach, or cut while he kept the pressure even. Once a sudden pull brought him forward and injured his face. He settled himself again. The fish was strong in the darkness. Santiago watched the stars and kept his body against the line. He had given up the chance of lesser catches. All that remained of the day's work was moving ahead of him where he could not see it.

With morning a small bird came to the boat and rested. It perched on the line as if it had found a safe branch, and Santiago spoke to it. The bird was tired. Beyond its rest there would be hawks and more water to cross. He would have liked it to stay, but a movement from the fish disturbed them both. The line cut his right hand and the bird was gone. He put the hand in the sea and watched the blood wash away. The wound was in a place he would need to use. There was no reason to be angry about that. He had to remember to eat the tuna, and he had to keep the line from making a worse cut. These were matters he could still do something about.

He cut strips from the tuna and chewed them carefully. The food was raw and without salt or lime, but it would go into his blood and help him. His left hand cramped until it was hardly a hand at all. He disliked its refusal to work and spoke to it with contempt, then tried to be patient. A cramped hand could not be argued loose. Sunlight and food might release it. In the meantime the right hand and the rest of his body had to take more of the load. The fish continued with a strength that seemed to have no need of rest. Santiago could imagine the boy rubbing the cramped fingers and opening them. The thought was useful only for a moment. Then he returned to what the hand could do, which was very little.

When the marlin finally rose, the old man saw how much of the struggle had been hidden. Its body came out long and shining, its great bill ahead of it, the stripes clear along its sides. It was longer than the skiff. The sight gave a shape to the immense weight he had been holding since the previous day. Then it went under again, and the line ran through his hands. He gave what he had to give and held what he could. He must not show it the limit of his strength. If the fish understood that it could break him by one determined rush, knowledge would finish what strength had begun. But it had its own knowledge, and that did not include the exact frailty of an old fisherman in a little boat above it.

He had seen great fish before, but he had never been joined to one like this while alone. Its size made the work harder and made him unwilling to imagine another fish in its place. He loved the way it moved. He meant to kill it. Both things stayed true as the sun climbed. He promised prayers and a pilgrimage if he could finish the fight, although prayer did not come to him easily. He said the words he remembered and went on attending to the line. God could hear a man while the man was working. He thought about DiMaggio playing with pain from a bone spur. He did not know exactly what such a spur was like, but he knew what it meant to continue with something wrong in the part of the body the work required.

His mind went back to Casablanca and an arm-wrestling match with a powerful man from Cienfuegos. They had sat across from each other while people watched and bet, their clasped hands above the table. The match lasted through the night. There had been strain enough to draw blood beneath the nails, and changes of watchers, and the other man's efforts to drive him down. Santiago had held. Then, in the morning, he found what he needed and put the other hand to the table. Men had called him a champion afterward. That strength was long past, but he remembered it precisely. He knew from having done it that a contest could last beyond the hour when a person watching would believe it ought to end. Memory helped him hold the present weight.

The left hand loosened at last. He received it back without forgiveness or celebration and used it. Around him the sea kept its own occupations. A plane passed high overhead. Birds found what they could. The fish's pace changed enough for him to read its fatigue, but fatigue was not surrender. He could not afford to mistake one for the other. A little more pressure might help, or it might break the line and leave him with the useless end in his hands. He had to spend his strength as carefully as he spent his water. The sun made the hours long and the movements of the boat repetitive. He thought about baseball scores he could not know. Then the line reminded him of the one contest whose score he could feel.

Toward evening he caught a dolphinfish on a small line. He worked it aboard while keeping the marlin's line secure, then killed it for food. Later he opened it and found flying fish inside. There was something clean and fortunate about those smaller fish. The flesh of the dolphinfish was less pleasant to eat raw, but he needed it. Preparation was work done with a knife, with one balance kept while another was adjusted. He cleaned up as well as he could and studied the great fish's movement. The two of them had been tied together through a day and a night and another day. Neither could keep doing this forever. The old man had to arrange some sleep before lack of it took away the judgment on which his hands depended.

He found a position where the pressure would remain on his body and a movement of the line would wake him. It was a poor bed, but it was a bed he could make. The stars were out, and he was grateful that the work of a fisherman did not require killing them. There were enough things to kill already. He wondered whether the fish slept. Somewhere in the water it was enduring the hook while he prepared to close his eyes. He could pity it and eat the food needed to defeat it. He could feel himself growing weak and still plan the next act of the fight. At last he let sleep come. It carried him through dreams of porpoises and familiar shelter toward the African beach where the lions appeared again.

The line woke him violently. It was running out, burning across his hands, and the marlin was jumping in the dark. He was pulled down against the bow. His face struck the food he had left there, and for a time there was nothing to do except bear the movement without losing the fish. He could hear the weight strike the water. Each jump spent some of its strength and opened new danger for him. He worked his way into a position where his body could slow the line. When the rush eased he washed his face and tried to clear his head. The sleep had ended. His hands were damaged further, but they could still hold. He needed them to keep doing that until the fish's strength came down to the level of his own.

At daylight on the third day the marlin began to circle. Santiago could feel the change before he could see the shape of it. The line came toward him and he drew it in. Then the fish went away on the curve and he yielded under the pull. Each circuit offered a little gain if he used it properly. He had to bring the circles smaller without forcing too much. Black spots crossed his vision. Sweat ran into his eyes, and there was a faintness he distrusted more than pain. Pain could be worked with. If he lost consciousness, the boat would still float and the fish would still swim, but his part in the contest would be finished. He wet his head and kept himself at the work, measuring the circle by the line.

The great tail showed beneath the water. Then he could see the fish passing deep beside the boat, larger each time it came nearer. Small fish traveled with it. Its body seemed almost impossible to turn with the strength that remained in him. He pulled when the moment was right and felt its direction change, but it recovered and went around again. He tried once more. There was no one to encourage him except himself, and the things he said were orders to stay clearheaded and use the next chance. He had brought it so far that losing it now would require him to remember every hour afterward. Yet wanting did not move it. His shoulders, his legs, his back, and his hands had to turn that desire into a few inches of change.

On one of the close passes he summoned what was left and brought the marlin onto its side. He set the line beneath his foot, raised the harpoon, and drove it into the fish behind the great fin, toward the heart. He put his weight behind the thrust. The marlin came out of the water in its last enormous movement, and when it fell the sea struck over the old man and the skiff. Then it lay silver at the surface. Blood spread beneath it. The line that had made him a prisoner now led to something he had killed. He was so weak that the victory could scarcely become a feeling. There was the fish. There was the boat. He had to bring them together and prepare them for the long journey back.

The marlin could not be taken aboard. Santiago worked ropes around it and fastened it alongside, securing the head and tail so they could travel together. Its length made the skiff seem small. He examined it with a fisherman's knowledge and a tired man's difficulty believing what had happened. Here was enough weight to bring a considerable price. He thought of the men who would see it and of what DiMaggio might think of the fight. The old man had proved something to himself, but the proof still had to be brought home. He stepped the mast and raised the patched sail. The wind could do work his body could not. With the fish beside him and the boat pointed toward land, he began the part of the task that should have been easier.

He ate small shrimp gathered from weed and took more water. His hands were cut, but salt water would help them, and rest would come when he reached the shack. He watched the fish move beside the skiff. There were moments when he wondered which of them was bringing the other home. The current slipped along the shining body, and the ropes held them in their strange companionship. He had lived so closely with its strength that its stillness did not seem entirely real. He could touch it and know it was there. The wind was good. He knew the direction. For a little while he could sit and let the boat make distance. Behind them, unseen beneath the surface, blood from the wound was traveling through the sea.

The first shark found that blood and came up fast. It was a mako, built for speed, with a terrible mouth and no hesitation about using it. Santiago saw it coming and prepared the harpoon. He had killed the marlin in a contest that had taken nearly all his strength. This would be quick, and he would have to strike exactly. The shark tore into the fish while he drove the iron into its head. It died fighting, taking the harpoon and rope down with it. A large piece of the marlin was gone. The old man had stopped one shark and lost the weapon best suited to stopping another. Fresh blood went into the water. The damage could be measured in flesh and money, but he felt it more nearly than either measure could express.

He tried to steady himself by thinking about what a man could endure. His body might be broken without his consent. He could still refuse to give his consent. That distinction was something to hold while the sail carried him on. He wished he had never hooked the fish. He wished the fish were swimming below with no wound in it and no boat beside it. But the wish came after the killing and could not be used to mend anything. He tied his knife to an oar to make another weapon. There was work in that, and work was better than watching the damaged flank. He still had much of the marlin. He still had a blade and enough strength to use it. He would attend to the next shark when it came.

His thoughts moved into questions for which he had no settled answers. Perhaps killing the fish had been a sin. He had done it for food and because fishing was his trade, but there had been pride in it too. He had wanted this particular fish because of its greatness. Loving it did not make the killing disappear. Neither did killing it end the love. He was too tired to follow the matter far, and hunger and pain were poor company for deciding such things. The sea supplied no judgment. It supplied the wind, the wake, and the creatures following the blood. He tasted a little of the marlin's flesh and knew how good it was. It should have fed people. Now he had to defend what could still be brought to them.

Two shovel-nosed sharks came next. They were different from the first, ugly in their approach and determined to feed. One went beneath the boat, shaking the fish as it bit. Santiago worked the oar with the knife against them, striking where a blow might enter the brain or the eye. He killed them, but they took more flesh before they were finished. The boat and the marlin moved heavily during the struggle. Afterward the old man looked at what was missing and spoke to the dead fish with regret. He had gone beyond the distance from which he could protect it. The greatness of the catch had not shortened the way home. There were miles of water ahead, and every shark he stopped left the trail stronger for whatever followed.

Another shark cost him the knife. The blade broke in the attack, and the weapon became an oar again, with the useless part still bound to it. He thought of things he should have brought: another knife, some better way of fighting, tools that would have mattered now. They were all ashore or nowhere. He had an oar handle he could use as a club, the remaining oars, and the tiller. He counted them as a man counts what is actually in a boat. Regret would not sharpen the broken steel. The fish was being changed into something he did not want to look at. He kept his course. The wind continued to serve him, though the service was slow beside the speed with which a mouth could remove the earnings of three days.

Late in the day more sharks came, and he fought them with the club. He struck their heads when they rose close enough and felt how little reach and force he had left. Once he would have hit harder. That knowledge did not add anything to the blow. He used both hands and struck again. The sharks withdrew with more of the fish. Much of it was gone now, enough that he began to think of the remaining body as a ruin he was escorting. He apologized to it. Their struggle had been worthy of the strength they brought to it, and the return had become this waste. He had wanted to come back with a fish beyond anyone's doubt. He was coming back with something the sea was taking apart in front of him.

Darkness hid the damage without ending it. He watched for the glow of Havana and the lights that would tell him he was nearing home. He thought of the boy and the people who might be worrying about him. Even those who had laughed could be concerned when a boat stayed out too long. That thought brought the shore nearer in a way the lights could not. He was very cold and very tired. He no longer expected to save the whole fish, but he could keep sailing and keep a weapon within reach. Once he considered what had beaten him and found the answer in the distance he had chosen. He had gone farther than he could make good. Saying it plainly did not reduce the distance still left to travel.

The last attacks came in the night. He could hear the feeding and see enough movement to strike into it. He clubbed at heads and jaws until a shark took the club. Then he pulled the tiller free and used that. It broke, and he fought with the splintered end. The effort had lost any promise of success, but his arms continued the work as long as there was something to strike. At last there was no flesh worth another attack. The sharks left. He tasted something strange in his mouth and spat into the water. The boat moved more easily now. Beside it were the great head, the backbone, and the tail. He fitted what remained of the tiller so he could steer and turned all his attention toward getting into the harbor.

He came in late, when the village was asleep. There was no crowd to receive him and no buyer waiting for the catch. He secured the skiff and looked at the skeleton beside it in the light from shore. Its size was still there. Then he took down the mast and put it on his shoulder. The climb to the shack showed him how completely his strength was spent. He fell and had to rest before getting up. Farther on he sat down again. The mast was an ordinary piece of his equipment, and he had carried it many times, but now it demanded nearly everything. He stopped repeatedly before he reached the door. Inside, he drank water, lay on the bed, and drew the blanket over him. He slept with his wounded hands open.

In the morning Manolin found him there. The boy had been coming to the shack during the days of absence, and now he saw that the old man was breathing. Then he saw the hands and began to cry. He went out for coffee and could not stop crying as he walked. The wind was keeping the boats in, and fishermen had gathered around Santiago's skiff. One measured the skeleton. The fish had been eighteen feet long. The boy already knew enough from looking at it and looking at the sleeping man. He asked that Santiago be left to rest. At the Terrace, Martin supplied coffee and sent his concern. There were things to bring and people to answer. Manolin did them while the tears continued, without troubling to hide them.

When Santiago woke, the boy gave him coffee. The old man felt beaten and said so. Manolin would not allow the fish to receive the credit for that. The fish had not beaten him. What happened afterward belonged to the sharks. Santiago accepted that much, though his exhaustion remained. They spoke about the search for him and how small a skiff could be in so much water. The old man was relieved to have someone beside him again. He could speak and be answered. Manolin said they would fish together now. His parents' decision and the matter of luck had ceased to settle it. There was still much for the boy to learn, and he meant to learn it with Santiago. The old man began to think about the equipment they would need.

They would have a better weapon for sharks, made from good steel, and they would make it ready properly. The old man had broken his knife. His hands needed treatment, and he spoke of the strange feeling he had suffered in his chest during the night. The boy attended to these things with a firmness that left room for no argument about care. He would bring food and newspapers so Santiago could learn what had happened in baseball while he was gone. Pedrico could have the fish's head for bait, and Manolin could keep the bill. Even the wreck of the catch had uses among people who understood fish. The boy went to make the arrangements. Once outside, he cried again. His decision to return to the boat had not made the wounds smaller.

At the Terrace some tourists noticed the great backbone and tail moving in the water beside the skiff. A woman asked what it was. The waiter tried to explain the sharks, but his explanation left her believing she was looking at the remains of one. She admired the tail without knowing what had happened to the creature that carried it. Below the surface the tide moved what the old man had brought back. The fishermen knew its length. The boy knew the hands. There was no need for the sleeping man to tell the story yet. He lay in the shack, and Manolin sat beside him. The boat could wait and the plans could wait. In sleep he had reached the African shore again, and the lions were there.
