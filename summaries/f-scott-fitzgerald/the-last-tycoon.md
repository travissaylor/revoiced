It was the sort of flight that seems, in retrospect, to have contained already the whole shape of the story that followed, the way a first bar of music can carry in it the whole symphony, though you do not know it yet. Cecilia Brady was flying west, home to Hollywood for the summer, and she was not quite nineteen years old. She had left Bennington with the peculiar feeling of suspension that attends the end of a school year, when the structures that have held your days together dissolve and you are returned to yourself with a kind of gentle violence. On the plane she found herself in the company of two men who orbited her father's world: Wylie White, a screenwriter of middling reputation and considerable thirst, and a man called Schwartz, who had once been a producer of some consequence and was now a ruin. They were the sort of men who turn up at the periphery of power, clever enough to recognize it, too weak or too unlucky to seize it for themselves.

The plane was forced down in Nashville by a storm, and the three of them found themselves stranded in the Southern dark, that particular quality of American night that smells of earth and distance. They visited Andrew Jackson's home, the Hermitage, and there was something in the gesture that seemed already elegiac, as though they were pilgrims visiting a shrine to an older, more forthright kind of American ambition. Schwartz, who had been growing more agitated throughout the journey, left a cryptic warning for Monroe Stahr, Cecilia's father's partner, and then, before the plane departed Nashville, put an end to himself. It was the first death, and like most first deaths in a story, it established the key in which everything that followed would be played.

Monroe Stahr was thirty-five years old and he had been, for nearly a decade, the presiding intelligence of a major Hollywood studio. He had come to pictures as a boy, one of those prodigies of will and instinct whom the industry occasionally produces, and by his middle twenties he was running things with a sureness that older men found unnerving. He was not educated in the way that word is understood back East, at places like Princeton or New Haven, but he possessed a rarer thing, which was an almost infallible sense of what a picture needed to be. He could look at a rough cut and know precisely where it failed, could sit with a writer for an hour and untangle a script that had defeated three previous craftsmen. His talent was not for creation exactly, but for the orchestration of other people's creations, and he exercised it with a devotion that had, over the years, consumed nearly everything else in his life.

He had been married once, to Minna Davis, a star of considerable beauty and no small talent, and her death had left in him a wound that would not close. He carried it the way some men carry a war injury, with a careful stoicism that fooled almost everyone. He worked. That was his answer to grief and to everything else. He worked with a ferocity that his doctors warned him against and his associates marveled at, arriving at the studio before dawn and remaining long after the last technician had gone home. The work was killing him, and he knew it, and he did not stop.

Cecilia Brady was in love with Monroe Stahr. She had been in love with him for some time, in the way that very young women sometimes fall in love with men who are both powerful and melancholy, mistaking their remoteness for depth and their exhaustion for romantic suffering. Stahr was kind to her, as he was kind to most people, but he did not see her, not really, not in the way she wanted to be seen. She was Pat Brady's daughter, and that fact positioned her simultaneously too close and too far away. She narrated all of this with a frankness that occasionally surprised even herself, looking back on it later from a distance that was not merely temporal but spiritual, as though she were examining the artifacts of a civilization she had once inhabited and could no longer quite believe in.

Her father, Pat Brady, was a different species of Hollywood man altogether. Where Stahr was an artist in a businessman's clothing, Brady was a businessman through and through, possessed of that particular cunning that requires no imagination and is therefore almost impossible to defeat. He had come into his share of the studio through a combination of luck and low strategy, and he maintained it by the same methods. He understood money and contracts and the mechanics of corporate betrayal; what he did not understand, and what Stahr understood with every fiber of his being, was pictures. This incomprehension did not trouble Brady. It is a peculiarity of the American business temperament that it can regard its own ignorance of the product it sells as a kind of higher wisdom, a freedom from sentimental attachment that allows for clearer thinking. Brady thought very clearly indeed, and most of his clearest thoughts concerned the removal of Monroe Stahr.

The earthquake came on a summer evening, one of those convulsions of the California earth that remind you that the ground beneath all that sunlit artifice is restless and unreliable. Cecilia had come to the studio for her father's birthday, and the tremor buckled the lot, rupturing water mains and sending a flood coursing through the back lot like a small biblical disaster. The sets, those elaborate facades of other worlds, were suddenly awash, and amid the wreckage and the rising water, two women were found clinging to the head of a goddess, a prop from some picture or another, floating along like a pagan apparition. One of them was a woman whose face stopped Stahr's heart.

She looked like Minna. Not exactly like Minna, perhaps, but close enough that in the uncertain light and the confusion of the moment, Stahr felt something tear open inside him that he had spent years carefully sealing shut. It was the kind of resemblance that is more dangerous than an exact replica, because it allows the imagination to complete what the eye has begun, and Stahr's imagination, starved of everything but work for so long, completed it extravagantly. The woman disappeared into the chaos of the evening, and Stahr was left with nothing but the memory of a face and the terrible rekindling of a feeling he had believed was dead.

But first there was the matter of the studio itself, the daily machinery of Stahr's empire, which Fitzgerald rendered with the loving attention of a man who understood that the texture of work is the texture of life. Stahr's days were symphonies of decision, each one containing a hundred small crises that would have overwhelmed a lesser man. He reviewed dailies in the early morning darkness, alone in the screening room with a cup of coffee and his infallible eye, noting the angle that was wrong, the performance that was false, the cut that came a beat too late. He met with writers, sometimes four or five in a single morning, each bringing a different problem to lay at his feet, and he solved them all with a facility that made it look easy, which is the surest sign that it was not. He understood actors with the intuition of a man who had never wanted to be one, knowing exactly how much direction to give and when to leave them alone, knowing that the best performances come not from instruction but from the creation of conditions in which truth can emerge.

There was George Boxley, for instance, a distinguished English novelist whom the studio had imported at great expense, operating under the common delusion that a man who could write good prose could also write good pictures. Boxley could not. He produced scripts that were literate and lifeless, beautifully constructed sentences that died the moment an actor tried to speak them aloud. Stahr worked with him patiently, trying to explain the fundamental difference between a medium that lives on the page and one that lives on the screen, and the sessions had the quality of a collision between two civilizations, each convinced of its own superiority. Stahr told him a story, an apparently simple anecdote about a woman in an office, a pair of black gloves, a nickel, and a stove, and the story was designed to demonstrate something about the way pictures communicate, through images and actions rather than words, through the accumulation of concrete details that add up to an emotion that could never be stated directly. Boxley listened and did not understand, which was neither his fault nor Stahr's but simply the distance between two different ways of seeing the world.

And there was Pete Zavras, a cameraman of genuine skill who had been blacklisted on the false rumor that he was going blind. Stahr investigated, found the rumor baseless, and reinstated him, an act of simple justice that nonetheless required the expenditure of political capital that Stahr could ill afford. It was characteristic of him, this willingness to spend his influence on behalf of people who could do nothing for him in return. He treated the studio's employees with a decency that was almost feudal in its paternalism, believing that their welfare was his responsibility and that this responsibility was not merely ethical but practical, that a studio staffed by frightened, resentful people would make frightened, resentful pictures. Brady, naturally, regarded this philosophy as sentimental nonsense, a drain on profits that could not be justified by any ledger he knew how to read.

He found her. Of course he found her. Men like Stahr, who command entire studios and thousands of employees, who can summon writers and directors and stars with a word, do not lose track of a woman they are determined to find. Her name was Kathleen Moore, and she was Irish, and she had come to Hollywood by a route that was not entirely conventional. She had been, before coming to America, the companion of a European monarch, a deposed king who had kept her in a kind of gilded captivity that was not quite love and not quite anything else. She had escaped that life, or been released from it, and had come west with the intention of beginning again, which is the intention that California has always specialized in encouraging and almost never fulfills.

Stahr pursued her with a single-mindedness that alarmed even him. He was not a man accustomed to pursuing anyone; in his world, people came to him. But Kathleen Moore existed outside his world, which was precisely what drew him to her. She had no interest in pictures, no ambition to be discovered, no angle to work. She was simply a woman, beautiful and somewhat mysterious, and Stahr found in her presence a relief from the exhausting performance of being Monroe Stahr that he had not known he craved.

Their courtship, if it could be called that, had the quality of a dream, those encounters that feel both inevitable and impossible, charged with a significance that the participants can sense but not quite articulate. He took her to his unfinished house on the beach, a place he had begun building and never completed, and there, with the Pacific stretching out before them into the dark, they became lovers. It was, for Stahr, a resurrection. The dead part of him stirred and woke, and for a brief time he was not the boy wonder or the studio chief or the man who was working himself into the grave, but simply a man in love, which is the simplest and most complicated thing a man can be.

But Kathleen carried with her a complication that she revealed by letter, in that particular cowardice of the written word that people resort to when they cannot bear to watch the effect of what they have to say. She was engaged to be married. The man was someone she had known before Stahr, someone who represented safety and obligation and the kind of respectable future that a woman of her history might reasonably aspire to. She had not planned to fall in love with Stahr, and the fact that she had did not, in her estimation, release her from her prior commitment. There is a certain kind of honor that looks very much like self-destruction, and Kathleen possessed it.

Stahr received this news with the cold shock of a man who has allowed himself to hope and been punished for it. He might have fought for her. He might have marshaled all the resources of his considerable charm and power and made a case for himself that no reasonable woman could resist. But something in him hesitated, some combination of pride and fatalism and the lingering conviction that happiness was not meant for him, and in that hesitation, Kathleen made her choice. She married the other man. Her telegram arrived with the brutal economy of the form: I WAS MARRIED AT NOON TODAY GOODBYE. And that was that, or should have been.

But it was not. The affair continued, as affairs do, in secret and in shame, acquiring in its clandestine phase a feverish intensity that it might never have achieved in the open. Kathleen came to Stahr and Stahr went to Kathleen, and between them there existed a passion that was inseparable from its own impossibility. They loved each other best when they were most forbidden to do so, which is the oldest and saddest pattern in the history of human attachment. Stahr's unfinished house on the beach became their meeting place, that half-built structure standing on the sand like an architectural confession of its owner's inability to complete anything personal, a man who could bring a hundred pictures to flawless completion each year but could not finish the house in which he meant to live. The Pacific rolled in beneath them and the stars hung over them and everything was beautiful and temporary, which is to say everything was exactly like Hollywood itself.

Cecilia, meanwhile, watched all of this from her particular angle of exclusion. She was close enough to Stahr to observe his transformation, to note the way his eyes changed when he spoke of a certain woman whose name he did not mention, to register the small alterations in his mood that signaled a heart in the process of reopening. She loved him with the hopeless devotion of someone who knows she will never be chosen, and there was in her love a generosity that coexisted with its pain, a willingness to want his happiness even when that happiness depended on someone else. She was, in her way, the most selfless character in the story, though she would not have described herself that way, preferring instead the wry self-deprecation that was her defense against a world that kept disappointing her. Wylie White, the screenwriter, orbited her with his own brand of hopeless devotion, seeing in her connection to Pat Brady a useful alliance that he tried to dress up as romance. Cecilia saw through him with the clarity of a woman who had grown up watching men calculate, but there were moments when his company was better than loneliness, which is the basis for more relationships than anyone likes to admit.

Meanwhile, the studio was becoming a battleground. The writers were organizing, as writers in Hollywood periodically do, seized by the sudden conviction that they deserved better treatment from an industry that used their talents and discarded their persons with equal facility. A Communist labor organizer named Brimmer had appeared on the scene, a man of considerable intelligence and no sentimentality, who saw in Hollywood's labor disputes an opportunity to advance certain larger ideological goals. Stahr, who was neither a political man nor an ideological one, viewed the labor question with the practical concern of someone who needed his writers working, not striking, and who genuinely believed that he treated them fairly, which, by the standards of the time, he more or less did.

Stahr arranged, through Cecilia, to meet with Brimmer, imagining that he could reason with the man, that the force of his personality and the evident justice of his position would carry the day. It was the miscalculation of a man accustomed to getting his way in every room he entered. The meeting took place on the evening that Stahr received Kathleen's telegram, and he arrived at it already wounded, already off balance, already reaching for the whiskey that he almost never drank. The evening deteriorated with the inevitability of a classical tragedy. Stahr, drunk and grieving and unable to separate his personal anguish from the professional confrontation before him, became belligerent. He said things about his colleagues that were uncharacteristically cruel. He challenged Brimmer physically, which was both brave and absurd, since Stahr was a slight man weakened by years of overwork and Brimmer was not. The organizer knocked him unconscious with what witnesses described as reluctance, and Stahr lay on the floor in a posture of defeat that seemed to encompass not merely this one encounter but his entire trajectory, the long downward arc of a man who had climbed too high on too little strength.

Pat Brady watched all of this with the attentiveness of a predator. He had been waiting for Stahr to weaken, and now Stahr was weakening everywhere at once, in his body, in his judgment, in his grip on the studio. Brady moved to consolidate his position with the methodical cruelty of a man who has no imagination but excellent instincts. While Stahr was away in Washington, attempting to persuade the Eastern stockholders to hold the line on salaries, Brady pushed through the wage cuts that Stahr had opposed, betraying the writers and anyone else who stood in the way of the economies he believed the studio required. It was a betrayal executed with boardroom precision, the kind of corporate violence that leaves no fingerprints and generates no sympathy.

When Stahr returned, diminished by illness and defeat, he found the landscape of the studio altered against him. Brady had the advantage now, and he pressed it with increasing boldness. He discovered Stahr's continuing affair with Kathleen and seized upon it as an instrument of destruction. The threat of a lawsuit for alienation of affection, the public exposure of the relationship, the ruin of whatever remained of Stahr's reputation for probity and self-control, these were the weapons Brady assembled, and he deployed them with the cheerful ruthlessness of a man who has finally found his adversary's weakness.

Stahr, cornered, responded in the only way that seemed available to him, which was to escalate. The logic of their conflict had acquired its own momentum, carrying both men past the boundaries of corporate rivalry into something more primitive and more deadly. Brady hired a man to kill Stahr. This was not metaphorical. This was not the usual Hollywood talk of destroying someone's career or reputation. This was murder, contracted and paid for, the final expression of a business disagreement that had metastasized into something personal and absolute.

And Stahr, learning of this or sensing it with the instinct that had served him so well in pictures and so poorly in life, hired his own man to kill Brady. It was a decision made in extremity, in the grip of that particular desperation that overtakes a man when he realizes that the rules he has lived by no longer apply, that the civilized surface of his world has cracked open to reveal the wilderness beneath. He arranged the killing and then he boarded a plane to fly east, to New York, where he would be far from the scene and could claim the innocence of distance.

But somewhere over the dark continent, at altitude, with the engines droning and the night pressing against the windows, Stahr changed his mind. It came to him the way his best decisions about pictures had always come to him, not through reasoning but through a sudden clarity of vision, a recognition that what he was about to do was not merely wrong but impossible, that it would transform him into something he could not be and still be himself. He decided to telephone from the next stop and call the whole thing off. He would find another way. There was always another way, if you had the imagination to see it and the courage to take it.

The plane never reached its next stop. It went down somewhere in the darkness, carrying Monroe Stahr and his unexecuted reprieve into oblivion. The crash was the kind of accident that seems, in the narrative of a life, less like chance than like fate, as though the story had found its own ending despite the intentions of everyone involved. Stahr died with his better nature intact but unacted upon, which may be the most Fitzgerald of all possible deaths, the redemption that arrives a moment too late, the green light that recedes even as you reach for it.

The wreckage was found by children from a remote area, mountain children who knew nothing of Hollywood and its hierarchies, and among the scattered belongings of the dead they discovered Stahr's personal effects, his briefcase, his letters, the small artifacts of a life lived at the highest velocity, and they distributed them among themselves with the innocent acquisitiveness of scavengers who do not understand the value of what they have found. A producer's memoranda became a child's plaything. A letter from Kathleen, perhaps, became a curiosity to be puzzled over and discarded. There was something terrible and fitting in this image, the remnants of a great man's life parceled out among strangers who would never know his name, a final democratization that Hollywood, for all its democratic pretensions, would never have permitted in life. It was the kind of image that Fitzgerald specialized in, the single concrete detail that carries the weight of an entire theme, and it spoke to everything the novel had been saying about the impermanence of power and the indifference of the universe to human achievement.

Fitzgerald had planned that Stahr's funeral would be an event of spectacular hypocrisy, every figure in the industry turning out to mourn publicly a man they had privately conspired against, a grotesque inversion of Gatsby's empty funeral, which had been tragic in its loneliness where Stahr's would be tragic in its crowdedness, in the sheer volume of false grief that would fill the chapel and overflow into the California sunshine. Brady would be there, of course, wearing the appropriate expression, perhaps even shedding the appropriate tear, the assassination he had ordered rendered moot by an act of God. The writers Stahr had championed and the executives who had resented him and the actors whose careers he had built would all gather in a display of unity that would have been unthinkable in life, and the eulogies would be magnificent and false, full of the kind of sentiments that Hollywood produces more reliably than it produces good pictures. It would be a funeral worthy of the industry, which is to say it would be a production, carefully staged, expertly performed, and utterly devoid of the one thing that might have redeemed it, which was sincerity.

Cecilia Brady survived to tell the story, as narrators do, though her survival was not without its own costs. She told it from a tuberculosis sanatorium, looking back on the events with the double vision of someone who had been close enough to see everything and young enough to understand almost nothing at the time. Her love for Stahr remained unrequited, a footnote to the larger romance that consumed and destroyed him, and there was in her narration a quality of elegy that extended beyond Stahr to encompass the whole world he had inhabited, that bright, brutal, ephemeral kingdom of celluloid and ambition that was Hollywood in its golden age.

The story Fitzgerald left behind was incomplete, a cathedral with one wall missing, but what remained was sufficient to suggest the grandeur of the intended whole. Monroe Stahr was his most fully realized character, more complex than Gatsby, more sympathetic than Dick Diver, a man whose virtues and whose destruction were equally American. Stahr believed in the work. He believed that pictures mattered, that they could be made well and honestly, that the commercial machinery of the studio system need not crush the artistic impulse but could, in the right hands, serve it. This belief was his glory and his undoing, because the system he served did not share it, and the men who controlled the system, men like Brady, understood that belief of any kind is a vulnerability in a world that runs on calculation.

What Fitzgerald understood about Hollywood, and what he translated into prose with the precision of a man who had lived it and nearly been destroyed by it, was that the industry was not merely a business but a way of life, a complete civilization with its own laws and customs and moral codes. The hierarchies of the studio lot were as rigid and as arbitrary as those of any feudal court, and the people who inhabited them understood their positions with a clarity that would have impressed a medieval herald. There were the stars, of course, who occupied the highest rank and enjoyed the most privileges, but whose power was the most precarious, dependent as it was on the shifting tastes of a public that could love you on Tuesday and forget you by Thursday. There were the directors, who believed themselves to be artists and were sometimes right. There were the writers, who knew themselves to be essential and were treated as though they were expendable. There were the producers, who controlled the money and therefore controlled everything, and among the producers there were men like Stahr, who used their power in the service of something larger than themselves, and men like Brady, who used it in the service of nothing at all.

The novel moved through this world the way Fitzgerald's earlier work had moved through Long Island and the Riviera, with an eye for the telling detail, the gesture that reveals the soul of a place. The studio lot, with its false fronts and its manufactured weather, its armies of extras and its hierarchies of talent, was a landscape as rich and strange as any Fitzgerald had ever inhabited, and he rendered it with the appreciative precision of a man who recognized in its gorgeous fraudulence a mirror of everything he had spent his life writing about. For what was Hollywood but the American dream made literal, projected onto screens in darkened theaters for the consumption of millions, the promise of beauty and success and transformation that had always been the country's most seductive and most dangerous export?

Stahr moved through this world as both its master and its prisoner. He could make the dream, frame by frame, but he could not live it. His own life was a study in the distance between the perfection he could achieve on screen and the chaos that attended his every personal endeavor. His love for Kathleen was real and doomed, like all the loves in Fitzgerald's fiction, and it was doomed for the same reason, because the world does not organize itself around the desires of the heart, no matter how pure or how desperate those desires may be. Kathleen married the wrong man because she could not trust the right one, and Stahr lost her because he could not move fast enough, because the hesitation that was his curse in love was the mirror image of the decisiveness that was his gift in work.

The industrial machinery of the studio provided Fitzgerald with a metaphor that he exploited with characteristic subtlety. The making of a picture is a collaborative act that depends upon the subordination of individual vision to collective effort, and yet the best pictures, like the best works of any art, bear the unmistakable stamp of a single sensibility. Stahr was that sensibility. He was the auteur before the word existed, the man whose taste and judgment shaped every frame that left his studio, and his struggle to maintain that authority against the encroachments of commerce and politics and the simple entropy of institutional life was Fitzgerald's most mature engagement with the themes that had preoccupied him since the beginning.

There was in The Last Tycoon a quality of acceptance that distinguished it from the earlier novels, a recognition that defeat is not merely possible but inevitable, and that the measure of a man is not whether he wins but how he conducts himself in the losing. Stahr lost everything, his health, his love, his studio, and finally his life, but he lost it all with a kind of dignity that redeemed the loss, that transformed it from mere failure into something approaching tragedy. He was better than the world he lived in, which is the definition of a Fitzgerald hero, and the world destroyed him for it, which is the definition of a Fitzgerald plot.

The dual narration of the novel, that peculiar blend of Cecilia's first-person account and the third-person scenes she could not have witnessed, gave the story a quality of hovering between intimacy and omniscience, as though the tale were being told by someone who had pieced it together afterward from fragments and confessions and her own informed guessing. Cecilia admitted as much, acknowledged that some of what she related she had learned secondhand, that some of it she had inferred, that some of it she had simply imagined, and this honesty about the limitations of her knowledge gave her narration a paradoxical authority. She was not pretending to know everything, and because she was not pretending, you trusted her more when she claimed to know something. It was a narrative technique of considerable sophistication, the kind of thing that looks effortless and is not, and it allowed Fitzgerald to move freely between the interior life of his characters and the exterior world they inhabited without the awkwardness that usually attends such transitions.

The book was the last thing Fitzgerald wrote, and it carried in its unfinished pages the weight of everything he had learned about writing and about life. He was forty-four when he died, which is too young by any measure, and he died knowing that his reputation had declined, that the books he had written were out of print, that the literary world had moved on to other enthusiasms. He could not have known that the reputation would revive, that Gatsby would become the great American novel in the estimation of generations yet unborn, that his name would outlast the names of almost everyone who had dismissed him. He knew only that he had one more story to tell, and that it was a good one, perhaps the best he had ever attempted, and that he was running out of time.

The pages that survive are enough. They are enough to see the shape of the thing, the way you can see the shape of a Greek temple from its remaining columns, enough to feel the power of the story and the beauty of the prose and the depth of the feeling that informed every sentence. Monroe Stahr walks through these pages with the doomed vitality of a man who has been granted a temporary reprieve from mortality and knows it, and the world he walks through glitters with the hard, transient brilliance of a dream that is just beginning to dissolve in the light of morning. It is a fragment, yes, but it is a fragment of something that would have been magnificent, and even in its incompleteness it stands as a testament to what American fiction might achieve when it is written by a man who has nothing left to lose and everything left to say.
