Late on a summer evening the proprietor of Manor Farm came unsteadily up the lane, smelling of gin from the Red Lion taproom and scarcely steady on his boots as he crossed the cobbled yard. He drew the heavy bolts upon the doors, blew out the lantern on the kitchen lintel, and stumbled into bed beside his sleeping wife. The moment his light vanished from the bedroom window, an expectant rustle swept across the barns, the sties, and the open paddocks. Word had traveled throughout the afternoon that Old Major, the prize Middle White boar who had commanded the respect of the entire parish for a dozen years, had experienced a strange dream which he intended to impart to the farm before he died.

They assembled inside the long threshing barn, where the crossbeams were thick with the dust of past harvests. Upon a low platform of clean straw beneath a hurricane lamp, the old boar sat waiting, his tusks yellowed with age but his gray eyes still bright with intelligence. The dogs, Bluebell, Jessie, and Pincher, were the first to settle themselves on the stones, followed by the pigs, who arranged their bulk directly before the dais. Next came the two cart-horses, Boxer and Clover, stepping into the sawdust with immense care and setting down their heavy, hairy hoofs as though fearful of trampling some smaller creature hidden beneath the chaff.

Muriel, the white goat, entered beside Benjamin the donkey, the oldest and sourliest inhabitant of the county, who seldom opened his jaws except to deliver some cynical remark on the vanity of labor. A flock of white ducks drifted in after them, quacking because their mother was gone, while Mollie, the foolish white mare who pulled the farmer's trap, trotted near the entrance and fluttered her white mane in hope of having her ribbons admired. Last of all, the barn cat crept toward the warmth of the horses' flanks, purring steadily and paying no heed whatever to the solemnity of the occasion.

When silence settled over the company, the old boar cleared his throat and addressed them with a voice that was deep, quiet, and surprisingly steady. He reminded them that the life of a working beast was short, miserable, and laborious. No creature in England was allowed to live out its natural term; each was fed merely enough to keep breath in its ribs, worked to the absolute limit of its sinews, and slaughtered without mercy the moment its usefulness failed. The fields around them were fertile and the climate mild, yet poverty remained their inescapable lot because the entire fruit of their sweat was stolen away by human hands.

Man alone, he told them, was the sole creature that consumed without producing. Man yielded no milk, dropped no eggs, was too feeble to draw a plow through heavy loam, and ran too slowly to catch a rabbit. Yet he was lord of every living thing upon the land, compelling the horse to haul the dung-cart and the hen to surrender her brood, returning only the barest scrap of chaff to prevent starvation while the remainder was squandered in idleness. The solution, Major insisted, was plain: the overthrow of human rule through an unsparing, universal rebellion.

He warned them against ever adopting the vices of their oppressors. No animal must ever live inside a brick house, sleep upon a mattress, dress in clothes, drink alcohol, touch paper money, or engage in trade. Above all, no creature must ever tyrannize or destroy another of its kind, for all creatures that went upon four legs or possessed wings were brothers in misfortune. He then recalled the dream of his youth, describing an earth purged of whips, harnesses, bits, and slaughterhouses, and taught the assembly an ancient anthem of liberation that stirred the barn with such wild ferocity that Jones woke, seized his gun, and discharged a charge of buckshot blindly into the darkness.

Three nights afterward the old boar died peacefully in his sleep, and was buried at the edge of the orchard. During the early spring months, his words worked like yeast beneath the surface of the farm's routine. The work of organizing fell upon the pigs, acknowledged by all to be the cleverest beasts of the yard. Two young boars stood out: Snowball, an active, inventive beast with a gift for lively speech, and Napoleon, a large, fierce-looking Berkshire boar who spoke seldom and had a reputation for having his own way. Between them stood Squealer, a small fat porker with round pink cheeks, nimble movements, and a shrill voice that seemed to bounce like a rubber ball, whose persuasive tongue was said to turn black into white.

These three elaborated Old Major's teachings into a system of thought they called Animalism, holding secret nocturnal meetings in the hayloft to explain its tenets to the simpler creatures. They met with stiff resistance from beasts who regarded Jones as their lawful master and worried who would feed them if he were gone. Moses, the tame raven, flew from roof to roof telling tales of Sugarcandy Mountain high above the clouds, where sugar grew on hedges and clover flourished in every season. The pigs had to labor tirelessly to dispel these fantasies. Their most dependable disciples were the carters, Boxer and Clover, who absorbed every word with reverent simplicity and passed it along to the others.

The uprising arrived far earlier than anyone had dared to hope. During the summer Jones fell into serious financial trouble, having lost money in a lawsuit, and spent his afternoons drinking heavily at the Red Lion while his laborers grew idle, dishonest, and neglectful. The pastures choked with weeds, the fences decayed, and the animals were frequently left without food. On Midsummer's Eve, Jones drove into town and did not return until Sunday noon, leaving his hands to go rabbiting without bothering to feed the stock. By evening the beasts were frantic with hunger. One of the cows broke the door of the store-shed with her horn, and the animals rushed in to help themselves to the grain bins.

At that moment Jones and his men burst into the yard with cart-whips, lashing out furiously in every direction. Hunger instantly turned to uncontrolled rage. Without signal or premeditation, the animals flung themselves upon their tormentors, kicking, butting, and biting with an unexpected fury that terrified the men out of their wits. Within five minutes the human beings were fleeing in panic down the muddy lane toward the main road, with the beasts pursuing them to the five-barred gate. Mrs. Jones bundled a few belongings into a carpet-bag and slipped out through the orchard hedge, while Moses flapped after her with a loud croak. When the gate slammed shut, the animals were masters of the farm.

Their first act was to gallop around the boundaries to assure themselves that no human remained in hiding; their second was to wipe out every trace of Jones's reign. The bits, nose-rings, dog-chains, knives, and hated whips were gathered from the harness-room and cast into a roaring bonfire in the middle of the yard. Snowball threw the ribbons with which the carthorses' manes had been decorated into the flames, declaring that ribbons were the emblem of a human master and that no animal should go dressed. Next morning they toured the farmhouse with hushed footsteps, gazing at the carpets and feather beds. By unanimous consent the house was declared a museum, with a solemn resolution that no animal should ever reside within its walls.

Then Snowball and Napoleon assembled them before the big barn. The pigs revealed that during the preceding three months they had taught themselves to read and write from an old spelling book found in the rubbish. Snowball took a brush between the knuckles of his trotter, climbed a ladder, and painted out the name Manor Farm upon the main gate, substituting Animal Farm in clean white lettering. Returning to the tarred wall of the barn, he inscribed the Seven Commandments of their new philosophy in tall characters: Whatever goes upon two legs is an enemy; whatever goes upon four legs, or has wings, is a friend; no animal shall wear clothes; no animal shall sleep in a bed; no animal shall drink alcohol; no animal shall kill any other animal; all animals are equal.

Before they could turn their attention to the hay, the dairy cows set up a loud lowing, their udders swollen after twenty-four hours without milking. The pigs fetched wooden pails and milked them with practiced trotters, producing five brimming buckets of creamy milk that drew the thirsty gaze of the entire company. When several animals asked what was to be done with the pails, Napoleon stepped in front of them and commanded everyone to turn toward the fields, promising that the harvest was far more urgent and that the milk would be attended to without delay. When the company returned from the hayfield in the cool of the dusk, the five buckets were entirely empty.

The hay harvest proved to be a magnificent success, gathered more rapidly and cleanly than in any year under human stewardship. Every creature participated according to its ability, the ducks and hens carrying stray wisps of grass in their beaks while the horses pulled the wagons with steady power. The pigs did not engage in manual labor, explaining that their superior brains were required to supervise operations and resolve dilemmas. Boxer stood out above all others, straining his immense chest against the harness and moving from morning until night with the strength of three ordinary horses. Whenever a load was heavy or a wheel stuck fast in the ruts, his answer was always the same quiet resolution: "I will work harder."

Sunday was set aside as a day of ceremony and rest. It began with the raising of a green tablecloth upon which Snowball had painted a white hoof and horn, symbolizing the green fields of England and the future republic of the beasts. Afterward came the general assembly in the barn, where work for the coming week was debated and put to the vote. It was observed that while the other animals offered occasional comments, the motions were invariably introduced by the pigs, and that Napoleon and Snowball were never in agreement upon any single measure, each contesting the other's proposals with bitter, stubborn tenacity.

During the afternoons Snowball organized the animals into committees. There was a clean tails league for the cows, an educational campaign for the wild rabbits and rats, and systematic reading classes for all species. The results were unequal: the dogs learned to read tolerably well, Muriel could read scraps of newspaper retrieved from the rubbish heap, and Clover knew the alphabet without being able to join the letters into syllables. Boxer could never advance beyond the letter D, tracing the four characters in the mud with his hoof and staring at them in defeat. For the sheep, who could not master a single letter, Snowball condensed the principles of Animalism into an easily remembered maxim: "Four legs good, two legs bad."

The mystery of the disappeared milk was soon joined by that of the early windfall apples, which had been swept into the harness-room for the exclusive consumption of the pigs. When murmurs of dissatisfaction arose among the sheep and cows, Squealer was dispatched to explain the administrative necessity. He assured them with vigorous whisks of his tail that the pigs did not consume the apples and cream out of selfishness, but solely to preserve their health and intellectual stamina. The management of the farm, he insisted, depended entirely upon their brainwork; should the pigs fail for lack of proper nourishment, Jones would undoubtedly return to claim his property. Faced with the dread prospect of the farmer's return, the animals agreed without another word that the whole apple harvest should be reserved for the pigs alone.

As summer waned, flocks of pigeons were dispatched across the county to spread the news of the rebellion and teach the melody of their anthem to the beasts of neighboring estates. The human farmers were thoroughly frightened. Mr. Pilkington, the easy-going gentleman who owned Foxwood, an enormous and neglected property overgrown with brambles, and Mr. Frederick, the shrewd, tight-fisted owner of Pinchfield, a smaller but better-kept farm, were perpetually at odds, yet both were terrified that their own laborers might catch the contagion of revolt. They spread absurd rumors through the Willingdon public houses, claiming that the animals at Animal Farm were starving, practicing cannibalism, and torturing one another with red-hot irons.

In early October, Jones returned. Accompanied by half a dozen laborers from Foxwood and Pinchfield, all carrying heavy sticks, and armed himself with a twelve-bore gun, the farmer marched up the drive to recover his lost land. Snowball, who had made a thorough study of Julius Caesar's campaigns from a book found in the farmhouse library, had long prepared for this emergency. He launched his defense in three calculated phases, first throwing thirty pigeons over the men's heads to drop dung while the geese pecked viciously at their calves, then sending a detachment of sheep to butt them into confusion before executing a prearranged retreat into the main farmyard.

Thinking the day won, the men charged through the gate, only to find their rear cut off by three horses, two cows, and the remaining pigs emerging from the cowshed. Snowball charged straight at Jones, receiving a pattern of buckshot across his back and losing a sheep to the farmer's second barrel before driving his weight into the man's legs and tumbling him into a dung-heap. Boxer struck out with his iron-shod hoofs, catching a stable-lad on the skull and stretching him lifeless in the mud. Horrified by the sudden fury of the ambush, the surviving humans dropped their sticks and ran howling for the roadway, leaving the yard in the undisputed possession of the animals. Boxer wept over the motionless boy until the lad revived and fled. A solemn funeral was held for the fallen sheep, and a military decoration, Animal Hero, First Class, was conferred upon Snowball and Boxer, while Jones's gun was mounted beside the flagstaff to be fired twice a year.

As winter closed in, Mollie's behavior became increasingly troublesome. She reported late for work each morning, complained of mysterious pains in her hoof, and spent hours admiring her reflection in the horse-pond. Clover eventually confronted her, having spotted her near the boundary hedge allowing a neighboring groom from Willingdon to stroke her nose and feed her sugar. Upon searching Mollie's stall, Clover discovered a little hoard of sugar lumps and a bunch of blue ribbons hidden beneath the straw. Three days later the mare vanished altogether; pigeons reported that she had been seen standing outside a public house in Willingdon, newly clipped, wearing a scarlet ribbon in her forelock, and eating sugar from the hand of a fat man in checked breeches.

The winter months brought bitter frosts and fierce political dissension between the two boars. Snowball's fertile imagination led him to spend weeks locked inside the old incubator shed, drawing elaborate mechanical diagrams with pieces of chalk upon the smooth wooden floor. He proposed the construction of a stone windmill upon the highest knoll of the farm, which would drive a dynamo to supply electric light, heat the stalls in winter, run circular saws, and power milking machines. By this means, he argued, the heavy drudgery of the farm would be eliminated and the working week reduced to three days, allowing the animals leisure to cultivate their minds.

Napoleon showed nothing but contempt for the project. He walked to the incubator shed, examined the concentric circles and gear-teeth in absolute silence, then suddenly lifted his trotter, urinated over the blueprints, and strolled out without uttering a syllable. The entire farm divided into two hostile factions, arguing whether stone should be gathered for the windmill or whether food production should take precedence. The division reached its climax on the Sunday when the plans were completed and the animals assembled in the barn to decide the issue by a show of hands.

Snowball rose to defend his scheme with passionate eloquence, painting a picture of future abundance that completely captured the enthusiasm of the assembly. Just as he concluded, Napoleon stood up, cast a peculiar sidelong glance at his rival, and uttered a low, whimpering squeak that no one had ever heard him make before. At that instant nine enormous, ferocious dogs wearing brass-studded collars came bounding into the barn. They made straight for Snowball, who sprang from the platform just in time to escape their snapping jaws, tearing out through the door with the pack in deadly pursuit. He scrambled through a gap in the hedge, grazed by the teeth of the leading hound, and vanished from the farm forever.

The animals stood frozen in silent terror as the dogs trotted back into the barn and took up positions directly behind Napoleon, wagging their tails in the same servile fashion that dogs had once used toward Jones. Napoleon mounted the platform and announced in a hard voice that Sunday morning debates were abolished immediately. All questions relating to the administration of the farm would henceforth be decided by a special committee of pigs presided over by himself, meeting in private. Squealer was sent through the yards to explain the arrangement, assuring the beasts that Napoleon had assumed the dreadful burden of leadership only out of pure self-sacrifice, and hinting darkly that Snowball's role at the Cowshed had been greatly exaggerated. Boxer pondered the matter deeply and adopted a second motto: "Napoleon is always right."

A fortnight later, Napoleon surprised the farm by announcing that the windmill would be built after all. Squealer explained confidentially that Napoleon had favored the windmill from the very beginning, and that the diagrams on the incubator floor had actually been stolen from Napoleon's personal papers. When the animals asked why he had spoken so vehemently against it, Squealer skipped merrily from side to side, whisking his tail and explaining that this was a masterstroke of tactics designed to get rid of the dangerous and corrupting influence of Snowball. The explanation sounded complicated, but Squealer spoke so persuasively and the growling dogs were so large that the beasts accepted the story without further dispute.

The building of the windmill proved to be an exhausting, protracted ordeal that consumed the entire year. The stone lay in an old limestone quarry on the property, but breaking the boulders into manageable pieces seemed an insurmountable task until the pigs hit upon the expedient of dragging the immense rocks to the summit of the quarry and toppling them over the edge so that they shattered on the floor below. Boxer spent hours hauling limestone under the blazing sun, getting up an hour before the others to work alone in the quarry before the day's regular labor commenced. Food rations were reduced, and work on Sunday afternoons was made strictly voluntary, though any animal who absented himself suffered a fifty percent reduction in his corn allowance.

Despite their heroic efforts, serious shortages of manufactured goods began to plague the estate. There was an urgent need for paraffin oil, nails, string, horse-shoes, and iron for the windmill machinery. Napoleon therefore informed the assembly that Animal Farm would enter into commercial relations with the neighboring human parishes, not for the purpose of trade in the ordinary sense, but purely to secure the materials necessary for the completion of the mill. To handle the negotiations, he engaged Mr. Whymper, a sharp-featured little solicitor from Willingdon, who visited the farm every Monday morning to receive his instructions. The sight of a human being taking orders from four-legged beasts gave the animals a strange thrill of pride, though it troubled their recollection of the early resolutions against dealing with humanity.

Unease deepened when the pigs quietly abandoned the sties and moved their sleeping quarters into the farmhouse itself, taking their meals in the kitchen and using the drawing-room as a place of recreation. Rumors spread that the leaders were sleeping in the feather beds. Clover, remembering a distinct injunction against mattresses, walked to the barn wall and asked Muriel to read the Fourth Commandment. With some difficulty Muriel spelled out the words: "No animal shall sleep in a bed with sheets." Squealer, who chanced to be passing with a bodyguard of dogs, explained that a bed was merely a place to sleep, and that a pile of straw in a stall was as much a bed as any other; the true evil lay in the use of sheets, which were an artificial human invention. The pigs, he added, had removed the sheets and slept between blankets, which was essential if their brains were to remain rested enough to prevent the return of Jones.

In November a terrible gale tore across the countryside, stripping the tiles from the barn roof and uprooting a great elm tree near the orchard. When daylight broke, a cry of despair went up from the yard: the half-built windmill lay in ruins, its stone walls leveled to the foundation and its masonry scattered across the grass. As the animals stood gazing at the destruction in stunned silence, Napoleon paced among the debris, sniffing the wet earth with short, jerky snorts. Halting abruptly, he pointed his trotter toward the grass and declared in deep, solemn accents that the ruin was the work of Snowball, who had crept onto the farm under cover of darkness to destroy the labor of an entire year. On the spot he pronounced a sentence of death upon the traitor, offering a bushel of apples to any animal who brought him to justice, and declared that the rebuilding of the walls would begin that very morning, regardless of weather.

The following winter was bitter and cruel, bringing sleet, freezing mud, and a desperate scarcity of food. The corn ration was drastically reduced, and the potato crop was ruined when the clamps were insufficiently banked against the frost. Aware that human observers were watching for signs of failure, Napoleon took elaborate precautions to conceal the famine from Whymper. He ordered the almost empty storage bins in the granary to be filled to the brim with dry sand, over which the remaining grain was smoothed in a thin layer, and instructed the solicitor to be conducted through the building so that he might report to the outside world that food was plentiful on Animal Farm. To obtain grain, Napoleon contracted through Whymper to deliver four hundred eggs a week from the poultry. When the hens protested, flying to the rafters to smash their clutches, Napoleon stopped their rations completely, decreeing that anyone offering them food would be executed. Nine hens died of starvation before the flock capitulated.

Throughout the spring, Snowball was transformed into an invisible, malevolent presence that haunted every corner of the estate. Whenever a window was broken, a drain blocked, or a key misplaced, it was attributed without hesitation to Snowball's nocturnal raids. Squealer soon convened a special meeting to deliver a dreadful revelation: secret documents had been discovered proving that Snowball had been in league with Jones from the very start, and that at the Battle of the Cowshed he had actively attempted to deliver the animals into the hands of the enemy. When Boxer objected, pointing out that Snowball had fought bravely and received the decoration of Animal Hero, First Class, Squealer grew indignant and described how Napoleon had sprung forward at the critical moment, sinking his teeth into Jones's leg while Snowball fled. Boxer remained skeptical until Squealer asserted that Comrade Napoleon had stated the fact plainly, whereupon the honest carter yielded at once, declaring that if Napoleon said so, it must undoubtedly be true.

Four days later, Napoleon summoned a general assembly in the yard. He emerged from the farmhouse wearing both his military medals, guarded by six massive hounds that uttered low growls that sent shivers down the spines of the spectators. At a signal from their master, the dogs leaped forward, seized four young porkers by the ears, and dragged them squealing with pain to Napoleon's feet. Several dogs suddenly turned upon Boxer, snapping at his flanks; the great horse lashed out with his hoof, pinning a hound to the earth beneath his iron shoe while the others retreated in terror. Napoleon commanded Boxer to release the dog, then turned his gaze upon the terrified porkers, who confessed that they had entered into an agreement to surrender the farm to Mr. Frederick of Pinchfield. As soon as their confession was complete, the dogs tore their throats out upon the cobbled stones. Then the three ringleader hens, a goose, and several sheep confessed to fantastic crimes and were slaughtered on the spot until a heap of corpses lay before the leader and the heavy air reeked of warm blood.

When the executions ended, the surviving animals huddled together on the small knoll where the half-built windmill stood, trembling with cold and horror. Boxer remained on his feet, shaking his head and declaring that such things could never have happened if there had not been some terrible fault within themselves; his only remedy was to rise an hour earlier in the morning and work harder. Clover lay down in the grass with the smaller beasts gathered around her. As she looked down the slope toward the peaceful fields, the orchard, and the red roofs of the barns, her eyes filled with tears. This was not the society of equals and brothers that Old Major had promised in his dying vision; it was a place of terror, where no creature dared speak its mind and fierce dogs prowled the yard. To comfort themselves, the beasts began singing the old anthem of liberation, humming the melody softly through their tears. They had barely finished when Squealer arrived, accompanied by two dogs, and announced that by special decree of Comrade Napoleon, the singing of "Beasts of England" was abolished forever. In its place, the poet Minimus composed a short, obedient verse that failed entirely to stir their blood.

A few days later, several animals were troubled by the memory that the Sixth Commandment explicitly forbade the taking of animal life. Clover asked Muriel to read the inscription on the barn wall, and Muriel discovered that it now read: "No animal shall kill any other animal without cause." It was evident that the executions had been entirely justified, since the traitors had manifestly plotted with Snowball to overthrow the state. Meanwhile, the deification of Napoleon proceeded apace. He was styled with formal reverence as "our Leader, Comrade Napoleon," inhabited separate apartments in the farmhouse, took his meals alone off Crown Derby porcelain, and was preceded by a black cockerel who sounded a loud fanfare before the leader spoke. A poem celebrating his wisdom was inscribed upon the barn wall beneath a profile portrait of Napoleon executed by Squealer in white paint.

Complicated negotiations were conducted regarding a pile of seasoned timber that had been stacked in the yard since the days of Jones. Napoleon played the two neighboring proprietors against one another, pretending to favor Mr. Pilkington while secretly conducting talks with Mr. Frederick. Throughout the summer Frederick was denounced as a monster who starved his cows and tortured his horses, while the pigeons were forbidden to enter Pinchfield. In the autumn, however, the farm was astounded to learn that Napoleon had sold the timber to Frederick after all, having extracted an exorbitant price and insisted upon payment in real five-pound banknotes rather than a banker's draft. The triumph was short-lived. Three days after the timber was carted away, Whymper rode into the yard on his bicycle with the dreadful news that Frederick's banknotes were clever forgeries. Napoleon immediately convened the assembly, pronounced a sentence of death upon Frederick, and warned that an armed invasion was imminent.

The attack came with the dawn. Frederick and fifteen men, armed with fowling-pieces, marched onto the property and opened fire as soon as they crossed the gate. The animals were driven back into the barns by the hail of pellets, while the humans surrounded the completed windmill. Two men bored a deep hole into the base of the stone tower and packed it with blasting powder. A moment later a deafening explosion shook the earth, and the windmill dissolved into a cloud of black smoke and flying rubble. The sight of their destroyed monument roused the animals to a pitch of fury that overcame all fear of guns. They charged across the open grass in a savage rush, hurling themselves upon the raiders. Though three sheep, a cow, and two geese were killed, and nearly every animal received buckshot wounds, the humans were driven back across the hedge and fled down the high road. Boxer's hoofs were bleeding, his shoe was torn away, and a dozen pellets were buried in his hide, yet as he surveyed the scattered ruins, Squealer came skipping across the pasture, announcing that a glorious victory had been won.

Jones's gun was fired in salute, and Napoleon instituted a new decoration, the Order of the Green Banner, which he conferred upon himself. That evening loud shouts and songs of revelry echoed from the windows of the farmhouse; the pigs had discovered a case of whisky in the cellars which had been overlooked during the initial inspection. Next morning Squealer emerged walking with a feeble gait, informing the animals that Comrade Napoleon was dying, and that the drinking of alcohol was to be punished by death. By evening, however, the leader had rallied, and two days later Napoleon gave orders that the small paddock behind the orchard, set aside as a grazing ground for retired beasts, should be plowed up and sown with barley. A curious incident occurred soon afterward when a loud crash in the yard roused the farm at midnight: Squealer lay sprawling in the mud beneath the barn wall beside a broken ladder, a lantern, and an overturned pot of white paint. Two days later, Muriel noticed that the Fifth Commandment now read, "No animal shall drink alcohol to excess."

The rebuilding of the windmill began on the day after the celebrations, with Boxer working through the winter cold despite his painful hoof. Rations were cut once more for every animal except the pigs and dogs, Squealer explaining that an excessive equality of food was contrary to the principles of Animalism. To compensate for their empty bellies, Napoleon instituted Spontaneous Demonstrations, in which the beasts marched in military formation, listening to speeches and poems in praise of the leader. In April, Animal Farm was formally proclaimed a Republic, and an election was held to choose a President. There was only one candidate, Comrade Napoleon, who was elected by a unanimous vote. Meanwhile Moses the raven suddenly reappeared, perching on the barn roof and preaching of Sugarcandy Mountain with the same easy fluency as of old. The pigs declared his stories to be absurd lies, yet they permitted him to remain without working and granted him an allowance of a gill of beer a day.

Boxer's strength was visibly failing. His coat was dull, his great flanks seemed sunken, and his hide was scarred with old wounds, yet he refused to rest, insisting that his only desire was to see the windmill well underway before he reached his twelfth year, which was the statutory age for retirement. One warm summer evening, word spread like wildfire through the yard that Boxer had fallen. Clover and Muriel galloped to the quarry, where they found the immense horse lying on his side between the shafts of a cart, blood trickling from his mouth and his neck stretched out upon the stones. He whispered feebly that his lung had given way, but that he was comforted to think they had collected stone enough to continue the work without him. Squealer soon arrived, breathless with concern, and announced that Comrade Napoleon had made arrangements for Boxer to be treated at the modern hospital in Willingdon, where the veterinary surgeon could restore him to health.

Boxer remained in his box for two days, taking medicine from a bottle found in the farmhouse pantry, while Clover and Benjamin tended him after working hours. On the third afternoon, while the other animals were weeding turnips under the supervision of a pig, Benjamin came galloping toward them across the fields, braying at the top of his voice in an agitation no one had ever witnessed before. He shouted for them to come at once, crying out that they were taking Boxer away. The beasts rushed to the yard, where a large closed van drawn by two horses stood outside the stable door. As the animals crowded around, wishing their comrade a speedy recovery, Benjamin pushed through the throng, stamping his small hoofs in fury. He demanded to know if they were blind, ordering them to read the black letters painted on the side of the vehicle: "Alfred Simmonds, Horse Slaughterer and Glue Boiler, Willingdon." A cry of horror rose from the beasts. Clover dashed forward, shouting to Boxer to kick his way out, but the drumming of hooves from within the dark interior was weak and muffled. In a few moments the drumming died away, the driver whipped up his horses, and the van rattled out through the gate, disappearing down the road forever.

Three days later Squealer convened the assembly to announce Boxer's death. He described with deep emotion how he had been present at the hospital bedside, and how the great horse had passed away in comfort, praising the glories of Animal Farm with his dying breath. Squealer then addressed the rumors concerning the knacker's van, explaining that the vehicle had formerly belonged to the horse slaughterer and had been purchased by the veterinary surgeon, who had not yet had time to paint out the old name. The explanation brought immense relief to the animals, who wept with gratitude at the thought that their beloved comrade had received such compassionate treatment. On the following Sunday, Napoleon delivered a short eulogy at the foot of the flagstaff, and a large wooden crate of whisky was delivered to the farmhouse that evening, from which the sounds of wild singing were heard until dawn.

Years passed, and the seasons followed their appointed rounds. The old days receded into a dim, legendary past until there were few animals left who remembered the rebellion. Jones was dead, having passed away in an inebriates' home in another part of the country; Snowball was forgotten; and Boxer was only a name preserved by Clover and Benjamin. The farm was larger and more prosperous, having been enlarged by two fields purchased from Mr. Pilkington, and the rebuilt windmill was finally completed. It was not used for generating electric power or providing heated stalls, however, but was employed solely for grinding corn, which brought in a handsome profit. The farm seemed richer, yet the animals themselves were no better off; their bellies were still empty, their straw was thin, and their work was unending. Only the pigs and the dogs had multiplied and prospered, their endless labor in compiling reports, memorandums, and minutes consuming the wealth of the estate without producing a single morsel of food.

In early summer, Squealer led the sheep away to a secluded birch-coppice at the far end of the property, where he kept them for an entire week, instructing them in a new piece of work. On the evening of their return, the silence of the yard was broken by a terrified, piercing neigh from Clover. The animals rushed into the yard and stopped dead in their tracks, frozen with amazement and dread. There, emerging from the farmhouse door, was Squealer, walking clumsily upon his two hind legs. A moment later a long line of pigs filed out into the open, balancing themselves with comical difficulty, followed finally by Napoleon himself, who marched majestically upright, carrying a long leather whip in his trotter. Before the horrified watchers could recover their senses or utter a cry of protest, the sheep in the pasture burst into a deafening, mechanical chant that drowned out every other sound: "Four legs good, two legs better!"

The bleating continued for five unbroken minutes, and by the time it subsided, the pigs had marched in solemn procession back into the house. Clover led Benjamin to the tarred wall of the big barn, where the white letters of the Seven Commandments had once stood. Her sight had grown dim with age, and she asked him to read what remained upon the wall. For the first time in his long life, Benjamin consented to read the masonry. There was now only a single inscription upon the tar: "All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others."

After that day, the pigs carried whips when they inspected the fields, purchased a wireless telephone, and clothed themselves in the finest garments from Mr. Jones's wardrobes. A week later, several neighboring human proprietors were invited to inspect the estate, marveling at the clean ditches, the high fences, and the disciplined labor of the beasts. That evening, sounds of boisterous laughter drifted through the lighted windows of the farmhouse dining-room. The common animals crept through the garden and pressed their muzzles against the glass to peer inside. Around the table sat half a dozen human gentlemen and half a dozen prominent pigs, with Napoleon occupying the seat of honor. Mr. Pilkington rose to deliver a gracious toast, observing with admiration that the lower animals on Animal Farm worked longer hours and received less food than any animals in the county. Napoleon replied with grave courtesy, announcing that the custom of addressing one another as "Comrade" was abolished, the marching past Old Major's skull suppressed, and the ancient title of Manor Farm restored.

As the company burst into applause and emptied their glasses, a sudden uproar broke out at the card table. Napoleon and Mr. Pilkington had simultaneously played an ace of spades, and each accused the other of violent cheating. Shouts, oaths, and blows were exchanged as chairs were overturned and glasses shattered upon the floor. Those gathered in the dark grass outside pressed their faces against the damp glass, their eyes traveling from the red, shouting visages of the men to the heavy jowls of the pigs, and from pig to man once more, yet no watcher in the garden could discover any difference between them.
