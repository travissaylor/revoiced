On the pleasant shore of the French Riviera, about halfway between Marseilles and the Italian border, stood a large, proud, rose-colored hotel. Deferential palms cooled its flushed facade, and before it stretched a short dazzling beach. In the early morning the distant image of Cannes, the pink and cream of old fortifications, the purple Alp that bounded Italy, were cast across the water and lay quavering in the ripples and rings sent up by sea-plants through the clear shallows. It was into this world, this bright arrangement of land and sea and money, that Rosemary Hoyt arrived in the summer of 1925, nearly eighteen years old, astonishingly beautiful, with the dew still on her.

She came with her mother, Mrs. Elsie Speers, a woman of considerable self-possession who had buried two husbands and emerged from both marriages with the hard-won poise of someone who understands that life is mostly a matter of endurance dressed up as adventure. She had steered her daughter through the machinery of Hollywood and deposited her on the other side with a film called Daddy's Girl and a face that people on the beach turned to watch -- a face so fresh and unmarked by experience that it seemed almost an affront to the older women who lay browning in the sun, their own beauty long since negotiated into marriages and settlements and the particular, resigned contentment of women who have traded youth for security and are not entirely sure they got the better end of the bargain.

The Riviera in those years was not yet given over to the great August crowds; it was still the province of a small, charmed circle of Americans who had discovered the summer shore and made it their own, who spread their blankets and arranged their umbrellas with the proprietary air of people who have invested something more than money in a place -- who have given it their youth and their particular, irreplaceable brand of carelessness. There were two groups on that beach, and Rosemary, with the instinct of the young for social cartography, identified them immediately. One group was loud and disorganized, spread out on the sand with the chaotic energy of people who have money but no style. The other was smaller, more contained, arranged around a pair of bright umbrellas with the deliberate grace of a painting -- and it was this group, the Diver group, that drew her with a force she did not entirely understand.

Among these were Dick and Nicole Diver, and it was toward them that Rosemary found herself gravitating with all the helpless certainty of a compass needle swinging north. Dick Diver possessed a charm that was not the common, shopworn article but something rarer and more perilous -- a grave, attentive quality that made each person in his company feel singled out, uniquely understood. He arranged people. He created the atmosphere of a party the way a composer arranges a symphony, with each guest playing an instrument he did not know he had brought. His voice carried the warmth of absolute interest, and when he looked at you, you existed more completely than you had existed before. This was his gift and, as it would prove, his undoing.

Nicole was hard and lovely and pitiful. She had the complexion of a Nordic princess and the carriage of someone accustomed to the weight of great wealth borne gracefully. She spent money with a largesse that seemed almost compulsive, as though purchasing things -- bright scarves, heavy bracelets, whole shops of merchandise -- could fill some cavity that had been hollowed out long ago by an injury too terrible to name. Together, the Divers presided over their stretch of beach like monarchs of a small, sun-drenched kingdom, and around them gathered their court: Abe North, a once-promising composer now given over entirely to drink, and his long-suffering wife Mary; Tommy Barban, a half-French, half-American mercenary soldier, brown and hard and restless, with the contained violence of a man who has killed and found it neither pleasant nor unpleasant but merely a thing that sometimes must be done.

On the beach, Dick performed small miracles of social engineering. He raked the sand smooth before his guests arrived. He mixed drinks with the precision of a chemist and the flair of a showman. He drew out the shy and restrained the tedious, and he did all of this with such apparent effortlessness that it was easy to miss the enormous expenditure of energy it required -- the way it is easy to watch a swan glide across a lake without considering the furious paddling beneath the surface. There was Luis Campion, a homosexual of indeterminate origin who fluttered at the edges of the group; there were the McKiscos, Albert striving and literary and Violet loud and indiscreet; and there was the dark, watchful presence of Tommy Barban, who seemed to exist in a state of permanent readiness, as though the world might at any moment require him to fight something or someone, and he intended to be prepared.

Rosemary fell in love with Dick Diver with the swiftness and totality that only the very young can manage, when love has not yet taught them the defensive crouch, the strategic retreat. She watched him at dinner parties where his magnetism drew people to him like filings to a lodestone; she watched him orchestrate evenings of improbable beauty on the terrace above the darkened sea; and she decided, with the bright conviction of seventeen, that he was the most wonderful person she had ever known. She told her mother this, and Mrs. Speers, who was wise enough to know that some lessons must be suffered rather than taught, gave her tacit permission to proceed into heartbreak.

At a party at the Divers' villa one evening, something happened that cast the first shadow across the brilliant surface of their world. Violet McKisco, wife of the aspiring novelist Albert McKisco, stumbled upon a scene in the bathroom that left her shaken and voluble -- some private spectacle involving Nicole that hinted at the dark machinery grinding beneath the Divers' polished exterior. Tommy Barban, with the chivalric ferocity that was his particular mode of devotion to Nicole, challenged McKisco to a duel to prevent the gossip from spreading. They met on a misty morning with pistols, and both men missed, and the matter was considered settled -- though of course nothing was settled at all.

Dick took Rosemary to the studio where her film was being shown, and he watched Daddy's Girl with a complicated expression -- this saccharine confection about a young girl's devotion to her father, played by the same beautiful creature who was now directing that devotion, or something like it, toward him. The irony of this -- a girl who had made her name playing a daddy's girl now falling for a man old enough and paternal enough to fill that role -- was not lost on him, though he did not yet understand how deeply the pattern would come to define his life.

The Divers threw a party at their villa, and it was one of those evenings that Dick created with the skill of an artist working in the medium of human interaction. Chinese lanterns hung in the garden; the night was soft with the scent of jasmine and the distant sound of the sea; and the guests moved through the rooms and across the terrace with the pleasurable sensation of being part of something beautiful, something that would be remembered. Dick made everyone feel that they were the most interesting person in the room, and the remarkable thing was that in the moment of his attention, they were. He had that gift. He spent it like a millionaire.

The group traveled to Paris, and it was in Paris that the gorgeous, careless world of the Divers began to show its seams. Abe North was drinking himself into a stupor with the methodical determination of a man who has given up on his talent and decided to make failure his life's work instead. At the Gare Saint-Lazare, amid the steam and bustle of departure, a woman named Maria Wallis drew a pistol and shot a man on the platform -- one of those sudden eruptions of violence that seemed, in the world the Divers inhabited, to lie always just beneath the surface of things, like a body of water beneath thin ice. Dick helped manage the chaos with the efficient calm of a man accustomed to managing other people's disasters, and Rosemary watched him and loved him more.

In the corridors and darkened taxi-cabs of Paris, Dick and Rosemary exchanged stolen kisses and murmured declarations -- he drawn to her youth and freshness as to a mirror that still showed him the man he had been, she intoxicated by his sophistication and his sadness, which she was too young to recognize as the most dangerous thing about him. But he held back. There was a line he would not yet cross, though the reasons were as tangled as the roots beneath a great tree -- professional ethics, marital loyalty, and something else, some instinct for self-preservation that was already, even then, beginning to erode.

Then the dead man appeared. Jules Peterson, a Negro who had become entangled in one of Abe North's drunken misadventures, was found murdered in Rosemary's hotel room -- his body sprawled across the bright coverlet like a dark accusation. It was a scandal that could have destroyed Rosemary's career, and Dick moved the body with the grim efficiency of a man who had chosen, irrevocably, to use his considerable gifts in the service of managing catastrophe rather than building anything of his own. Nicole, confronted with the blood and the ugliness, had one of her episodes -- a cracking of that lovely, composed surface that revealed the abyss beneath. And Rosemary, watching Nicole come apart in the bathroom, understood at last what Violet McKisco had seen, and the knowledge changed everything and nothing.

To understand what had happened to Nicole, and therefore to Dick, and therefore to all of them, it is necessary to go back to a day in 1917, in Zurich, when the world was at war and a young American psychiatrist named Richard Diver was completing his studies with the most brilliant promise his professors had seen in a generation. He was twenty-six years old, a product of Yale and then of Johns Hopkins, the son of a clergyman from Buffalo who had given him, in lieu of money, an education in the old American virtues -- courtesy, courage, and an almost religious belief in the possibility of being good. Dick had come to Zurich to study with the great minds of European psychiatry, and he had written a slender, remarkable treatise on psychiatric classification that his colleagues recognized as the work of a man who might, if he was careful, if he was lucky, reshape the field.

It was in Zurich that he met Franz Gregorovius, a Swiss psychopathologist with whom he would later open a clinic, and it was through the machinery of that clinic that he first encountered Nicole Warren. She was sixteen years old, the daughter of a fabulously wealthy Chicago family, and she had been brought to Doctor Dohmler's sanatorium because she was broken in a way that no amount of Warren money could fix. Her father, Devereux Warren, had committed an act of such monstrous betrayal -- he had, in the stark clinical language that doctors use to contain the uncontainable, committed incest with his own daughter -- that Nicole's mind had fractured under the weight of it. She suffered from what they called schizophrenia, though the word seems too clean, too medical, for the particular devastation that had been visited upon her.

Nicole began writing letters to Dick -- letters of startling lucidity and terrible need -- and he, reading them, felt something shift inside him that was not merely professional sympathy. He was warned. Franz warned him; Doctor Dohmler warned him; the entire apparatus of professional ethics warned him that to become emotionally involved with this patient was to step off a cliff. But Dick Diver, for all his brilliance, suffered from a flaw that one of his university friends had identified years earlier: he had no judgment about himself. He could diagnose others with the precision of a surgeon, could see into the tangled architecture of other people's psyches and find the load-bearing wall, but when it came to his own motives, his own needs, he was as blind as any patient on any couch.

There was a period of correspondence -- Nicole's letters arriving with the regularity of a heartbeat, each one a little stronger, a little more assured, as though the mere act of writing to him was itself therapeutic. And then there was a period of deliberation, during which Dick consulted his conscience and his ambition and found them, as they so often are in young men of talent, hopelessly entangled. He told himself he was helping her. He told himself that his feelings were under professional control. He told himself many things, and some of them were even true, but the essential truth -- that he was falling in love with a patient whose wealth and beauty and desperate need for him constituted a trap as elegant as any that had ever been devised -- this truth he kept at arm's length, the way a man keeps a letter he does not want to read.

He married Nicole. He married her wealth and her beauty and her damage, and for a time it seemed as though the marriage might be the making of both of them -- she gaining in stability what he provided in steadiness, he gaining in scope what her fortune provided in freedom. They traveled. They had two children, Lanier and Topsy. They acquired the villa on the Riviera and the particular, envied life that went with it. But the transference -- that Freudian mechanism by which a patient redirects feelings for a parent onto the therapist -- had never been resolved. Dick was Nicole's husband and her doctor and, in some subterranean way that neither of them could articulate, a replacement for the father who had destroyed her. He could not be all three. No man could. And the strain of trying began to pull him apart, as slowly and surely as water pulls apart stone.

Nicole's sister, Baby Warren, regarded Dick with the cold appraisal of a woman who understood money's power over human arrangements. She had essentially purchased Dick for Nicole -- or so she saw it -- the way one might purchase a particularly well-recommended piece of medical equipment. That Dick had his own ambitions, his own interior life, his own trajectory that had nothing to do with the Warren fortune, did not interest her. She wanted a keeper for her sister, and Dick, with his psychiatric credentials and his good manners and his willingness to be absorbed, fit the specifications admirably.

They invested in a clinic on the Zurichsee with Franz, and for a while this venture provided Dick with the illusion that he was still building something, still moving forward along the professional path he had mapped out in those brilliant early years. But the money was Nicole's -- it was always Nicole's -- and the knowledge of this sat in Dick's chest like a stone. His work, his clinic, his reputation -- all of it rested on a foundation of Warren capital, and a man cannot maintain his self-respect indefinitely when the ground beneath him belongs to someone else.

Then came the accusation. A woman wrote to the clinic claiming that Dick had behaved improperly with her daughter, a former patient. The charge was false, but it detonated something in Nicole. In a fit of rage and paranoia, she seized the steering wheel of the car while Dick was driving with their children in the back seat and sent them careening off the road. It was the kind of moment that strips away all pretense and reveals the essential architecture of a situation -- and what it revealed was that Dick had built his life on a fault line, and the ground was giving way.

Nicole's episodes came and went like weather -- sudden storms that blew through the household and left everyone shaken, followed by periods of calm so complete that it was possible to believe, briefly, that the storms had been imagined. She would be fine for weeks, for months, moving through their social world with the serene confidence of a woman who has everything, and then something would trigger it -- a word, a memory, the wrong inflection in someone's voice -- and the surface would crack and the darkness would come pouring through. Dick managed these episodes with the dual competence of husband and physician, holding her through the worst of it, adjusting her treatment, explaining away her behavior to friends and acquaintances with the smooth fabrications that had become second nature. He was magnificent in crisis. It was in the long, grinding stretches between crises that he began to come apart.

His father died. The Reverend Diver, that repository of the old virtues, that last connection to the world of Buffalo and clerical humility and the simple American decencies, was gone. Dick sailed to America for the funeral and felt, standing in the graveyard, that something had been severed -- not merely a family tie but a connection to the person he had once been, the promising young man who had gone to Zurich with his treatise and his good intentions and his fatal inability to judge himself. America was no longer home. He had been away too long. He had become a European, or rather he had become something that was neither European nor American but merely expatriate, which is to say rootless, which is to say lost.

He returned to Europe and found Rosemary again -- in Rome now, no longer the dewy girl of the Riviera but a successful actress, sophisticated, assured, still carrying a torch for him that had not dimmed but had changed in character, as torches do, from the bright flare of first love to something steadier and more knowing. They consummated the affair that had been suspended all those years, and if Dick had hoped that this would restore something -- his youth, his sense of possibility, the feeling of being desired for himself rather than for the function he performed -- he was disappointed. The act of possession revealed only how much had already been lost. Rosemary told him she would always love him, and the words, which would once have been a lifeline, fell through him like coins through water.

There was a moment in Rome -- one of those moments that seem, in retrospect, to have been the hinge on which everything turned -- when Dick sat in a cafe and understood, with a clarity that was almost physical, that his life had taken a shape he had not chosen and could not alter. He was forty years old. His book remained unfinished. His clinic had been purchased with another family's money. His wife was his patient. His mistress was a movie star who loved the idea of him more than the reality. And he was sitting in a Roman cafe, slightly drunk at three in the afternoon, watching the pigeons strut across the piazza with more purpose and direction than he himself possessed.

What followed was a descent that had the quality of inevitability, as though Dick were acting out a script that had been written long ago. He drank. He drank with a thoroughness that went beyond mere indulgence into something that resembled a project -- the systematic dismantling of a personality. In Rome, drunk and belligerent, he fought with Italian taxi drivers and was arrested and beaten by the police. Collis Clay, a young American acquaintance, witnessed the degradation, and the knowledge that his collapse was now a matter of public record only accelerated it, the way exposure to air accelerates the decay of something that has been preserved too long.

Franz, who had watched this deterioration with the particular anguish of a man who remembers what his partner once was, finally asked Dick to leave the clinic. It was done with professional tact, as such things are done among the educated classes, but the substance of it was brutal: Dick was no longer fit to practice. The life's work, the brilliant early promise, the treatise that was going to reshape psychiatry -- all of it had been consumed by the machinery of the Divers' marriage, by alcohol, by the corrosive effect of living on someone else's money, by the impossible task of being simultaneously a husband and a doctor to the same woman.

Back on the Riviera, the Divers resumed something that resembled their old life but was in fact its photographic negative -- the same shapes and arrangements but with all the light and dark reversed. They attended the same parties, visited the same beaches, moved through the same social world, but now it was Nicole who was vibrant and Dick who was fading. The people who had once gravitated toward him began to drift away, sensing, with the cruel social instinct of the leisure class, that the source of his magnetism had gone dry. He had begun to say the wrong things at parties, to drink too much and too visibly, to pick fights with people who had done nothing to deserve his anger except be present during his collapse. He insulted the Duchess of something-or-other at a gathering and was not invited back. He made a scene at a restaurant. He behaved, in short, like a man who no longer cared about the social contract that had been the foundation of his entire adult life, which was perhaps the truest sign that the man himself was gone.

It was here that the final unraveling occurred with a kind of terrible symmetry. Dick tried to perform a stunt on the aquaplane -- an acrobatic feat that he had once executed with easy grace -- and failed, repeatedly, publicly, while the people on the beach watched. It was a small thing, an afternoon's embarrassment, but it carried the weight of metaphor: the man who had once arranged everything, who had made the world graceful and beautiful for others, could no longer manage even this simple physical act. His body had betrayed him as completely as his judgment had betrayed him years before.

Nicole, meanwhile, was blooming. Her affair with Tommy Barban had given her something that Dick's careful, therapeutic love never could -- a passion that asked nothing of her except that she be beautiful and desired, that did not monitor her moods or calculate her stability or treat every interaction as a potential clinical event. Tommy wanted her simply, directly, with the uncomplicated appetite of a man of action, and this simplicity, after years of being managed and analyzed, was itself a cure. She was growing stronger as Dick grew weaker, as though there were only a fixed quantity of vitality between them and she was reclaiming her share.

Tommy Barban, who had loved Nicole for years with the patient, undemonstrative devotion of a soldier who knows how to wait, now pressed his suit openly. He was everything Dick was not -- direct where Dick was subtle, physical where Dick was cerebral, unencumbered where Dick was weighted down by the accumulated freight of a decade's emotional labor. Tommy did not analyze Nicole. He did not monitor her for signs of relapse. He did not measure his words or calibrate his touch to accommodate her fragility. He simply wanted her, and the force of that uncomplicated wanting was, for Nicole, like stepping out of a darkened room into sunlight.

She asked for a divorce. She asked for it clearly and without guilt, and Dick, who had given her everything -- his career, his ambition, his best years, the particular and irreplaceable gift of his attention -- agreed with a readiness that was either grace or exhaustion, or perhaps both, for in the end the two become indistinguishable. He understood, with the diagnostic clarity that had always been his greatest professional asset, that Nicole was cured -- that her affair with Tommy, her assertion of independence, her willingness to leave him, were themselves the evidence of her recovery. The transference was resolved. She no longer needed a father or a doctor. She needed only a husband, and that, it turned out, was the one thing Dick had never quite been.

Dick made the sign of the cross over the beach before he left -- a small, private benediction that no one saw or, if they saw, did not understand. It was the gesture of a priest consecrating ground he will never visit again, and it contained within it all the tenderness and all the loss that the word goodbye is too small to hold.

Nicole married Tommy Barban. She married his strength and his simplicity and his willingness to take the world as he found it rather than trying to arrange it into something more beautiful and more fragile. And Dick -- Dick went back to America, to the country that was no longer home, and began a slow, quiet disappearance that was as complete in its way as death. He practiced medicine in a series of small towns in upstate New York -- Batavia, then Lockport, then Geneva, then Hornell -- each one smaller and more obscure than the last, as though he were trying to reduce himself to a point of invisibility, to become so small that the weight of his failure could no longer find him.

Nicole heard about him from time to time, in the way that one hears about people who have passed out of one's life but not yet out of the world. He had attempted to practice in Buffalo, but some trouble, the nature of which was not specified, had driven him away. He was involved in a lawsuit about a girl, though the details were vague and might have been exaggerated. He moved again. He was living in a small town, somewhere in the heart of the continent, and he was, as far as anyone could tell, alone.

The story of Dick Diver is the story of a man who spent himself -- not in the grand, dramatic manner of a man who burns out but in the slow, aching manner of a man who is used up. He had taken Nicole's illness into himself, had absorbed her damage the way a sponge absorbs water, and when she was wrung out and clean, there was nothing left of him but the stain. His charm, which had been a real and valuable thing, had been deployed too often in the service of others and never in the service of himself. His brilliance, which had been genuine, had been diverted from its proper channel -- the work, the writing, the contribution to human knowledge that he had once seemed destined to make -- and spent instead on the management of a wealthy woman's neurosis and the maintenance of a social world that glittered but did not endure.

There is a particular cruelty in the destruction of a man who was built for something fine. The world is full of mediocre people who come to mediocre ends, and there is no tragedy in that -- only the dull, expected disappointment of potential that was never really there. But Dick Diver had the equipment. He had the mind and the training and the instinct for greatness, and what destroyed him was not a lack of ability but an excess of sympathy, a willingness to pour himself out for others that left nothing in the vessel for himself. He was, in the end, too good for his own good -- a man undone not by his vices but by his virtues, which amounts to the same thing when the virtues are not tempered by the cold, necessary selfishness that survival requires.

The Riviera went on without him. The beach filled with new people who did not know his name, who spread their blankets where he had once arranged his elegant, ephemeral world. Nicole was happy with Tommy, or at least she was well, which is not the same thing but is, perhaps, enough. Rosemary continued making films, continued being beautiful, continued living in that bright, forward-moving way that belongs to people who have not yet been broken by the thing they love. And Abe North was dead -- killed in a speakeasy, beaten to death in the kind of sordid, pointless violence that was the final destination of his long, committed journey into ruin.

The world that Dick Diver had created -- that exquisite, temporary world of dinner parties on the terrace and afternoons on the bright beach and evenings when the conversation rose and fell like music -- was gone as completely as if it had never existed. And perhaps that was the point. Perhaps the beauty of it had always been inseparable from its transience, the way the beauty of youth is inseparable from its brevity, the way the beauty of a summer night is inseparable from the knowledge that it will end. Dick had known this. He had always known it. He had built his world on sand, and the tide had come in, and he had stood there watching it dissolve with the resigned, diagnostic gaze of a man who has always understood the prognosis but could not bring himself to prescribe the cure.

In the last accounting, what remained was a man in a small town in the middle of America, far from the bright water and the brilliant company and the life that had once seemed so full of promise that it could only have been borrowed from someone else's dream. He was there, in that small town, and he was practicing a medicine that was no longer informed by brilliance but merely by competence, and he was alone, and the afternoon light fell through his office window with the same indifferent clarity that it falls everywhere, on the lucky and the ruined alike, and he did not write, and he did not publish, and the great work that he had once planned -- the work that was going to be his contribution, his justification, the thing that would outlast the parties and the charm and the terrible, beautiful waste of it all -- remained unwritten, the way so many great works remain unwritten, not because the author lacked the talent but because he lacked, in the end, the selfishness to save himself.
