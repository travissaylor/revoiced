In the end, even the crows must eat.

The War of the Five Kings had burned its way across Westeros like wildfire through a summer wood, and when the last ember had guttered out, it left behind a realm of ash and bone and shattered men. Five kings had raised their banners, and four were dead or broken. Only young Tommen Baratheon sat the Iron Throne now, a boy of eight with golden curls and a sweet trusting nature, and if the throne was his by right of blood and conquest both, the power behind it belonged to his mother. Cersei Lannister meant to rule.

The book opens not in King's Landing, however, but in Oldtown, that ancient seat of learning where the great stone tower called the Hightower stands sentinel above the mouth of the Honeywine. Here a novice named Pate sweated through his nights at the Citadel, dreaming of a girl called Rosey whose maidenhead could be purchased for the price of a single gold dragon. Five years Pate had studied at the Citadel without managing to forge even one link for his maester's chain, and his desperation made him easy prey. A man who called himself the Alchemist came to him in the Quill and Tankard and offered him that golden coin in exchange for a key stolen from old Archmaester Walgrave, a key that opened every door in the Citadel. Pate made the trade and bit the coin, and the taste of gold was still on his lips when he fell dead upon the cobblestones. The Alchemist took the key and left Pate where he lay, and those who knew the shape of a Faceless Man's work might have recognized the hand of Jaqen H'ghar in the killing, though the face he wore was not one any man in Oldtown had seen before.

In King's Landing, the great sept blazed with a thousand candles for the vigil over Lord Tywin Lannister, whose corpse lay in state with painted stones upon his eyes and a stench rising from his bowels that no amount of embalming spice could mask. The mighty Lord of Casterly Rock, Hand of the King, the man who had truly ruled the Seven Kingdoms, was dead at the hand of his own dwarf son, and the smell of him filled the sept like a last jest. Cersei stood her vigil with a fury burning inside her that had nothing to do with grief. Her father was gone, and her monstrous little brother Tyrion had escaped his cell and vanished into the bowels of the earth, and all the power her father had wielded had fallen into her lap like a bloody gift.

She meant to wield it well, or so she told herself. She was the lioness of the Rock, Tywin Lannister's true heir, not Jaime with his white cloak and his lost hand, and certainly not Tyrion, who was no true Lannister at all. She would show them. She would show them all.

What followed was a reign of staggering folly dressed in the silks of cunning. Cersei Lannister had spent her life watching men rule and believing she could do better, and now she would prove it. Her uncle Kevan, a capable and seasoned man who had served her father faithfully for decades, offered to serve as her Hand, but only on the condition that she return to Casterly Rock and leave the governance to him. Cersei refused him with a cold fury. She would not be sent away like some broodmare. Instead she filled her small council with creatures of her own choosing: Harys Swyft, a weak and pliable man, as her Hand; Gyles Rosby, who could scarcely draw breath without coughing, as master of coin; Aurane Waters, a handsome young bastard of Driftmark whose resemblance to her dead lover Rhaegar Targaryen pleased her eye, as grand admiral; and Qyburn, a disgraced former maester stripped of his chain for performing unspeakable experiments upon the living, as her master of whisperers. It was a council of sycophants and incompetents, and Cersei was too blind to see it. She commissioned a new fleet of war dromonds and gave Aurane Waters the gold to build them, never suspecting that the pretty bastard's loyalty was as thin as summer ice. She defaulted on the crown's debts to the Iron Bank of Braavos, a decision that would have made her father choke on his wine, for the Iron Bank was not an institution that forgave or forgot. When you owed the Iron Bank, you paid; when you did not pay, the Iron Bank found someone who would, and gave them the gold to take your throne. But Cersei did not think about such things. She was too busy watching the roses grow.

The Tyrells were her obsession. They had come to King's Landing like a creeping vine, wrapping their tendrils around everything Cersei held dear. Margaery Tyrell, that smiling little rose, was betrothed to Tommen, and Cersei saw in her the fulfillment of a prophecy that had haunted her since childhood. When Cersei was a girl of ten, she had sought out a woods witch called Maggy the Frog and demanded to know her future. The old woman had tasted her blood and spoken words that had curdled in Cersei's belly ever since: she would wed the king, not the prince; she would be queen, until there came another, younger and more beautiful, to cast her down and take all she held dear; she would have three children, and gold would be their crowns and gold their shrouds; and when her tears had drowned her, the valonqar -- the little brother -- would wrap his hands about her pale white throat and choke the life from her. Cersei had lived her whole life in the shadow of that prophecy. She had believed the younger queen to be Sansa Stark, then Margaery Tyrell, and the valonqar to be Tyrion, always Tyrion, the twisted little monster who had murdered her firstborn son and now her father.

Her fear of the prophecy drove her every decision, though she would never have admitted it. She armed the Faith of the Seven, permitting the new High Septon -- a barefoot, humble man the smallfolk called the High Sparrow -- to reconstitute the Faith Militant, those holy warriors who had been disbanded centuries ago by the Targaryens. Cersei thought she was being clever. The crown owed the Faith a staggering sum, and by granting the High Sparrow his soldiers, she could defer that debt and gain a weapon to use against the Tyrells in one stroke. She did not see, could not see, that she was arming a power that answered to no lord and no queen, a power that would judge her as readily as it would judge any other sinner.

She also drank. Wine had always been Cersei's companion, but now it became her counselor, and the counsel it gave was dark and reckless. She took lovers -- Osney Kettleblack, a sword-for-hire with more muscle than sense, and others -- and convinced herself that her appetites were no different from any king's. Robert had whored his way across the Seven Kingdoms, hadn't he? A queen could do as she pleased behind closed doors.

Meanwhile she schemed against Margaery with a clumsiness that would have made her father weep. She used the Kettleblack brothers as her instruments, dispatching Osney to seduce the young queen and then confess the affair to the High Sparrow, providing false evidence of Margaery's infidelity. She suborned witnesses and hatched a plot so tangled in its own threads that it was bound to unravel. And unravel it did. When Cersei brought her accusations before the High Sparrow, he listened gravely, and then he listened to other whispers too -- whispers about the queen regent herself, about her adultery with her cousin Lancel, with Osney Kettleblack, with others. The Faith arrested Margaery, yes, but they arrested Cersei too, and locked her in a tower cell in the Great Sept of Baelor, stripped of her finery and her power and her pride, while the sparrows stood guard outside her door with their swords and their stars and their terrible certainty.

The irony was perfect. Cersei had restored the Faith Militant to destroy her rival, and it had destroyed her instead. The woman who believed herself her father's true heir, more capable than Jaime, more ruthless than Tyrion, had proven herself the architect of her own ruin. She had alienated every ally, squandered every advantage, and armed her own executioner. And in the black cells beneath the sept, Qyburn labored at his dark work, stitching together something from the massive body of Ser Gregor Clegane, the Mountain That Rides, who had died -- or had he? -- from the poisoned spear of Prince Oberyn. Whatever Qyburn was building in those cells, it was large, and it was silent, and it would serve when the time came. When she sent a desperate raven to Jaime begging him to champion her in a trial by combat, he read the letter and fed it to the flames. The woman who had been the center of his world for as long as he could remember was calling to him, and he turned away.

Jaime Lannister had changed, though the change was slow and strange, like a sword being reforged in a fire he did not fully understand. The loss of his hand had taken more than his skill with a blade; it had taken his certainty about who he was and what he was worth. He was no longer the greatest swordsman in the Seven Kingdoms, and if he could not be that, what was he? The question gnawed at him as he rode through the Riverlands to settle the siege of Riverrun, the last bastion of Tully resistance.

The Riverlands were a wasteland. Every village Jaime passed bore the scars of war: burned holdfasts, hanged men swinging from the trees like grotesque fruit, fields gone to seed and ruin. Wolves roamed in packs so large that even armed parties gave them wide berth, and the smallfolk who survived did so by hiding in the brush like rabbits. This was the kingdom Jaime's family had fought to hold, and the sight of it stirred something in him that felt uncomfortably like shame.

At Riverrun, he found a siege in disarray under the command of incompetent Freys, and he took charge with the cold efficiency his father would have admired. The Blackfish, Brynden Tully, held the castle and refused to yield. Jaime's task was to take it without bloodshed, for every man who died here was a man who could not serve the crown, and the crown had precious few men left. He summoned Edmure Tully from his cell -- Edmure, the rightful Lord of Riverrun, prisoner since the Red Wedding -- and made him an offer wrapped in velvet over steel. Yield the castle, and Edmure and his wife and their unborn child would live. Refuse, and Jaime would put the child in a trebuchet and launch it over the walls. He said the words with a face as cold as his golden hand, and Edmure believed him, though whether Jaime truly would have done it was a question even Jaime could not answer.

Edmure entered Riverrun under a peace banner and ordered the garrison to open the gates. The Blackfish escaped in the night, swimming the river under cover of darkness, and Jaime let him go without much effort to pursue. The castle was taken without a drop of blood spilled, and Jaime felt something that was not quite pride and not quite disgust. He was becoming his father, or perhaps he was becoming something else entirely. In the quiet of the night, he dreamed of Brienne of Tarth and her earnest eyes and her damnable honor, and when Cersei's letter came begging for his sword, he burned it and turned his face away from King's Landing.

Brienne's quest through the Riverlands was a journey through the aftermath of a war that had chewed up the common people and spat out their bones. Jaime had given her a Valyrian steel sword -- Oathkeeper, he called it, reforged from the melted blade of Eddard Stark's great sword Ice -- and sent her to find Sansa Stark and keep her safe. It was an impossible task, for Sansa had vanished as thoroughly as morning mist, but Brienne pursued it with the stubborn devotion that defined her.

She traveled with young Podrick Payne as her squire, a boy so earnest and tongue-tied that he could scarce string two words together, and later Ser Hyle Hunt joined their party, a hedge knight with an easy manner and a sardonic tongue who had once humiliated Brienne at Renly's camp in a cruel wager over her maidenhead. The Riverlands showed Brienne its worst face: burned septs and broken men, orphans begging by the road, silent women with hollow eyes. She met the Elder Brother on the Quiet Isle, a place of peace amid the devastation, and learned that the Hound -- Sandor Clegane -- was dead, or so the Elder Brother said, though he spoke of it in terms that suggested a more complicated truth. The man was dead, perhaps, but whether the dog had been buried with him was another matter. Brienne heard rumors of Sansa at every turn, each one false, each one leading her deeper into the broken heart of the kingdom. At the crossroads inn she fought a band of outlaws led by a brute who wore the Hound's helm -- Rorge, one of the criminals from the black cells, not Sandor Clegane at all -- and she killed him, but his companion Biter savaged her terribly, gnawing at her face and her flesh until the timely arrival of Gendry and the Brotherhood Without Banners saved her life.

But the Brotherhood was not what it had been. Beric Dondarrion was dead, truly dead at last, having given the kiss of life one final time -- not to heal himself, but to resurrect another. The Brotherhood now followed a hooded woman they called Lady Stoneheart, and when they brought Brienne before her and pulled back the cowl, Brienne saw a face that had once been Catelyn Stark's. The river had bloated it and the knife had opened it from ear to ear, and the thing that looked out from those dead eyes was not Catelyn but something harder and colder, a creature of pure vengeance with no mercy left in her waterlogged heart. Lady Stoneheart accused Brienne of serving the Lannisters, pointed to the Lannister sword at her hip, and offered her a choice: take that sword and kill Jaime Lannister, or hang. Brienne refused, and the rope tightened around her neck, and Podrick's neck too, and as the darkness closed in Brienne screamed a word -- one word that the text leaves unrevealed -- and the hanging stopped.

Far from the burned villages and broken castles of the Riverlands, in the high mountain fastness of the Eyrie, Sansa Stark lived a lie. She was Alayne Stone now, the bastard daughter of Lord Petyr Baelish, and she wore the lie like a second skin. Littlefinger had spirited her out of King's Landing after the murder of Joffrey, and brought her to the Vale, and murdered her aunt Lysa Arryn by shoving her through the Moon Door when Lysa's jealousy threatened to expose them both. He had pinned the crime on the singer Marillion, who confessed under duress and died in a sky cell, and now Littlefinger ruled the Vale as Lord Protector in the name of Lysa's sickly son, young Robert Arryn.

The descent from the Eyrie was treacherous, down the narrow mountain path called the Giant's Lance, and Sansa managed it with young Robert clinging to her, the boy weeping and shaking as the mule picked its way along ledges with nothing but sky below. They came down to the Gates of the Moon, the castle at the mountain's base, and there Sansa settled into the rhythms of her new life, presiding over the household as Littlefinger's bastard daughter, learning to manage servants and balance accounts and smile at men she despised. Robert was a frail creature, small for his age and given to shaking fits that the maesters called the falling sickness. He crept into Sansa's bed at night and asked if she was his mother now, and Sansa told him yes, because the lie was kinder than the truth. Littlefinger was patient and careful and ruthless in ways that Cersei could only dream of being. He played the lords of the Vale against each other with the subtlety of a master harpist, and he revealed to Sansa that he intended to betroth her to Harrold Hardyng, a young knight who stood next in line to inherit the Vale should Robert die. When Robert died -- and Littlefinger's tone made it clear this was a matter of when, not if -- Sansa would reveal her true identity, claim Winterfell, and become the most powerful woman in the North. It was a pretty plan, elegant in its patience, and Sansa was learning, slowly and painfully, to play the game of thrones that had killed her father and destroyed her family.

Across the narrow sea, in the fog-shrouded canals of Braavos, another Stark was learning a different kind of lesson. Arya had crossed the sea with nothing but a coin and a name -- valar morghulis -- and found her way to the House of Black and White, that strange temple on its isle of stone where the Faceless Men served the Many-Faced God, the god of death in all his thousand aspects. The kindly man who tended the temple told her she must become no one. She must surrender her name, her memories, her very self, and become an empty vessel that the god could fill. Arya tried. She gave up her clothes and her possessions, though she could not bring herself to throw Needle into the canal -- she hid the slender sword in a crack in the stones of the sea steps, wrapped in her old clothing, because Needle was Jon Snow's smile and Winterfell's grey walls and everything she had been before the world broke apart.

They sent her out into the city as Cat of the Canals, a girl who sold cockles and clams and mussels from a barrow by the harbor. She learned the Braavosi tongue and brought back three new things each day, as the kindly man required. She learned the city's secrets, its currents and eddies, the way the tide of money flowed through the counting houses and the insurance men and the courtesan barges that glided along the canals. But Arya could not truly become no one. The list of names she whispered each night before sleep -- the names of those she meant to kill -- was proof enough of that. When she encountered Dareon, a brother of the Night's Watch who had deserted his vows to sing in a Braavosi tavern, she cut his throat and left him in a canal. The Faceless Men did not miss the killing. When Arya woke the next morning, she was blind, her eyes turned to milk, and whether this was punishment or the next stage of her training, only the kindly man knew for certain.

Euron Greyjoy's ironborn, meanwhile, were already raiding the western coast of Westeros, seizing the Shield Islands that guarded the mouth of the Mander and threatening Oldtown itself. The Reach, the most fertile and populous kingdom in Westeros, suddenly found its flank exposed and its navy outmatched. This was another gift of Cersei's incompetence -- Aurane Waters had fled with the new-built dromonds that might have met the ironborn at sea, absconding into piracy the moment word of Cersei's arrest reached the harbor. The crown had no fleet, and the ironborn smelled blood.

On the Iron Islands, the death of King Balon Greyjoy had set the ironborn to squabbling over the Seastone Chair. Balon had fallen from a bridge at Pyke during a storm -- or been pushed, as many whispered, by a faceless man hired by his own brother. Balon's daughter Asha was fierce and capable, a warrior and a captain who had taken Deepwood Motte and held it, and she meant to claim the throne. But the ironborn were not a people accustomed to queens, and custom demanded a kingsmoot, a great assembly on the holy isle of Old Wyk where every captain could put forth his claim.

It was Aeron Greyjoy, the Damphair, priest of the Drowned God, who called the kingsmoot. Aeron was a hard man, lean and salt-weathered, with seaweed in his hair and scripture on his lips, and he had hoped to see his brother Victarion crowned -- Victarion, the Lord Captain of the Iron Fleet, a blunt and brutal man but honest in his way, a warrior who could lead the ironborn to glory in battle. But when the captains gathered on Old Wyk and each claimant rose to speak, it was neither Asha nor Victarion who carried the day.

Euron Greyjoy had returned. Euron Crow's Eye, the most feared of all the Greyjoy brothers, a man who had been banished from the Iron Islands by Balon himself, a man who claimed to have sailed the Smoking Sea and walked the ruins of Valyria. He came to the kingsmoot with a horn banded in red gold and Valyrian steel, a dragon horn he called Dragonbinder, and his men blew it with a blast that seemed to shake the very stones of Nagga's Hill. The sound was like a scream from the depths of some black hell, and the man who blew it died with blisters on his lips and his lungs cooked inside his chest, but the horn's promise was worth any price: with it, Euron claimed, he could bind dragons to his will. He promised the ironborn the world. Not the bleak shores and stony fields of the North that Balon had wasted lives to capture, but Westeros entire, taken with fire and blood, with dragons to serve the Kraken's will.

The ironborn chose Euron. Of course they did. They were reavers and raiders, and he offered them the greatest reaving in the history of the world. Asha fled the kingsmoot, knowing her life was forfeit if she stayed. Victarion bent the knee, though bitterness curdled in his gut like sour ale. Euron sent him east across the seas with the Iron Fleet, bearing the dragon horn to Slaver's Bay, there to find Daenerys Targaryen and bring her back as Euron's bride. Victarion went, though he harbored plans of his own. The dragon queen and her dragons might serve a better master than the Crow's Eye, and Victarion was not a subtle man, but he was not so dull as his brother believed.

In the far south, in Dorne, where the sun hammered the red sands and the blood oranges ripened in the Water Gardens, a different kind of fury simmered. Prince Oberyn Martell was dead, his skull crushed by the Mountain in a trial by combat at King's Landing, and his bastard daughters -- the Sand Snakes, the smallfolk called them -- howled for vengeance. Obara, the eldest, wanted war. Nymeria wanted assassinations. Tyene, sweet-faced and deadly, wanted poison. They came to Prince Doran in his chair, the gout-ridden prince who ruled Dorne from his Water Gardens, and demanded blood.

Doran gave them chains instead. His paramour Ellaria Sand, who had watched Oberyn die before her eyes, pleaded for peace. "Oberyn wanted vengeance for Elia," she said. "Now the three of you want vengeance for him. I have four daughters. I will not have them take vengeance for you." But the Sand Snakes were their father's children, and they would not be denied.

So Doran had them locked away, the three of them, and sat in his water gardens watching the children play and the blood oranges fall from the trees and split on the ground below, and the Dornish whispered that their prince was craven. They thought him weak, this old man in his wheeled chair, watching children play in the pools while his brother's blood dried in the dust of King's Landing. His own daughter Arianne thought him weak, and she hatched a plot to crown Tommen's sister Myrcella as queen of the Seven Kingdoms -- for by Dornish law, the eldest child inherits regardless of sex, and Myrcella was older than Tommen. If Myrcella were crowned, war would follow, and Dorne would have its vengeance.

Arianne gathered her conspirators: Ser Arys Oakheart of the Kingsguard, who had fallen hopelessly in love with her; Darkstar, Ser Gerold Dayne, a dangerous man with cold eyes and a hunger for glory; and others. They meant to steal Myrcella from the Water Gardens and crown her in the desert. But Doran's captain of guards, Areo Hotah, intercepted them in the passes. Ser Arys charged and died on Hotah's longaxe, brave and foolish to the last. Darkstar, in the chaos, slashed at Myrcella's face, cutting off her ear and leaving her scarred and bleeding. The plot collapsed, and Arianne was taken prisoner by her own father.

But when Doran finally spoke to his daughter in private, the words he said were not what she expected. He was not weak. He had never been weak. He had been patient, which was a different thing entirely, and patience was a weapon the young could seldom wield. He told Arianne of a pact sealed in secret years ago, a marriage alliance with the Targaryens. Her brother Quentyn had been sent across the narrow sea to find Daenerys Targaryen and offer her Dorne's allegiance and his hand in marriage. Fire and blood, Doran whispered, and the words hung in the air like the smoke of a pyre. Vengeance. Justice. Fire and blood. The Martells had been playing a longer game than anyone had imagined, and the board was not yet set.

Samwell Tarly's journey southward was a miserable affair of creaking ships and sour seas. Jon Snow had sent him to the Citadel at Oldtown to forge his chain and become a maester for the Night's Watch, and Sam had gone obediently, though every mile from the Wall felt like a betrayal. He sailed aboard the Cinnamon Wind with Maester Aemon, ancient and frail, the last Targaryen who had lived his whole life in quiet service to the realm. With them traveled the wildling girl Gilly and a babe that was not hers -- Mance Rayder's son, swapped with Gilly's own child by Jon to keep the boy safe from Melisandre's fires, for king's blood had power, and Mance had been a king of sorts.

The journey was hard. In Braavos they were stranded when their companion Dareon, the singer who was meant to recruit for the Night's Watch, spent their coin on whores and wine instead of passage. Sam found him in a tavern and confronted him, but Sam was Sam, and Dareon laughed in his face. It was not Sam who ended the deserter -- that was Arya's work, Cat of the Canals with her sharp little blade, though neither knew the other. Old Maester Aemon grew weaker by the day, his wits wandering, and in his fevered ramblings he spoke of Daenerys Targaryen and her dragons and the prophecy of the prince that was promised. The prince, Aemon said, was wrong -- the word in High Valyrian was gender-neutral, and the prince that was promised might well be a princess. Daenerys was the one. She had to be warned, had to be helped, had to be guided. But Aemon was a hundred and two years old, and the sea was cold, and before the Cinnamon Wind could reach Oldtown he slipped away in his sleep, the last of his line, gentle and wise to the end.

Sam wept for him, and then Sam went on, because that was what Sam did. He arrived in Oldtown, that vast and ancient city of learning, and made his way to the Citadel, and there he found Archmaester Marwyn, a thickset man with a shaved head and a reputation for dabbling in the very magics that the Citadel had spent centuries trying to suppress. Marwyn listened to Sam's tale of Aemon's words and Daenerys's dragons, and his eyes lit with a fierce intelligence. The grey sheep, he called the other archmaesters -- the men who had conspired to rid the world of magic and dragons centuries ago, and who would do the same again if they could. Marwyn would not wait for their permission. He took the Cinnamon Wind himself and sailed for Slaver's Bay to find the dragon queen, and he left Sam at the Citadel with instructions to forge his chain and speak to no one of prophecy or dragons.

Sam's guide to his new quarters was a novice who introduced himself as Pate, like the pig boy from the old story. He smiled pleasantly enough, this Pate, but the real Pate was dead and rotting in an alley, and the thing that wore his face had come from the House of Black and White, and it had a key that opened every door in the Citadel, and its purposes were its own.

This, then, was the world that remained when the crows came to feast: a realm of broken oaths and scheming lords, of holy men with swords and dead women who would not stay buried, of children playing at power and old men sailing toward dragons. The war was over, but the killing went on. Cersei had reached for the crown and grasped a viper instead. Jaime had looked in a mirror and seen a stranger. Brienne had gone seeking honor in a land where honor was a death sentence. The ironborn had chosen a madman for their king, and Dorne had revealed a patience that was more frightening than any fury.

Across the narrow sea, a girl who had been Arya Stark sat in darkness and saw nothing, and in the canals of Braavos the tide rose and fell as it always had, carrying with it the refuse of the city, the blood and the filth and the flowers, all mixed together in the black water. In the south, the sun hammered the Dornish sands, and in the north, the wind howled through empty holdfasts where no smoke rose from the chimneys. The crows circled and descended, and they did not discriminate between the victorious and the vanquished. They ate them all the same. That was the lesson of A Feast for Crows, if it had a lesson: that in the game of thrones, the winners rot beside the losers, and the crows care nothing for the banners men raise or the songs they sing. The feast goes on.
