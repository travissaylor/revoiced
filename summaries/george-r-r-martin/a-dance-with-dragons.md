The cold had teeth, and it gnawed at the dying man with relish.

Varamyr Sixskins lay in the snow beyond the Wall, his stolen body failing him at last. He had been a skinchanger of great power once, a man who had worn the skins of bears and shadowcats and wolves, who had flown on the wings of an eagle and torn the throats of men with fangs not his own. Now he was nothing. His three wolves had scattered, his shadowcat had fled, his great snow bear was long dead, and the woman Thistle crouched beside him with eyes that said she knew what he was, what he wanted. Haggon, the old mentor he had murdered, had taught him the rules of their kind. An abomination it was, to steal another's skin, to feast on human flesh while in a beast, to mate as wolf or bear. But Varamyr had broken those rules all his life. When death came for him at last, he reached out with his failing will and tried to seize Thistle, to pour himself into her and claim her body for his own. She fought him. She screamed and clawed at her own face and he felt her terror and her fury like a wall of fire, and then the cold took him and he slipped instead into One Eye, the oldest of his wolves, and the world turned to scent and sound and the taste of snow. Somewhere in the darkness beyond the Wall, the dead were walking, and the cold was rising.

The world had grown larger than any maester's map, and darker too.

At the Wall, a young man named Jon Snow wore the cloak of Lord Commander of the Night's Watch and found that it fit him ill. The 998th man to hold that office, the youngest in living memory, a bastard born of Eddard Stark and some woman whose name men still argued over in their cups. He had been chosen by his brothers in a desperate election while the corpses of wildlings still cooled on the frozen ground, and Stannis Baratheon -- the king who had come to save the realm when the realm had not known it needed saving -- watched from the shadows of his towers with the red priestess Melisandre at his side, her ruby choker glowing like an ember at her throat.

Jon's troubles were as numerous as the stars above the ice. Stannis demanded fealty, castles, men. The wildlings had been shattered at the Wall but not destroyed, and thousands of the free folk still wandered the haunted forest, prey to cold and hunger and the dead things that moved in the night. The Others were coming. That much Jon knew in his bones, knew it the way a wolf knows the scent of blood on the wind. The enemy was death itself, and death did not treat with kings or respect the boundaries men drew upon their maps. So Jon Snow, the bastard of Winterfell, made a choice that would have horrified every Lord Commander who had come before him. He opened the gates. He let the wildlings through the Wall.

It was the right thing to do. He told himself that, again and again, in the cold hours before dawn when sleep would not come. The free folk were not the enemy. The dead were the enemy. Every wildling who died beyond the Wall was a corpse that would rise again, blue-eyed and murderous, to serve the Others. Tormund Giantsbane came through first, the old raider with his booming laugh and his talk of member and mead, leading thousands of his people -- women, children, warriors, and spearwives with frost in their hair and axes on their hips. Jon settled them along the Wall in the abandoned castles, those crumbling towers and empty halls where no black brother had kept watch in a hundred years. He gave them land and roofs and asked only that they help defend the realm of men.

His brothers did not understand. Bowen Marsh, the Lord Steward, First Ranger, and half the garrison looked at him as though he had gone mad. They saw only enemies coming through those gates -- the same wildlings who had killed their friends, who had battered the Wall with a hundred thousand screaming raiders not so long ago. Jon tried to explain. He tried to make them see. But some walls, he was learning, were harder to breach than the one made of ice.

Melisandre watched from her tower, where the fires burned day and night and the shadows danced. She had seen this boy in her flames -- Jon Snow, the one the red god had shown her, though she had prayed for a glimpse of Azor Ahai reborn and did not understand what the Lord of Light intended. She warned him of daggers in the dark, of enemies who smiled. She showed him things in the fire, shadows and snow and a girl on a dying horse who might have been his sister. The girl who came was not Arya but Alys Karstark, fleeing a forced marriage, and Jon gave her sanctuary and wed her to Sigorn of Thenn, binding wildling and northerner in a union no one had foreseen. He sent Mance Rayder south in secret, glamored by Melisandre's magic to wear another man's face, tasked with rescuing the girl the Boltons called Arya Stark. At the Wall, Jon played a game more dangerous than he knew, balancing wildling and crow, king and priest, duty and desire, and all the while the cold crept closer.

He sent ships to Hardhome, that cursed ruin on the coast beyond the Wall where thousands of wildlings huddled in squalor, dying of cold and hunger and worse things still. The reports that came back spoke of dead things in the water and dead things in the woods, of slavers from across the sea and storms that shattered ships. Mother Mole had promised her people that ships would come to carry them to safety, and some ships had come, but not enough, never enough, and the horrors at Hardhome grew with each passing day.

Then came the letter. A pink wax seal, the flayed man of the Dreadfort pressed into it, and words that burned like wildfire. Ramsay Bolton wrote -- or someone who wished Jon to believe it was Ramsay -- that Stannis was dead, his host destroyed, his magic sword taken. The letter demanded his bride back, demanded Melisandre and Val and the wildling prince, demanded Theon Greyjoy, and promised that if Jon did not comply, he would cut out his bastard's heart and eat it. Jon Snow read that letter to a hall full of wildlings and black brothers, and something inside him broke or crystallized or both. He would ride south, he told them. He would take the fight to Ramsay Bolton. The free folk roared their approval. The Night's Watch did not. Jon had forgotten, or chosen to forget, the oldest law of the Watch -- the Watch takes no part. As he walked through the snow toward his horse, Bowen Marsh stepped from the shadows with tears streaming down his fat cheeks, and the first knife went in. "For the Watch," Marsh whispered, and the blade slid between Jon's ribs. Then another brother, and another, each one murmuring those same words like a prayer -- *for the Watch, for the Watch* -- and Jon fell to his knees in the snow and reached for Longclaw but his fingers had no strength, and the last word on his lips was *Ghost*, the name of his white direwolf, and then the cold took him and the world went black.

Stannis, meanwhile, had marched south with his knights and his queen's men and his red god's fires, seeking to win the North by reclaiming Winterfell from the Boltons who had stolen it. The Bastard of the Dreadfort, Ramsay Bolton, had taken Jon's childhood home and made it a charnel house. Stannis gathered the northern mountain clans to his banner -- the Wulls, the Norreys, the Flints of the mountains -- grim hard folk who remembered the Starks and would not bend to Bolton. But winter had come in truth now, and the snows fell so thick and heavy that Stannis's army ground to a halt in the wolfswood, men and horses freezing, the supply wagons lost in drifts taller than a mounted knight. In the camps men ate their dead horses and prayed to whatever gods they followed, and the queen's men looked at the flames and whispered of sacrifice.

In the halls of Winterfell, Roose Bolton held court like a spider in the center of a web, his pale eyes betraying nothing. He had married his bastard Ramsay to Arya Stark -- or the girl the world believed was Arya Stark. In truth she was Jeyne Poole, a steward's daughter, Sansa's childhood friend, trained and terrorized into playing the part. Theon Greyjoy knew the lie. Theon, who had been Reek, who had been the Prince of Winterfell, who had been nothing at all. Ramsay had broken him the way a man breaks a horse, with the flaying knife and worse, and what remained was a cringing wretch who flinched at his own name. Theon had known Jeyne in the days before. He had danced with her at feasts in this very hall when the Starks still ruled and the world made sense. Now he watched her weep and could do nothing.

But the old gods had not forgotten Winterfell. In the godswood, the heart tree wept red tears, and a voice whispered through the rustling leaves -- *Theon* -- and the broken man remembered that he had been someone, once. Beneath Ramsay's cruelties and the ghosts that haunted every corridor, conspiracies seethed like rats in the walls. Wyman Manderly had come to the wedding feast, that vast lord of White Harbor who could scarcely sit a horse, and he brought pies and smiles and murdered Freys in secret, baking them into the pastry he served at the feast while the bards sang of the Rat Cook. The northerners remembered the Red Wedding. The North remembered.

When the moment came, it was Theon who acted. With the washerwoman -- Abel's women, Mance Rayder's spearwives in disguise, for Mance was not dead after all but had been sent by Melisandre wearing a glamor to rescue the false Arya -- Theon took Jeyne Poole by the hand and led her to the battlements. The snow was deep below, impossibly deep. Jeyne was weeping, her face raw from the cold, and Theon looked at the drop and thought of Bran, whom he had pushed from a tower in another life. They jumped. They fell through the white and the silence and the cold, and lived, landing in a snowdrift soft enough to break their fall. Stannis's men found them beyond the walls, half-frozen and more dead than alive, and brought them before the king who ground his teeth and judged.

Far to the south, Davos Seaworth -- the Onion Knight, Stannis's most faithful servant -- had been sent to White Harbor to treat with Lord Manderly and win his fleet and his allegiance. Manderly was a man who had lost a son at the Red Wedding and would not forgive, but he had Lannister envoys watching his every move and Frey kin demanding justice. So he played a mummer's game. He took Davos prisoner, condemned him publicly, and displayed a head and hands upon the walls -- another man's head and another man's hands, the fingers shortened with an axe to match the smuggler's. In the darkness of the Wolf's Den, Manderly came to Davos in secret and told him the truth. Rickon Stark lived. The youngest son of Eddard Stark was hidden on the savage isle of Skagos, where the old tales spoke of cannibals and unicorns. Bring me my liege lord, Manderly said, and White Harbor and all its strength would rally to the Stark cause. Davos, who had lost sons of his own and understood what it meant to protect a child, agreed.

Beyond the Wall, in caverns deep beneath the frozen earth where the roots of weirwoods formed a lattice like the bones of the world, Bran Stark found what he had been searching for. The three-eyed crow was not a bird at all but a man -- or what was left of one. Brynden Rivers, Lord Bloodraven, a bastard of House Targaryen born more than a century past, one-eyed and ancient beyond reckoning, his flesh grown into the weirwood roots so that it was impossible to tell where the tree ended and the man began. The children of the forest moved in those caverns, small dark creatures with great eyes and hands of four fingers, the last remnants of a race older than men. They fed Bran a paste of weirwood seeds, and the world opened like a flower. He looked through the eyes of the heart trees and saw the past and the present laid bare before him. He saw his father cleaning Ice in the godswood of Winterfell. He heard voices on the wind. Time was a river, and Bran learned to swim in it, though the current was strong and the old man warned him that the past was written, the ink was dry. Yet there were moments when Bran called out and the leaves seemed to answer, and he wondered whether the past was truly as fixed as Bloodraven believed.

Across the narrow sea, Tyrion Lannister was drinking himself to death and not doing a very good job of it. The dwarf had murdered his father with a crossbow bolt through the bowels while Lord Tywin sat upon the privy, and he had strangled his former lover Shae in the Hand's bedchamber, and the memory of both acts sat in his gut like a stone. Varys the Spider had smuggled him out of King's Landing in a cask, and after weeks of darkness and his own filth, Tyrion emerged in the manse of Illyrio Mopatis in the Free City of Pentos, where the Magister of cheese and smiles told him of a dragon queen across the world and urged him to seek her out.

Tyrion traveled east along the Rhoyne on a poleboat called the *Shy Maid*, drinking wine and playing cyvasse with a company that included a man called Griff and his son Young Griff, a lad with blue-dyed hair and a quick mind, and Haldon Halfmaester, and a septa named Lemore who swam naked in the river each morning and had stretch marks that spoke of children she never mentioned. It did not take Tyrion long to see through the mummer's farce. Griff was Jon Connington, once Hand of the King to Aerys Targaryen, exiled and believed dead. And Young Griff -- Young Griff was Aegon Targaryen, the son of Prince Rhaegar and Princess Elia, the babe who had supposedly been dashed against a wall by Gregor Clegane during the Sack of King's Landing. Or so Connington and Illyrio believed. Varys had smuggled the infant away and replaced him with a tanner's child, and Aegon had been raised in secret, tutored and trained to be the perfect king. Tyrion looked at the boy and saw ambition waiting to hatch, and because Tyrion Lannister could never leave well enough alone, he planted a seed. Why wait for Daenerys? he asked. Why cross the world to beg a woman for her hand when you could seize a kingdom with the army you already have? Go west, not east. Take what is yours.

The seed took root. Aegon turned his ships for Westeros, and Jon Connington -- who was hiding the grey death of greyscale beneath his glove, the spreading doom he had caught when stonemen had dragged him into the river in the ruins of the Sorrows, that cursed stretch of the Rhoyne where the fog hung thick as wool and the Bridge of Dream loomed above the water and the grey men reached with their cracked and scaling hands -- led the Golden Company to the shores of the Stormlands. The Golden Company had never broken a contract in its history, but for this they broke their word to Myr, because the Company had been founded by Bittersteel, a Targaryen bastard, and its ranks were filled with the sons and grandsons of exiles who had followed the Blackfyres into defeat and dreamed of going home. Connington took Griffin's Roost first, his own ancestral seat, the castle he had lost when Robert's Rebellion had sent him into exile. He moved swiftly, taking castles along the cape before word could spread, and soon enough the Golden Company held half the Stormlands. Aegon, the boy who would be king, led from the front as Connington had taught him, and the realm trembled with the rumor: a Targaryen prince had returned.

Tyrion, meanwhile, was seized by Jorah Mormont, that exiled bear of a knight who had once spied on Daenerys for Varys and been banished for it, and who now sought to win back her favor by bringing her a gift -- the dwarf who had been condemned for murdering King Joffrey, the son of the man who had destroyed House Targaryen's grip on the Iron Throne. Jorah dragged Tyrion east toward Meereen, through the ruins of old Valyria where the Smoking Sea still churned and the stonemen lurched from the fog. There Jorah touched grey flesh and the disease took hold in him, though he hid it well. They were captured by slavers, sold at auction in the market of Meereen's besiegers, and Tyrion found himself performing in a grotesque jousting show astride a pig alongside a dwarf girl named Penny, whose brother he had unwittingly caused the death of when Cersei had put a price on any dwarf's head. In the slave pens and the fighting pits and the stinking camps of the Yunkish host, Tyrion did what Tyrion always did. He talked. He schemed. He survived. He wormed his way into the Second Sons, a sellsword company, and began to plot their betrayal of Yunkai and their return to Daenerys's cause, for Tyrion Lannister had decided that he would see this dragon queen and offer her what remained of his shattered wits, and if the world burned for it, well, the world had not been particularly kind to him.

In Meereen, Daenerys Targaryen sat a throne she had never wanted in a city she could not leave. She had conquered Slaver's Bay with fire and blood, freed the slaves, and broken the masters, and now she reaped the harvest of her justice. The Sons of the Harpy murdered her freedmen in the streets, leaving their corpses with brass masks upon their faces. The great families of Meereen smiled at her by day and sharpened their knives by night. Yunkai had raised new armies and hired sellsword companies and built a fleet to blockade the harbor. Astapor, the first city she had freed, had descended into plague and chaos, a hell of bloody flux and warring kings, and the survivors streamed toward Meereen in a river of misery that Daenerys could not turn away.

She had three dragons, and they were growing. Drogon, the black beast, the winged shadow named for her dead husband, had killed a child -- a little girl, charred bones and blackened flesh, and the memory haunted Daenerys like a ghost. She had chained Viserion and Rhaegal in the great pit beneath the pyramid, and their cries echoed through the stone and through her dreams. Drogon she could not catch. The black dragon had fled to the Dothraki sea and made his lair in a scorched ruin the Ghiscari called Daznak's Hill, and Daenerys told herself that it was for the best, though her heart knew otherwise. A dragon is not a slave, she had told the masters of Astapor. But could a dragon be a queen?

She did what she thought she must. She made peace. She married Hizdahr zo Loraq, a Meereenese nobleman of ancient lineage, a man of perfumed words and silken robes who promised that the killings would stop if she would reopen the fighting pits and wed him. Daenerys loathed the pits. She had seen men die in the arena and found no glory in it, only waste. But the blood price was being paid in her streets every night, and she was tired of burying children, so she spoke the words and took Hizdahr into her bed and told herself that this was ruling, this was the price of peace.

It was not enough. It was never enough.

The Shavepate, Skahaz mo Kandaq, that brutal bald man with his mask of bronze, urged her to answer blood with blood, to unleash the Unsullied on the great families and put every son of the harpy to the sword. Daenerys refused. She would not be the butcher queen. She told herself that, and tried to believe it, even as her people died in the alleys and the pale mare -- the bloody flux -- crept through the camps outside her walls, carried by the refugees from Astapor who had nowhere else to go. She opened her gates to them, the sick and the starving, and her advisors warned that she would bring plague into the city, and she did it anyway because she could not watch them die at her doorstep.

Daario Naharis, her lover, the sellsword captain with the forked blue beard and the golden tooth and the swagger of a man who feared nothing, had been sent away as a hostage to the Yunkai'i as part of the peace accords, and Daenerys ached for him in ways she would never admit to her counselors. She knew that Daario was a poor choice -- a sellsword, a killer, a man with no lands and no lineage -- and that Hizdahr was the wiser match. But wisdom and desire pulled in different directions, and Daenerys was young, for all her titles, and the heart wants what the heart wants. Barristan Selmy, the old knight who had served her father and Robert Baratheon both, watched her with worried eyes and said little, for he was a man of the sword and not the tongue, and the politics of Meereen bewildered him as they bewildered them all. He missed the simplicity of Westeros, where a knight knew his duty and a queen did not have to choose between peace and justice every morning before she broke her fast.

The peace lasted until the fighting pits reopened, and even before the day of Daznak's Pit there was treachery in the air. Someone had prepared a bowl of honeyed locusts for the queen -- sweet and spiced and deadly. Hizdahr pressed them on her, urged her to eat, but Daenerys had no appetite for insects that day, and the poisoned treats found other mouths instead. Strong Belwas, the eunuch pit fighter who served as her guard, devoured the entire bowl and nearly died of it, purging and shaking for days after.

Hizdahr had his spectacle, his blood and sand and screaming crowds, and Daenerys sat above it all in her tokar of white silk fringed with pearls and watched men die and tried not to show what it cost her. Then the shadow fell across the arena. Drogon came. Drawn by the noise and the blood, the great black dragon descended from the sky with wings that blotted out the sun, and the crowd dissolved into chaos. Men ran screaming. Spears were hurled. Someone struck the dragon with a lance and Drogon shrieked and snapped and bathed the sands in flame, and Daenerys -- Daenerys Stormborn of House Targaryen, the Unburnt, the Mother of Dragons -- ran toward the fire. She cracked her whip at the beast's snout and he reared back and she climbed upon his back, and the scales were hot enough to blister her hands and the smoke stung her eyes and she pulled a spear from his flank and he screamed and leapt into the sky. She held on. She held on with thighs and hands and will, and Meereen fell away below her, and the fighting pit and the pyramids and the suffering she could not mend, and for a moment she was nothing but joy and terror and the wind in her hair, a girl on a dragon, flying.

Drogon carried her to his lair in the Dothraki sea, and there she wandered, sick and starving, eating berries that gave her bloody flux, talking to the stars about the house with the red door in Braavos that she remembered from childhood, about the choices she had made and the ones yet to come. Drogon fed on wild horses and slept in the sun, and Daenerys sat beside him and tried to understand what she was. When the Dothraki found her -- a khalasar led by Khal Jhaqo, one of the men who had abandoned her when Drogo died -- she stood beside Drogon on the scorched grass, and the dragon's shadow stretched across the plains like a promise or a threat.

In her absence, Meereen teetered on the brink. Barristan Selmy, old and stiff and honourable to the bone, discovered that Hizdahr had likely been complicit in an attempt to poison Daenerys at the fighting pit -- locusts dipped in some sweet venom that the queen had not eaten but others had. Selmy arrested Hizdahr and seized control of the city in Daenerys's name, and then the Yunkai'i attacked, their trebuchets hurling corpses over the walls, and the battle for Meereen began in earnest, though its ending lay beyond the pages of this tale.

Before the battle came the folly of Quentyn Martell. Prince Doran's eldest son had crossed the world to win a dragon queen's hand, carrying with him a secret pact signed in faded ink by long-dead men that promised Targaryen and Martell would be joined. He was a plain boy, earnest and well-meaning but without the fire that Daenerys desired. She refused him gently, and Quentyn, who had lost friends and years in the journey, decided that if he could not have the queen he would have a dragon. He went down into the pit beneath the Great Pyramid with a whip and a torch and the bravery of the desperate, and Viserion's jaws yawned wide and the heat poured forth, but it was Rhaegal who came from behind with a blast of flame that consumed the Dornish prince. Quentyn Martell burned for three days before he died, and the dragons were free, and Meereen's sky filled with their wings.

Across the sea, Victarion Greyjoy sailed the Iron Fleet toward Slaver's Bay, carrying with him a horn called Dragonbinder that Euron Crow's Eye had given him, a terrible artifact of Valyria that could supposedly bind dragons to the will of its master -- though the man who blew it would burn from the inside out. Victarion was not a clever man, but he was a brutal one, and he had plans of his own that did not include serving his brother's designs. A red priest named Moqorro had come aboard his ship, plucked from the sea, and this strange dark man with his tattooed face and his worship of R'hllor whispered of fire and destiny and healed Victarion's infected hand with prayers that turned the flesh black and smoking. Victarion meant to take Daenerys for his own and use her dragons to burn Euron from the Seastone Chair, and if there was irony in an ironborn reaver coming to court a queen with blood and fire, it was lost on him entirely.

In Braavos, a girl who had once been Arya Stark of Winterfell scrubbed floors and washed the dead and learned the ways of the Faceless Men in the House of Black and White. She had given up her name, or tried to. She was no one, the kindly man told her, and no one could be anyone. They blinded her, and in the darkness she learned to listen, to feel the lie in a man's voice, to move without sight. She found she could see through the eyes of a cat in the alleyway, the wolf blood in her veins whispering of abilities the Faceless Men did not fully understand. When her sight was restored, they gave her a face that was not her own and sent her to kill a man, and she did it well, and they called her apprentice and promised her more. But beneath the mask, Needle still waited, hidden in a step by the canal, and Arya Stark's list of names still ran through her head before sleep each night like a prayer.

In the Water Gardens of Dorne, Prince Doran Martell sat in his wheeled chair among the blood orange trees and the sound of children splashing in the pools, and behind his gout-swollen legs and his mild eyes, he moved his pieces across the board. He had sent Quentyn to Daenerys. He had sent his daughter Arianne -- though that tale belonged to another book -- and now he sent his nieces, the Sand Snakes, those fierce bastard daughters of his dead brother Oberyn, to King's Landing to sit on the small council and infiltrate the Faith, to watch and wait and whisper. Doran had been patient for seventeen years, since the day Gregor Clegane had crushed his sister Elia's skull and murdered her children. Patience, he told his angry nieces, was the weapon of the wise. The blood debt would be paid. He need only wait for the right moment, and the right moment, it seemed, had nearly arrived, for a young man with silver hair had landed in the Stormlands and called himself Aegon, Elia's son, and Doran's slow careful plans shifted and turned like the gears of a great machine.

In King's Landing, the Faith had sunk its claws into the flesh of power and would not let go. Cersei Lannister, who had armed the sparrows and raised the High Septon to mock Tyrion and torment Margaery, found herself caught in the trap she had built. The charges were fornication, conspiracy, and murder -- the murder of the previous High Septon, among other crimes. In the cells of the Great Sept of Baelor, Cersei wept and raged and finally broke, confessing to lesser sins to win her release while denying the greater ones. The High Sparrow granted her freedom on conditions that scraped her pride to the bone. She would walk from the Sept to the Red Keep, naked and shorn, through the streets of the city she had once ruled.

And so Cersei Lannister walked. They shaved her golden hair to stubble and stripped her bare and set her on the cobblestones, and the septas cried *Shame, shame* behind her, and the smallfolk lined the streets and screamed. They called her whore and showed her what they thought of her with rotten fruit and dung and worse, and Cersei, who had been the most beautiful woman in the Seven Kingdoms, who had been a queen, who had been the daughter of Tywin Lannister and had believed herself his true heir, walked through all of it with her feet bleeding and her flesh filthy and her eyes fixed on the Red Keep ahead. By the time she reached the castle her legs had given out and she was carried the last steps by Ser Robert Strong -- the newest member of the Kingsguard, an eight-foot knight in white armor who never spoke and never raised his visor and bore more than passing resemblance to a dead man named Gregor Clegane. The trial by combat would come, and Cersei would have her champion, but the walk had broken something in her that would not easily mend.

While Cersei walked and wept, her uncle Kevan Lannister tried to rebuild what she had destroyed. Kevan was a solid man, not brilliant but competent, and he had taken the regency for young King Tommen with the grim determination of a soldier repairing a fortification under siege. He mended fences with the Tyrells, calmed the Faith, steadied the small council. The realm began to breathe again. The cracks Cersei had opened started, slowly, to close.

Which was precisely why he had to die.

The epilogue belongs to Kevan Lannister, and it is the last thing he ever sees. Called to the chambers of Grand Maester Pycelle on an urgent matter, Kevan finds the old man dead, his skull crushed, and then a crossbow bolt punches through his chest and he turns to see a face he never expected -- Varys the Spider, the eunuch who had vanished from King's Landing in the hour of Tyrion's escape. Varys explains it all with the patient courtesy of a man teaching a child his letters. Kevan was too competent, too capable of uniting the realm. The Lannisters needed to be at each other's throats when Aegon Targaryen came. Pycelle was killed because he might have been useful to Kevan. Everything -- the chaos Varys had sown, the secrets he had hoarded, the wars he had whispered into being -- all of it had been for this: to clear the path for the boy he had saved from the Sack, the prince raised to be everything a king should be, just and wise and strong. Kevan tries to speak, to warn someone, but his strength is gone and the little birds come -- Varys's child spies, the little mice that crept through the walls of the Red Keep with their sharp knives and their silence -- and they finish what the crossbow began.

The candles guttered and went out, and darkness filled the room, and Kevan Lannister's blood spread across the cold stone floor while somewhere in the Stormlands a silver-haired boy called himself a king and raised his banners.

So ended the dance -- or so began another, larger one. Jon Snow lay bleeding in the snow, his brothers' knives still warm with his blood, the word *Ghost* dying on his lips. Daenerys stood in the Dothraki sea with a dragon at her back and a khalasar before her, a long way from home and uncertain of the path. Tyrion schemed in a siege camp outside Meereen, a small man with large ambitions and nothing left to lose. Stannis froze in the northern snows, Theon trembled in chains, Bran peered through time itself in a cave beneath the world, and in King's Landing the game of thrones continued as it always had, with blood and fire and the quiet murder of good men in dark rooms.

Winter had come at last, as the Starks had always promised, and the world was not ready. The dragons were loose and the dead were rising and the Wall still stood, though the cracks ran deep, and no man living could say whether dawn would come again or whether the long night had only just begun. In the game of thrones, you win or you die, and the players had never been more desperate nor the stakes more terribly high. The board was set. The pieces moved. And the story, like winter, was far from over.
