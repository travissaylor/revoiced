In my younger and more vulnerable years my father gave me some advice that I have been turning over in my mind ever since, and it is this: that the inclinations of a man's reserve are best understood by what he chooses to tell you, not by what he keeps silent. And so it was that in the summer of 1922 I came East to learn the bond business, and found instead something altogether different -- a story that has, in the intervening years, settled into my bones like the damp of a Long Island evening, impossible to shake, impossible to forget. I rented a house in West Egg, that less fashionable of the two peninsulas that jutted out into the Sound like a pair of enormous eggs, identical in contour but separated by a courtesy bay, and in every other particular separated by a gulf wider than the water between them. My house was a small eyesore wedged between two vast places that rented for twelve or fifteen thousand a season, and on one side, looming grander than any palace I had seen in France, stood the colossal mansion of a man named Gatsby.

I knew nothing of Gatsby then. I knew only that the lights from his house burned until dawn on summer nights, and that the music carried across the lawn to my little place like something remembered from a dream, and that motorcars lined his drive on weekends and his guests conducted themselves with an abandon that suggested the rules governing ordinary human behavior had been suspended by some vast and invisible authority. I had come East with the vague restlessness of a young man who suspects that life is being conducted elsewhere, and I had a cousin in East Egg -- Daisy Buchanan, nee Fay -- who had married enormously well and lived in a house that made Gatsby's look almost modest by the breeding of its architecture, if not by its scale.

I drove out to East Egg on a warm evening to have dinner with the Buchanans -- Tom and Daisy -- and it was there that everything began, though I did not know it at the time, as one never knows at the time. Tom Buchanan had been a figure of considerable celebrity at Yale, one of those men whose physical achievements at twenty-one cast a long and imperious shadow over everything that comes after. He was a sturdy, straw-haired man of thirty with a rather hard mouth and a supercilious manner, and two shining arrogant eyes that gave him the appearance of always leaning aggressively forward. His was a body of enormous leverage -- cruel, if you like -- and he used it with a carelessness that bordered on contempt for everything softer than himself. There was a touch of paternal contempt in his voice even toward people he liked, and there were men at New Haven who had hated him bitterly.

Daisy was another matter entirely. She sat on an enormous couch in a room where the windows were ajar and the curtains billowed like pale flags, and when she spoke her voice was the kind of voice that the ear follows up and down as if each speech were an arrangement of notes that would never be played again. It was full of money -- that was the inexhaustible charm that rose and fell in it, the jingle of it, the cymbals' song of it. She told me in her beautiful, low, thrilling voice that she had been everywhere and done everything and seen everything, and her eyes looked at me with an absolute faith that I had never seen in any woman's face before. She was appalling, and she was magnificent, and I think she knew it.

Jordan Baker was there too -- a slender, small-breasted girl with an erect carriage, a young woman whom I had seen somewhere before, whose name, I eventually remembered, appeared in the sporting pages. She was incurably dishonest, as I would later discover, but she had a way of looking at you that made dishonesty seem like a minor and forgivable failing, a concession to the general disorder of things. And there was a fourth presence at dinner that evening -- the presence of Tom's mistress, unspoken but hovering at the edge of every exchange like smoke you cannot quite locate but cannot ignore. When the telephone rang during dinner and Tom rose heavily from his chair, Daisy's face took on an expression so lovely and so desperate that I understood, without being told, that there was a woman on the other end of that line, and that everyone at the table knew it, and that no one would say a word.

After dinner, I walked down to the water's edge and looked out across the bay. And there, at the far end of Daisy's dock, there was a green light. And across the water, on my own side, I could just make out a figure standing on the lawn of the Gatsby mansion -- a solitary figure with his arms stretched out toward the dark water in a fashion that was trembling and ceremonial, as though he were performing some private rite of devotion to an object visible only to him. I looked out at the green light myself, and when I turned back, the figure was gone, and I was alone with the sound of the water and the distant music and the sense that something incalculable had just occurred.

Between West Egg and New York there lay a desolate stretch of land -- a valley of ashes, a grey stretch of waste where the dumping of industrial ash had created a landscape so bleak and terrible that it seemed to exist outside of time, outside of geography, in some purgatorial realm where the refuse of prosperity came to rest. Over this valley brooded the enormous eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg, painted on an old billboard -- blue and gigantic, their retinas a yard high, gazing out from behind enormous yellow spectacles over a landscape that bore no relation to anything human. They looked out of no face, but instead from a pair of enormous spectacles which sat on a nonexistent nose, and they watched everything and understood nothing, or perhaps understood everything and watched nothing -- it was impossible to say.

It was in this valley that Tom Buchanan kept the woman who complicated his life. Her name was Myrtle Wilson, and she was the wife of George Wilson, a spiritless man who ran a garage at the edge of the ash heaps. Myrtle was stout and faintly beautiful, with a vitality about her as though the nerves of her body were continually smouldering. Tom brought me to meet her one afternoon with the casual brutality of a man who has ceased to distinguish between cruelty and habit, and we proceeded to an apartment he kept in the city for the purpose of these encounters, and there was a party of a particularly wretched and aimless kind -- the kind of party where everyone talks too loudly and drinks too quickly and the gaiety is indistinguishable from desperation. It ended when Tom, in a short deft movement, broke Myrtle's nose with his open hand, because she had dared to say Daisy's name aloud.

But it was Gatsby's parties that defined that summer -- those enormous, glittering affairs that bloomed on his grounds every weekend like some gorgeous flower grown in a hothouse of ambition and loneliness. In his blue gardens men and girls came and went like moths among the whisperings and the champagne and the stars, and the air was alive with laughter and music and the sense that anything might happen, that the ordinary constraints of existence had been temporarily lifted by the sheer force of wealth and desire. There were buffets garnished with glistening hors-d'oeuvres, and there were two suppers served at midnight, and there was an orchestra -- not a thin five-piece affair but a whole pitful of oboes and trombones and saxophones and viols and cornets and piccolos and low and high drums. People were not invited -- they went there. They got into automobiles which bore them out to Long Island and somehow ended up at Gatsby's door. Once there, they were introduced by someone who knew Gatsby and after that they conducted themselves according to the rules of an amusement park.

The rumors about Gatsby were endless and extravagant. He was a German spy during the war. He had killed a man. He was a nephew of Von Hindenburg's. He was a bootlegger. He was an Oxford man. The rumors circulated among his guests with the cheerful irresponsibility of gossip exchanged by people who have no stake in the truth, and Gatsby himself did nothing to dispel them, which only fed them further. He was a mystery, and mysteries, in that summer of 1922, were a kind of currency -- they purchased interest and speculation and the flickering attention of people who might otherwise have moved on to the next diversion.

I attended one such party on a night in early summer, drawn by curiosity and a sense of proximity that felt almost like obligation. The party had a quality of enchantment about it that I had not anticipated -- the gardens were strung with colored lights and the bay was alive with the movement of boats, and there was a bar with a real brass rail set up on the lawn, stocked with gins and liquors and cordials so long forgotten that most of the female guests were too young to know one from another. By midnight the hilarity had increased, and a celebrated tenor had sung in Italian, and a notorious contralto had performed in jazz, and between the numbers people were doing stunts all over the garden, while happy, vacuous bursts of laughter rose toward the summer sky. It was there that I met Gatsby himself, though I did not recognize him at first. He was a man of about my age, with one of those rare smiles that possessed a quality of eternal reassurance in it, that you might come across four or five times in a life. It understood you just as far as you wanted to be understood, believed in you as you would like to believe in yourself, and assured you that it had precisely the impression of you that, at your best, you hoped to convey. And then it vanished, and I was looking at an elegant young rough-neck, a year or two over thirty, whose elaborate formality of speech just missed being absurd.

The truth about Gatsby came to me in pieces -- fragments delivered by Jordan Baker with the beautiful indifference of a woman accustomed to knowing things that other people merely suspected. Gatsby, she told me, had been a young officer stationed in Louisville in 1917 when he met Daisy Fay, and he had fallen in love with her with a totality that left no room for doubt or hesitation or the practical considerations that govern more sensible affections. He was penniless then, a young man without a past worth mentioning, and Daisy was the first nice girl he had ever known. Her house had a ripe mystery about it, a hint of bedrooms upstairs more beautiful and cool than other bedrooms, of gay and radiant activities taking place through its corridors, and of romances that were not musty and laid away in lavender but fresh and breathing and redolent of this year's shining motor-cars and of dances whose flowers were scarcely withered. He loved her for the world she represented -- the world of old money and casual grace, of certainty so profound it never needed to announce itself. And when he shipped out for the war, he carried with him a promise that he would return, and a determination so fierce it would reshape his entire existence.

Gatsby had been born James Gatz of North Dakota, the son of shiftless and unsuccessful farm people, and his imagination had never really accepted them as his parents at all. He had invented just the sort of Jay Gatsby that a seventeen-year-old boy would be likely to invent, and to this conception he was faithful to the end. He had worked as a clam-digger and a salmon-fisher and in any other capacity that brought him food and bed, until the day a yacht dropped anchor over the most insidious flat on Lake Superior and young James Gatz rowed out to warn its owner, a man named Dan Cody, of an approaching storm. Cody took him on in a vague personal capacity, and Gatsby learned the contours of wealth -- its manners, its vocabulary, its expectations -- the way a devoted student learns a language, until the borrowed elegance became indistinguishable from the genuine article. When Cody died, Gatsby was left with nothing but his education and his ambition, and from these two materials he constructed an empire.

What Gatsby did to accumulate his fortune was the subject of considerable speculation among his guests, who consumed his liquor with an enthusiasm inversely proportional to their curiosity about its provenance. There were rumors -- whispers about bootlegging and connections to a figure named Meyer Wolfshiem, a man who, it was said, had fixed the World's Series in 1919, a thing that had merely seemed impossible until you heard about it, and then it seemed merely inevitable. Gatsby took me to lunch one afternoon and introduced me to Wolfshiem, a small, flat-nosed Jew with two fine growths of hair luxuriating in either nostril, and cuff buttons made of human molars, and a sentimental attachment to the past that manifested itself in melancholy reminiscences about people who were no longer alive. The lunch left me with the impression that Gatsby's wealth had a quality of improvisation about it, as though the fortune had been assembled hastily and with methods that would not bear close examination.

But none of this mattered to Gatsby. The fortune, the mansion, the parties, the shirts from England -- all of it was scaffolding, erected for the sole purpose of reaching a single green light across the water. All of it was for Daisy. And so I agreed to help him -- I arranged a tea at my little house, and invited Daisy, and when she arrived, Gatsby was already there, pale as death, leaning against the mantelpiece in a strained counterfeit of perfect ease. The rain was coming down hard outside, and Gatsby knocked my clock off the mantel in his nervousness, and for a terrible half-hour the whole affair had the quality of a mistake, of something that should never have been attempted. But I left them alone, and when I returned, the rain had stopped, and Daisy's face was smeared with tears, and Gatsby literally glowed -- without a word or a gesture of exultation, a new well-being radiated from him and filled the little room.

He took her through his house then, and showed her everything -- the rooms, the gardens, the swimming pool, the hydroplane. He showed her his shirts, pulling them from the wardrobe in great stacks and tossing them before her in piles of every color and pattern, shirts of sheer linen and thick silk and fine flannel, and Daisy bent her lovely head and wept into the beautiful shirts, crying that she had never seen such beautiful shirts before. It was a strange and marvellous thing, that weeping, and I think it was not really about the shirts at all but about the five years of longing that the shirts represented, five years during which Gatsby had bent every force of his will toward this single moment, and now the moment was here, and it was more than she could bear, and less than he had imagined, and both of these things at once.

There was a moment during that tour of his house when Gatsby stood before the green light across the bay, and it occurred to me that the light had lost something for him now that Daisy was beside him. It had once been the object of his longing, the symbol of everything he desired, and now here was the thing itself, breathing and warm and real, and the symbol had been diminished by the arrival of its referent. The colossal significance of that green light had vanished forever. Compared to the great distance that had separated him from Daisy it had seemed very near to her, almost touching her. It had seemed as close as a star to the moon. Now it was again a green light on a dock. His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one.

For a while, things were beautiful. Gatsby and Daisy met at my house and at his, and the lights in his mansion no longer burned until dawn because the parties were no longer necessary. He had achieved the thing toward which he had been striving, or at least he believed he had, and there was a quality of stunned contentment about him that was almost touching. But Gatsby wanted more than contentment. He wanted the past -- not merely to recapture it but to obliterate everything that had intervened, to restore a state of affairs so complete and so perfect that it would be as though the intervening years had never occurred. He wanted Daisy to tell Tom she had never loved him. He wanted her to erase her history with a single sentence and step, pristine and unburdened, into the future he had prepared for her. It was the colossal vitality of his illusion that staggered me. No amount of fire or freshness could challenge what a man would store up in his ghostly heart.

I told him once that you cannot repeat the past, and his response was immediate and incredulous -- "Can't repeat the past?" he cried. "Why of course you can!" He looked around him wildly, as if the past were lurking here in the shadow of his house, just out of reach of his hand. And I understood then that Gatsby's dream was not merely romantic but metaphysical -- he believed, with a faith that was religious in its intensity, that the universe could be bent to the shape of human desire, that time itself was negotiable, that love, if it burned hot enough and long enough, could melt the very architecture of what had already happened and recast it into something new. It was magnificent and it was doomed and there was no one who could have told him so.

Tom and Daisy came to one of Gatsby's parties, and the evening was a disaster of the most subtle kind. Tom prowled the grounds with the suspicious vigilance of a dog in unfamiliar territory, and Daisy was appalled by the new money vulgarity of it all -- by the people and the noise and the theatrical quality of everything, by people who were nobody making familiar conversation with people who were somebody. Gatsby sensed her displeasure, and after they left he stood on his steps, bewildered and despondent, unable to understand that Daisy's world and his could never be reconciled, that the very lavishness he had constructed to win her was the thing that repelled her, because it announced, with every gilded surface and every overflowing glass, that he was not one of them and never would be.

And then came the day that shattered everything, a day of unbearable heat when the air itself seemed to have weight and the surfaces of things shimmered with a malicious intensity. Tom Buchanan, whose instincts, for all their crudeness, possessed a certain animal precision, had begun to sense that something was wrong. He had watched Gatsby's face at lunch, and he had seen there an expression of such naked devotion when Gatsby looked at Daisy that it required no interpretation. Tom suggested they all drive into the city, and the suggestion had the quality of a command, issued in the voice of a man who was accustomed to having his suggestions treated as commands, and in the confusion that followed, Gatsby ended up driving Tom's car with Daisy beside him, and Tom drove Gatsby's enormous yellow automobile with Jordan and me.

We stopped for gasoline at Wilson's garage, and there was George Wilson, a hollow-eyed, ashen man who told Tom he wanted to buy a car because he and Myrtle were going West. He had learned, somehow, about Myrtle's secret life, though he did not know whom it involved. The look on his face was the look of a man who has discovered that the foundation of his existence is rotten, and the look on Tom's face as we pulled away was the look of a man who has realized that two of the pillars of his world are crumbling simultaneously.

At the Plaza Hotel, in a stifling parlor with the windows open to admit nothing but more heat, the truth came spilling out with the gracelessness of things that have been suppressed too long. Tom confronted Gatsby with a directness that was almost admirable in its brutality. He asked Gatsby what kind of row he was trying to cause in Tom's house, and Gatsby, with the quiet intensity of a man who has rehearsed this moment a thousand times, told Tom that Daisy had never loved him. It was at this point that the dream began to die -- not with a dramatic collapse but with a slow and terrible deflation, like air escaping from something that had once been magnificent. Daisy could not say it. She tried, and the words faltered, and she admitted that she had loved Tom once, even if she had loved Gatsby more, and with that admission, the perfect architecture of Gatsby's dream developed a crack that could never be repaired. Tom, sensing his advantage, pressed on with the relentlessness of a man who has no imagination and therefore no mercy. He spoke of Gatsby's drugstores and his bootlegging and his association with Wolfshiem, and the information fell on Daisy like blows, and with each blow she retreated further into the safety of what she already knew and already had, which was Tom, which was money, which was the invulnerable certainty of her class.

Gatsby lost Daisy that afternoon, though he did not know it yet. They drove home separately -- Daisy with Gatsby in the yellow car, Tom with Jordan and me behind them -- and what happened next happened with the swiftness and the terrible logic of a tragedy that has been building since before the first page. As the yellow car came roaring through the valley of ashes, a woman ran out into the road. Myrtle Wilson had seen the yellow car coming and had rushed out to meet it, thinking it was Tom, thinking that Tom was her salvation and her escape, and the car struck her and tore her open and left her kneeling in the road with her life pouring out of her and her mouth wide open and her left breast hanging loose like a flap, and by the time anyone reached her she was dead. Gatsby told me afterward that Daisy had been driving. She had been too frightened to stop, and he had reached for the wheel but it was too late, and now he was going to say he was driving, because that was the sort of man Gatsby was -- a man whose capacity for devotion extended even to the absorption of guilt that was not his own.

I found Gatsby standing in the shadow of the Buchanans' house late that night, keeping a vigil beneath Daisy's window with the patient devotion of a medieval knight, watching for a signal that she was safe, that Tom had not hurt her, that there was still something left to salvage from the wreckage of the afternoon. But inside the house, through the pantry window, I could see Tom and Daisy sitting together at the kitchen table with a plate of cold fried chicken and two bottles of ale between them, and there was an unmistakable air of natural intimacy about the scene, and they were not happy, exactly, but they were together, and they were conspiring -- conspiring with the casual efficiency of people who have always had the means to extricate themselves from the consequences of their own actions.

I told Gatsby to go home, that nothing was going to happen that night, and he went, reluctantly, looking back at the house with the expression of a man who has given everything for a thing that was never really there.

I went back to Gatsby's house that night, and we sat together until dawn, and he told me the story of his youth -- how he had met Daisy in Louisville, how he had let her believe he was a person of wealth and standing, how they had driven through the autumn streets in her white car and he had felt the sidewalk beneath his feet give way to the stars. He told me how he had kissed her one October night, and as his lips touched hers she blossomed for him like a flower, and the incarnation was complete. He knew that when he kissed this girl, and forever wed his unutterable visions to her perishable breath, his mind would never romp again like the mind of God. So he waited, listening for a moment longer to the tuning-fork that had been struck upon a star. Then he kissed her. At his lips' touch she blossomed for him like a flower and the incarnation was complete. There was a quality of confession in his voice that night, as though he were unburdening himself of a secret he had carried for so long that it had become indistinguishable from his identity.

The next day was the day Gatsby died. He had not used his swimming pool all summer, and his chauffeur had instructions to drain it, but Gatsby told the man to wait -- he would go for a swim. I think he was waiting for a telephone call from Daisy, waiting for the phone to ring with the news that would make everything right again, that would restore the dream to its former magnificence. But the phone did not ring, or rather it did, but not with Daisy's voice. George Wilson, half-mad with grief and convinced by Tom Buchanan that Gatsby had been driving the car that killed Myrtle, had come to West Egg with a revolver. He found Gatsby lying on an air mattress in the pool, and he shot him, and then he turned the revolver on himself, and when Gatsby's chauffeur heard the shots and ran to the pool, there was a thin red circle of blood in the water and the laden mattress moved irregularly down the pool, and the dream was over.

In the aftermath I tried to do what seemed right. I tried to gather people for Gatsby's funeral, to assemble some semblance of mourning for a man who had entertained thousands, but no one came. The telephone rang constantly in those days, but the voices on the other end were not voices of condolence -- they were voices asking for Gatsby, unaware that he was dead, voices from the shadow-world of his business dealings that had no interest in his death because they had never had any interest in his life. Wolfshiem sent a letter but would not come. The party guests, those hundreds of glittering people who had drunk his champagne and danced on his lawns, vanished as completely as though they had never existed. The only people who attended Jay Gatsby's funeral were myself, a handful of servants, the postman from West Egg, Gatsby's father -- a solemn old man bundled in a long cheap ulster against the warm September rain -- and a man I knew only as Owl Eyes, a stout, middle-aged man with enormous owl-eyed spectacles whom I had met once in Gatsby's library and who had been impressed, genuinely and deeply impressed, that the books on the shelves were real.

Gatsby's father showed me a schedule that young Jimmy had written on the flyleaf of a book when he was a boy -- a daily schedule of self-improvement that included exercise, the study of electricity, work, sport, elocution, and the practice of poise. It was a document so earnest and so heartbreaking in its optimism that it seemed to contain the entire American faith in the possibility of transformation, the belief that a man might, through discipline and will, become the thing he imagined himself to be. And Jay Gatsby had believed it. He had believed it with a purity that was magnificent and a completeness that was insane.

Before I left the East I went to see Tom Buchanan one last time. He was sitting in his house with the casual authority of a man who has never doubted his right to anything, and when I asked him about Wilson, he told me without remorse that he had told Wilson that Gatsby owned the car. He said it with the air of a man who believes he has performed a public service. They were careless people, Tom and Daisy -- they smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money or their vast carelessness, or whatever it was that kept them together, and let other people clean up the mess they had made.

I went back to the West after that, because the East was haunted for me, distorted beyond my eyes' power of correction. I think of Gatsby most on autumn nights, when the air has a quality of elegy about it and the dark fields of the republic roll on under the night. I think of his extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person and which it is not likely I shall ever find again. He had come so far from the poor boy in North Dakota, and the green light at the end of Daisy's dock must have seemed so close that he could hardly fail to grasp it. He did not know that it was already behind him, somewhere back in that vast obscurity beyond the city, where the dark fields of the republic rolled on under the night.

And I think of the Dutch sailors who came to this shore three hundred years ago, the old island that flowered once for Dutch sailors' eyes -- a fresh, green breast of the new world. Its vanished trees, the trees that had made way for Gatsby's house, had once pandered in whispers to the last and greatest of all human dreams; for a transitory enchanted moment man must have held his breath in the presence of this continent, compelled into an aesthetic contemplation he neither understood nor desired, face to face for the last time in history with something commensurate to his capacity for wonder. And sitting there brooding on the old, unknown world, I thought of Gatsby's wonder when he first picked out the green light at the end of Daisy's dock. He had come a long way to this blue lawn, and his dream must have seemed so close that he could hardly fail to grasp it. He did not know that it was already behind him, somewhere back in that vast obscurity beyond the city, where the dark fields of the republic rolled on under the night.

Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us. It eluded us then, but that is no matter -- tomorrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther, and one fine morning -- and so we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past.
