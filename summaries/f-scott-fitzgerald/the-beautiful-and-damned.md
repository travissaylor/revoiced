In the year 1913, when the great fortunes of America still pulsed with the illusion of permanence, there lived in New York a young man named Anthony Patch who was, in every outward particular, the sort of person upon whom the gods had smiled with a carelessness bordering on negligence. He was twenty-five, handsome in the way that certain men of old families are handsome -- not with any vulgar exuberance of feature but with a kind of weary fineness, as if his face had been designed by someone who understood that true beauty must always carry within it some suggestion of its own extinction. He had graduated from Harvard, where he had accumulated not so much an education as an attitude, and he had come to New York to await, with a patience that was really only a refined species of laziness, the death of his grandfather Adam Patch, whose thirty millions stood between Anthony and the full expression of his own magnificent uselessness.

Anthony's parents had died when he was a boy -- his mother of a malady too fashionable to mention, his father in the bewildered aftermath of her passing -- and he had been raised by his grandfather, a man whose history was the very embodiment of American industry distilled to its bitterest moral essence. Adam Patch had made his fortune on Wall Street with the single-mindedness of a great general and had then, in old age, turned the ferocity of his acquisitive instinct toward the reform of the nation's morals. He was a prohibitionist, a crusader, a man who having gorged himself at the banquet of capitalism now wished to remove the tablecloth before anyone else could eat. Anthony regarded him with that particular blend of deference and contempt which is the natural attitude of youth toward wealth it has not earned but fully intends to spend.

The apartment on East Fifty-second Street where Anthony installed himself was a monument to the taste of a young man who had confused aesthetic discrimination with a philosophy of life. It was exquisite in its appointments -- the Japanese paintings, the Duncan Phyfe furniture, the well-considered arrangement of books that Anthony intended someday to read -- and it functioned as a kind of elaborate stage set upon which he performed, for an audience of one, the role of the cultivated gentleman. He had vague intentions of writing a book, something about the Middle Ages or perhaps the nature of aristocracy, a project that existed more comfortably as a topic of conversation than as an actual undertaking. The truth, which Anthony understood dimly and which the reader must understand clearly, was that he had no particular talent for anything except the appreciation of his own sensibility.

His two closest friends were Maury Noble and Richard Caramel, and together they formed one of those triangles of companionship that seem, in one's twenties, to be the very architecture of civilization. Maury was cynical in the manner of intelligent young men who have discovered that the world does not deserve their abilities -- a discovery that relieves one of the obligation to demonstrate those abilities in any practical fashion. He possessed a catlike face and a philosophy that ran toward an elegant nihilism, toward the conviction that all human endeavor was essentially absurd and that the only dignity available to man was the dignity of understanding this absurdity without flinching. Richard Caramel, whom everyone called Dick, was of a different temperament entirely: he was writing a novel, and with the peculiar optimism of those who have not yet published, he believed that this novel would establish him among the first rank of American writers. He was Gloria Gilbert's cousin, a fact that would prove, in the fullness of time, to be the single most consequential detail of his biography.

It was Dick who, in November of that year, introduced Anthony to Gloria. The circumstances were ordinary -- a dinner, a mutual acquaintance, the machinery of social life turning its familiar gears -- but the effect upon Anthony was something beyond the ordinary. Gloria Gilbert was twenty-two, a Kansas City girl who had come East with the unstudied assurance of a woman who has always been beautiful and who has therefore always been forgiven. Her beauty was not of the demure, retiring kind; it was assertive, almost aggressive, a beauty that entered a room several seconds before she did and that lingered, like expensive perfume, long after she had gone. She had yellow hair and a mouth that promised everything and guaranteed nothing, and she moved through the world with the serene conviction that her physical perfection entitled her to exemptions from the tedious rules that governed the lives of plainer women.

Anthony was captivated immediately and absolutely, in the way that only a man with nothing else to occupy his attention can be captivated by a woman. Gloria fascinated him not merely because she was beautiful but because her beauty seemed to validate something in his own nature -- his conviction that life should be lived as a series of exquisite sensations, that the purpose of existence was not to accomplish but to experience. She shared with him, or appeared to share, a horror of growing old, a passionate attachment to youth and all its privileges, and a bottomless indifference to the sort of practical considerations that engaged the energies of less decorative people. They talked about life in the grand abstract manner of young people who have not yet been required to live it in any particular way, and in their conversations they constructed a shared mythology in which beauty and intelligence were the only virtues and convention the only sin.

Their courtship unfolded through the winter and into the spring with the elaborate, self-conscious choreography of two people who are simultaneously falling in love and watching themselves fall in love. They met at teas and at dinners, at the theater and at those small, carefully curated gatherings where the young people of their set performed for one another the rituals of sophistication. Anthony telephoned her at hours that propriety would have forbidden had propriety held any authority over either of them. They exchanged the kind of witticisms that seem, in the moment, to constitute a private language, a code that only two people in all the world can decipher, though in truth these clever remarks were indistinguishable from the clever remarks exchanged by every pair of clever young lovers since the invention of cleverness. There were evenings when they sat together in her apartment on Fifty-seventh Street, talking until the small hours about everything and nothing -- about books they had read and books they intended to read, about people they admired and people they despised, about the nature of beauty and the meaning of courage and the impossibility of finding, in this vulgar and mercantile age, anything worth believing in. They agreed, with a fervor that felt like discovery but was really only the pleasure of hearing one's own opinions repeated in a more attractive voice, that the world was a disappointing place and that they were superior to it.

There was, however, another suitor -- Joseph Bloeckman, a man of the motion picture industry, who had been circling Gloria with the persistent, unsubtle devotion of a man who understands that his money is more impressive than his person. Bloeckman was rising in the world of cinema with the determination of a self-made man, and he saw in Gloria the leading lady of his private drama: a beautiful wife who would complete the picture of success he was so industriously composing. Gloria did not love him, but she did not entirely dismiss him either, maintaining that comfortable ambiguity which beautiful women cultivate as instinctively as they cultivate their complexions.

But it was Anthony whom she chose, or rather it was Anthony who, in proposing, provided her with the occasion to choose. He proposed with the desperate eloquence of a man who senses that the thing he desires most is also the thing most likely to destroy him, though this intimation surfaced only as a vague unease, a tremor at the periphery of his happiness, easily mistaken for the ordinary nervousness of a man in love. Gloria accepted immediately, casting off Bloeckman with the cheerful brutality that the beautiful reserve for those who bore them, and the engagement was sealed.

Anthony experienced, in the weeks before the wedding, certain misgivings of a financial character. His income, derived from the bonds and securities that constituted his inheritance-in-waiting, was perhaps six or seven thousand a year -- a sum that seemed generous in the abstract but that shrank alarmingly when measured against Gloria's appetites and the kind of life they both considered the minimum requirement of civilized existence. He performed a few calculations, found the results discouraging, and then did what he would do with increasing frequency as the years passed: he dismissed the problem, trusting to luck, to the eventual inheritance, to some unspecified miracle of fortune that would rescue him from the consequences of his own inaction.

They were married in the spring of 1914, and the early months of their marriage possessed that quality of incandescent happiness which is really nothing more than the absence of unhappiness -- a distinction that would become clearer as time went on. Their honeymoon revealed the first fractures. Gloria, it developed, had no interest whatsoever in domestic life; she could not cook, would not clean, and regarded the maintenance of a household as an imposition upon her dignity. Anthony, for his part, drove an automobile with the excessive caution of a man whose anxieties extended to all mechanical devices, and Gloria found this maddening. These were small things, irritations of the sort that every marriage produces, but they were also prophecies -- tiny rehearsals for the larger failures that lay ahead.

They returned to New York and took up the business of being young and married and beautiful in a city that existed, or seemed to exist, for the sole purpose of entertaining people exactly like themselves. They gave parties and went to parties. They drank cocktails before dinner and champagne during it and whiskey afterward. They stayed up until four in the morning and slept until noon. They surrounded themselves with a shifting constellation of bright, brittle people -- men who wrote or painted or composed or who, like Anthony, intended to do these things at some indefinite future date; women who were beautiful or witty or scandalous, and who were valued in proportion to their ability to generate the kind of gossip that made a dinner party worth attending. It was a life of perfect surfaces, and beneath those surfaces there was nothing at all.

There were servants to hire and dismiss, tradesmen to negotiate with, the thousand small indignities of domestic economy that Gloria found intolerable and that Anthony found beneath him. They ate in restaurants more often than not, spending money they could not quite afford on meals they did not quite enjoy, because the alternative -- a home-cooked dinner, a quiet evening, the acknowledgment that they were, after all, just two people living together in an apartment -- seemed a concession to the ordinary that neither of them was prepared to make.

Gloria demanded a house in the country, and Anthony, whose method of dealing with Gloria's demands was to capitulate to them as quickly as possible, rented a gray house in Marietta, in Connecticut, a place of faded charm and sufficient acreage to provide the illusion of pastoral retreat. The gray house became the theater for their summer entertainments -- weekend parties that began on Friday evening and dissolved, in varying states of dishevelment, sometime on Sunday afternoon. The liquor flowed with a generosity that was really a kind of desperation, as if sobriety might permit certain truths to surface that were better left submerged. Anthony and Gloria presided over these gatherings with the effortless authority of people who have nothing else to do, and for a time the gray house in Marietta seemed to be the very center of the universe, or at least that small, privileged corner of the universe in which they had agreed to live.

Meanwhile, the world beyond their charmed circle was changing. Dick Caramel published his novel, *The Demon Lover*, and it was a success -- not merely a critical success, which would have been tolerable, but a popular success, which meant money and attention and the kind of recognition that Anthony had always believed should come to him as a birthright rather than as a reward for effort. Maury Noble, too, was making his way in the world, applying his considerable intelligence to practical matters with a competence that seemed, to Anthony, a kind of betrayal. The friends of one's twenties, who had seemed so permanent, so essential, began to drift away, carried by the currents of ambition and responsibility into waters where Anthony could not follow.

It was during one of the parties at the gray house that disaster arrived, though it came in the guise of something as ordinary as an unexpected guest. Adam Patch, driven by some reforming impulse or perhaps by the simple curiosity of an old man who suspects he is being taken advantage of, appeared unannounced at the Marietta house on an evening when the revelries were at their most abandoned. What he found there -- the drinking, the noise, the spectacle of his grandson and his grandson's wife presiding over what could only be described as a bacchanal -- confirmed every suspicion he had ever harbored about the moral deficiencies of the younger generation. He departed without a word, but the word came soon enough: Anthony was disinherited. When Adam Patch died shortly thereafter, his fortune of thirty million dollars passed not to Anthony but to Edward Shuttleworth, his secretary and the instrument of his final, punitive morality.

The disinheritance fell upon Anthony and Gloria with the force of a physical blow. They had built their lives upon the expectation of that inheritance as surely as a house is built upon its foundation, and now the foundation had been pulled away, leaving them suspended, for a precarious moment, in midair before the inevitable fall. They began to liquidate their bonds, to sell the securities that had constituted their modest income, and for a time they maintained the illusion of their former life, like a man who has been fatally wounded but does not yet know it. They moved through the same rooms, attended the same parties, drank the same cocktails, but a new note had entered their existence -- a note of anxiety, of recrimination, of the particular bitterness that comes from watching a beautiful thing decay.

Their marriage, which had never been tested by anything more serious than Gloria's refusal to learn to cook, now became the arena for a kind of warfare that was all the more vicious for being conducted in the language of love. They quarreled about money, about the future, about whose fault it was that they found themselves in this predicament. Gloria blamed Anthony for his passivity, his failure to find employment, his contentment to drift while their resources dwindled. Anthony blamed Gloria for her extravagance, her vanity, her refusal to economize. They were both right, of course, which made the quarrels all the more unbearable, for there is nothing so corrosive to a marriage as the mutual recognition of shared failure.

Anthony attempted, with the half-heartedness of a man who has never been required to try, to find some means of earning a living. He applied for positions with a vagueness that guaranteed rejection. He attempted to write, producing a few stories that were returned by editors with a politeness that stung worse than outright contempt. He was, at thirty, a man without skills, without connections, without the fundamental capacity for effort that might have compensated for these deficiencies. The world, which had seemed so welcoming when he was young and rich and handsome, now revealed itself as a place of terrible indifference, a place where a man without money or talent was simply invisible.

It was at this juncture that the war intervened, and Anthony, who had never felt any particular obligation to his country or to anyone else, found himself drafted into the army. He was sent to a training camp in the South, and there, separated from Gloria for the first time since their marriage, he experienced something unexpected: relief. The structure of military life, with its schedules and its demands and its absolute prohibition against the kind of brooding self-examination that had been his primary occupation for the past decade, suited him in ways he had not anticipated. He did not become a good soldier -- he was too old for that, too accustomed to the prerogatives of his class -- but he became, at least, a functioning one, and in the anonymity of the camp he found a temporary reprieve from the person he had become.

It was in the South that he met Dorothy Raycroft, a young woman of no particular distinction whose chief attraction was that she was not Gloria. Dorothy was uncomplicated in the way that people who have never expected very much from life are uncomplicated, and Anthony, who had spent years in the company of a woman whose complications were legendary, found in this simplicity a kind of rest. Their affair was conducted with the furtive, ashamed energy of a man who knows he is doing something contemptible but who lacks the strength to stop. He did not love Dorothy -- he was not, by this point, capable of loving anyone in any meaningful sense -- but he needed her, which in the economy of human relationships sometimes amounts to the same thing.

Gloria, alone in New York, pursued her own form of escape. She had always harbored a secret belief that she was destined for the screen -- that her beauty, which had been her passport through life, might also be her salvation -- and she arranged, through the persistent Bloeckman, now risen to a position of considerable influence in the film industry and styling himself Black, a screen test. The result was devastating. The camera, that most democratic of arbiters, rendered its verdict with brutal clarity: Gloria, at twenty-nine, was too old. The lines that a generous mirror might still conceal were merciless under the studio lights, and the part she had imagined for herself -- the ingenue, the beautiful young heroine -- went to a younger woman. Gloria received this news with a dignity that was, in its way, more terrible than hysteria. She understood, with the finality of a mathematical proof, that her beauty -- the single asset upon which she had staked her entire existence -- was a wasting resource, and that she had already spent the greater part of it.

Anthony returned from the war without having seen combat, and the reunion with Gloria was marked by a passion that was really only the residue of habit. They fell back into their old patterns, their old quarrels, their old mutual dissatisfaction, with the weary familiarity of actors who have been performing the same play for too many seasons. The money continued to dwindle. Anthony's drinking, which had always been considerable, now became something more -- an occupation, a vocation, the single activity to which he applied himself with genuine dedication. He drank in the morning and in the afternoon and in the evening. He drank at home and in speakeasies and in the apartments of people whose names he could not always remember. The alcohol did not make him happy, but it made him less acutely aware of his unhappiness, and in this diminished state he found something that resembled, if not peace, at least the absence of the most intolerable forms of anguish.

They moved from apartment to apartment, each one a little smaller, a little less distinguished, a little further from the neighborhood where they had once lived as a matter of course. The furniture that had seemed so essential -- the mahogany tables, the silk curtains, the objets d'art that Gloria had accumulated with the discriminating eye of a woman who believed that beauty in one's surroundings could compensate for the absence of beauty in one's circumstances -- was sold off piece by piece, each sale a small funeral for the life they had imagined. They began to borrow money, first from friends and then from acquaintances and finally from people who were neither, and these debts accumulated with the silent, inexorable patience of a rising tide.

Their salvation, if it could be called that, lay in the lawsuit. Anthony had engaged lawyers to contest his grandfather's will, and the case wound its way through the courts with the excruciating slowness that is the particular genius of the American legal system. The lawsuit became the center of their existence, the thing around which all their hopes and fears revolved. It was their religion, their lottery ticket, their last remaining connection to the future they had once imagined for themselves. They spoke of it constantly -- when they would win, what they would do with the money, how their lives would be transformed -- and these conversations had the quality of incantations, as if the mere repetition of their desires might be sufficient to bring them into being.

There were nights when they sat across from each other in whatever apartment they currently occupied and stared at one another with the bewildered hostility of two strangers who have been locked in the same room by mistake. There were other nights, rarer and more painful, when some echo of their former tenderness surfaced -- a word, a gesture, a sudden memory of the people they had been -- and they clung to each other with a desperation that was closer to grief than to love. In these moments they were not husband and wife but fellow survivors, two people adrift on the same piece of wreckage, each aware that the other's weight was pulling them both under but unable to let go.

The years of waiting took their toll. Anthony, who had been handsome, became bloated and vague. Gloria, who had been beautiful, became thin and anxious. Their circle of friends, which had once been so bright and so various, contracted to a few loyal souls and a larger number of hangers-on who sensed, with the infallible instinct of social parasites, that Anthony and Gloria were descending to a level where they might soon be available for exploitation. Dick Caramel, whose early promise had curdled into the routine production of commercial fiction, remained loyal in his fashion, though his visits grew less frequent and his manner, when he came, carried the unmistakable taint of pity. Maury Noble had moved on entirely, into a world of business and respectability that Anthony regarded with the special contempt of a man who has failed at everything else.

Anthony's deterioration accelerated. He took to wandering the streets at night, visiting bars of diminishing quality, engaging in conversations with strangers who recognized in him the particular desperation of a man at the end of his rope. He tried, briefly, to sell bonds -- an occupation so ill-suited to his temperament that the attempt would have been comic had it not been so pathetic. He was fired, naturally, and the firing confirmed what he had always suspected: that the world of work was a conspiracy designed to humiliate people of his sensibility. He returned to the apartment, to Gloria, to the bottle, and to the interminable wait for the lawsuit to resolve itself.

One evening, after Gloria had spoken with a theatrical exhaustion about moving to Italy, about simply disappearing, about dying, Anthony suggested they borrow money. The conversation turned, as conversations between them always seemed to turn, toward Bloeckman, and when Gloria mentioned that it was Bloeckman -- Black, as he now called himself -- who had arranged her disastrous screen test, something in Anthony broke loose. He went out into the night, already drunk, and found his way to a club where Bloeckman was dining. What followed was less a fight than a demonstration of Anthony's complete collapse -- he struck Bloeckman, or tried to, and was thrown into the street by the staff, landing on the pavement with the graceless finality of a man who has exhausted every form of dignity available to him. He walked home through streets that seemed to tilt and swim, feeling not intoxicated but frenzied, as though some mechanism inside him had slipped its gears and was now spinning freely, disconnected from any useful purpose.

The end, when it came, arrived with the peculiar anticlimactic quality that characterizes most of life's decisive moments. Three weeks after the incident with Bloeckman, the lawsuit reached its final verdict. Gloria and Dick went to hear the announcement while Anthony remained at home -- not from indifference but from a superstitious terror that his presence might somehow jinx the outcome. He sat in his bathroom, that sanctuary of old, surrounded by the stamp collection he had kept since boyhood, turning the pages with the methodical care of a man who has retreated so far into himself that the outside world has become no more than a rumor. It was there that Dorothy Raycroft appeared -- Dorothy, whom he had not seen in years, who materialized in his doorway like a ghost from a life he could barely remember. Anthony, disoriented and wild, seized a chair and tried to hurl it at her, and then he collapsed, unconscious, among his stamps.

Gloria and Dick returned with the news: they had won. The will had been overturned. The thirty million dollars, or what remained of it after the lawyers and the years had taken their portion, was theirs. They found Anthony on the bathroom floor, insensible, surrounded by the small colored rectangles of his childhood collection, and they lifted him up and told him the news, and whether he understood it or not was difficult to say.

The final scene takes place aboard a ship -- an ocean liner bound for Europe, that traditional destination of Americans who have either achieved everything or lost everything and who can no longer tell the difference. Anthony sits in a deck chair, wrapped in blankets, attended by Gloria and by a nurse, and he is observed by the other passengers with that mixture of curiosity and embarrassment with which the healthy regard the ruined. He has won his lawsuit. He has his thirty million dollars. He has the fortune for which he waited and schemed and suffered and drank and raged and collapsed. And he sits on the deck of the ship, a man of thirty-three who looks like a man of fifty, his mind clouded, his body broken, his victory as hollow as the life that preceded it.

It was reported that Shuttleworth, the secretary, had taken his own life upon learning that the will had been overturned, and this detail, which might once have troubled Anthony's conscience, now passed through him like light through glass, leaving no impression whatsoever. He murmured something to himself -- the other passengers could not quite make it out -- something about having shown them, about it being a hard fight but having won in the end. And perhaps he had won, in the narrow, technical sense that he now possessed the money he had always believed was rightfully his. But the man who sat in that deck chair bore so little resemblance to the elegant, languid young man who had once waited for his inheritance in an exquisite apartment on East Fifty-second Street that the victory, such as it was, belonged to someone who no longer existed.

This, then, was the story of Anthony Patch and Gloria Gilbert -- of two people who were beautiful and who were damned, though whether the damnation was a consequence of the beauty or the beauty a premonition of the damnation it was impossible, finally, to say. They had believed, with the conviction that only the young and the privileged can muster, that life owed them something -- that their beauty, their charm, their position in the world entitled them to a happiness that would arrive without effort, like a package delivered to the door. They had waited for this happiness with the patience of saints and the industry of sluggards, and when it failed to arrive they had not sought it elsewhere but had instead turned inward, upon each other, with a destructive fury that consumed everything it touched.

The money, which was to have been the instrument of their liberation, became instead the occasion of their ruin. Not the money itself, for it arrived too late to matter, but the expectation of the money -- the promise that hung over their lives like a golden cloud, obscuring the landscape beneath, making every present sacrifice seem temporary and every present failure seem provisional. They could not work because they were going to be rich. They could not change because they were going to be rescued. They could not grow because growth requires the acknowledgment that one's present condition is permanent, and Anthony and Gloria lived always in the future tense, in the subjunctive mood, in a world of what would be rather than what was.

And so the years passed, and the beauty faded, and the money came, and it meant nothing. Gloria, who had been the most beautiful woman in any room she entered, became a woman whose beauty was a memory -- not a living, breathing fact but a story that others told, a legend that she herself could no longer quite verify in the mirror. Anthony, who had been a young man of promise and refinement, became a drunk, a failure, a cautionary tale that his former friends recounted with a mixture of sympathy and satisfaction. They had been given everything -- youth, beauty, money, social position, the freedom to make of their lives whatever they wished -- and they had made of their lives nothing at all.

There is a particular cruelty in stories like these, a cruelty that resides not in any external catastrophe but in the slow, relentless working-out of character. Anthony was not destroyed by a villain or by fate or by the cruelty of the world; he was destroyed by himself, by the fatal deficiency at the center of his nature -- the inability to act, to strive, to engage with life on any terms other than his own. Gloria was not ruined by age or by the loss of her beauty; she was ruined by the belief that beauty was enough, that a woman who was sufficiently lovely need not trouble herself with the development of any other quality. They were, both of them, incomplete people who had mistaken their incompleteness for a kind of superiority, and the world, which is indifferent to such distinctions, had treated them accordingly.

The gray house in Marietta stood empty now, its windows dark, its gardens overgrown, a monument to summers that would not come again. The apartment on Fifty-second Street had been given up, and the apartment that replaced it, and the one after that, each one smaller and shabbier than the last, like a series of Russian dolls opened in reverse. The friends had scattered. The parties were over. The bright, frantic, gin-soaked world of the early twenties, which had seemed so permanent and so glittering, had revealed itself as what it had always been -- a fever dream, a collective hallucination, a gorgeous, unsustainable lie.

And on the deck of the ocean liner, bound for a Europe that would not save him, Anthony Patch sat in his wheelchair and clutched his thirty million dollars and looked out at the gray Atlantic with eyes that saw nothing, and he whispered to himself that he had won. He had shown them all. It had been a hard fight, but he had won in the end.

This was his victory. This was what it looked like. The beautiful, at last, and the damned.
