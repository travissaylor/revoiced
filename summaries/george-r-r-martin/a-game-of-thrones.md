The morning that changed the world dawned cold and grey, as mornings so often do in the north. Beyond the Wall, where the trees grow wild and ancient and the snows never truly melt, three men of the Night's Watch rode out ranging and found what no man of the Watch had seen in eight thousand years. The wildlings they had been tracking were dead, every one of them, torn and savaged and arranged with a terrible deliberateness that spoke of something more than mere killing. Ser Waymar Royce, young and proud and hungry for glory as only a third son of a noble house can be, drew his sword and met the Other that came for him out of the darkness between the trees. The creature's blade was translucent, pale as milkglass, and it shattered Waymar's steel as if it were no more than ice. When Will crept back to look upon the body of his fallen brother, the dead man's eyes opened blue as frozen stars, and cold black fingers closed around his throat. Only Gared survived to flee south, and the Wall could not save him. Lord Eddard Stark found the deserter on a bright autumn morning in the north and took his head with Ice, the greatsword of his house, as the Starks of Winterfell had done for thousands of years. His son Bran, seven years old and watching his first execution, did not look away.

It was on the ride home from that grim duty that they found the direwolves. A dead mother, her throat torn by a broken antler, and five pups mewling in the snow. Jon Snow, Ned's bastard son, was the one who convinced his father to let the children keep them. There were five pups for five trueborn Stark children, he pointed out, and Lord Stark's sigil was the direwolf. It was an omen, though whether for good or ill none could say. Jon found the sixth pup later, the runt of the litter, white as the snow for which the bastard was named, with eyes as red as blood. Ghost, he would call it. The wolf never made a sound.

Word came to Winterfell not long after that the Hand of the King was dead. Jon Arryn, who had fostered Ned and Robert Baratheon both as boys, who had raised his banners in rebellion when the Mad King demanded their heads, who had served Robert faithfully for fourteen years upon the Iron Throne—Jon Arryn was dead, and the king was riding north to Winterfell with half the court in tow. Robert came with his queen, the golden Cersei of House Lannister, and her twin brother Ser Jaime, the Kingslayer, who had broken his most sacred vow when he drove his sword through the back of the king he was sworn to protect. He came with the queen's younger brother, the Imp, Tyrion Lannister, a dwarf with mismatched eyes and a sharp tongue and a mind as quick as any in the Seven Kingdoms. He came with Prince Joffrey, his supposed heir, a boy of twelve with his mother's golden curls and a cruel twist to his pretty lips. Robert himself was a ruin of what he had been, the great warrior king gone to fat and wine, but when he clasped Ned in his arms in the crypts beneath Winterfell, the old warmth was still there, and with it a desperate need. He wanted Ned beside him. He wanted Ned as his Hand, his right hand, his strong right arm.

And he wanted more than that. He wanted Sansa, Ned's eldest daughter, betrothed to Prince Joffrey. He wanted to join their houses with blood and marriage, as he and Ned's sister Lyanna might have been joined, had Rhaegar Targaryen not stolen her away and started the war that put Robert on the throne. Lyanna had died in a room that smelled of blood and roses, and Robert had never stopped loving her, though she was fifteen years in her grave.

Ned would have refused. He was a man of the north, carved from ice and honor, and the south was a nest of vipers. But a letter came in the night, sealed with the falcon of House Arryn, and it told a tale of murder. Lysa Arryn, Jon's widow and Catelyn Stark's own sister, wrote that the Lannisters had killed her husband. That the queen was not to be trusted. That danger was everywhere, and the king himself might be next. For the sake of the realm, for Robert, for the memory of the man who had raised them both, Ned could not refuse. He rode south to King's Landing with his daughters Sansa and Arya, and behind him the gates of Winterfell closed like the jaws of a trap.

He left behind a broken son. Bran, the boy who loved to climb, who scrambled up the walls of Winterfell as surely as a squirrel climbs an oak, had been exploring the broken tower when he found what he was never meant to find: the queen and her brother, golden twins tangled together in the oldest of sins. Jaime Lannister looked at the boy and said, "The things I do for love," and pushed him from the window. The fall should have killed him. Instead it broke his back and left him sleeping, and when he woke he could not walk and could not remember what he had seen or why he had fallen. A catspaw came for him in the night with a Valyrian steel dagger, to finish what the fall had not, and it was Bran's direwolf Summer who saved him, tearing out the assassin's throat while Catelyn Stark held the blade bare-handed, her fingers slick with her own blood.

Catelyn rode south in secret to warn Ned, taking ship from White Harbor with Ser Rodrik Cassel at her side. In King's Landing she was met by Littlefinger—Lord Petyr Baelish, Master of Coin, a man who had loved her since boyhood with an obsession that bordered on madness. It was Littlefinger who told her the dagger belonged to Tyrion Lannister, and Catelyn believed him, because she had known Petyr since they were children and trusted him as she trusted few others. It was the worst mistake she would ever make.

On the kingsroad home, Catelyn crossed paths with the Imp at the crossroads inn and, in a moment of rash courage that would set the realm ablaze, arrested him before a room full of witnesses and carried him to the Eyrie, the mountain stronghold of House Arryn. There her sister Lysa waited, and Lysa was not the girl Catelyn remembered. Grief and paranoia had curdled her, and she kept her sickly son Robin at her breast though the boy was six years old, and she saw Lannister knives in every shadow. She threw Tyrion into the sky cells, those doorless chambers that opened onto the empty air of the mountainside, where men went mad from the wind and the void and the slow, creeping certainty that they were going to fall.

But Tyrion Lannister was no common prisoner. He was sharp where others were strong, cunning where they were brave, and he knew the law as well as any maester. He demanded trial by combat, as was his right, and when no knight would champion a Lannister in the Eyrie, a sellsword named Bronn stepped forward. Bronn was lean and hard and practical, a man who killed without flourish or honor, and he danced around the ponderous Ser Vardis Egen until the knight was gasping and stumbling, then opened his throat and sent him tumbling through the Moon Door to shatter on the stones six hundred feet below. "A Lannister always pays his debts," Tyrion told the gaoler Mord as he tossed him a purse of gold, and he walked out of the Eyrie a free man, though the mountains of the Vale were no safe place for a dwarf with a price on his head.

The arrest of Tyrion lit a fire that would consume the Seven Kingdoms. Tywin Lannister, the richest and most ruthless lord in the realm, loosed his mad dog Ser Gregor Clegane upon the Riverlands, burning and raping and slaughtering smallfolk by the hundreds, and when Ned Stark sat the Iron Throne in Robert's absence and sent Lord Beric Dondarrion to bring the Mountain to justice, he drew the lines of a war that no one yet called a war. In the streets of King's Landing, Jaime Lannister confronted Ned with swords drawn and men at his back, and Ned's household guard died in the gutter, Jory Cassel among them, the captain who had served the Starks since before Ned's children were born. A horse fell on Ned's leg and broke it, and he woke in the Red Keep with Robert standing over him and the realm fracturing beneath their feet.

Ned should have left then. Every instinct, every cold northern sensibility should have driven him home to Winterfell. But he had made a promise to a dying man—or perhaps to a dead woman, long ago in a tower wreathed in blue winter roses—and he could not let the truth he had found go unspoken. Jon Arryn had died for that truth. The old Hand had been reading a ponderous book, The Lineages and Histories of the Great Houses of the Seven Kingdoms, and tracing Robert's bastards through the streets and brothels of King's Landing. Ned followed the same trail. He found Gendry, a smith's apprentice with Robert's black hair and blue eyes. He found Barra, an infant girl in a brothel, suckling at her mother's breast. And he found the truth that Jon Arryn had found, the truth for which Jon Arryn had been murdered: the seed is strong. Every Baratheon who had ever wed a Lannister had produced children with black hair and blue eyes, as the Baratheon blood ran thick and true. But Joffrey, Myrcella, and Tommen—the three golden children who sat in the line of succession to the Iron Throne—had golden hair and green eyes, all three. They were not Robert's children. They were Jaime's. The queen had been coupling with her brother, and every one of her children was born of incest, and the heir to the Iron Throne was a bastard with no more right to the crown than the rats in the walls.

Ned told Cersei what he knew. He gave her a chance to flee with her children before he told Robert. It was the honorable thing to do, and it was the act that killed him. Cersei did not flee. She plotted. Robert went hunting in the kingswood, and his squire, young Lancel Lannister, kept the king's wineskin full of strongwine until Robert was too drunk to see straight, and the boar that gored him drove its tusks through his belly and left him dying by inches. Robert named Ned Lord Protector of the Realm and regent until Joffrey came of age, and he died not knowing that the boy he called son was no son of his at all.

What followed was a game of thrones played at knife's edge, and Ned Stark lost. He might have taken the city with the gold cloaks, the City Watch of King's Landing, had Littlefinger not betrayed him. Ned had trusted the man who loved his wife, and Petyr Baelish put a knife to his throat and said, "I did warn you not to trust me." The gold cloaks turned their cloaks, as men of no true loyalty so often do, and Ned's household was butchered in the throne room. Sansa was taken hostage. Arya's dancing master, Syrio Forel of Braavos, the former First Sword to the Sealord, held off Lannister guardsmen with a wooden practice sword while Arya fled into the bowels of the Red Keep, and from there into the streets of the city, where she lived like a ragged shadow among the orphans and beggars.

They stripped Ned of his titles and threw him in a black cell beneath the Red Keep, where the darkness was absolute and the silence was worse. The realm believed what Cersei told them to believe: that Ned Stark was a traitor who had tried to steal the throne. Varys, the eunuch spymaster with his powdered cheeks and his soft slippers, came to Ned in the dark and told him to confess, to take the black and join the Night's Watch, to save his daughters if not his pride. For Sansa's sake, for Arya's, for the sake of a family scattered to the winds, Ned did what he had never done in his life. He stood before the crowd at the Great Sept of Baelor and he lied. He called himself a traitor. He named Joffrey the rightful king. He abandoned his honor, the only thing he had ever truly possessed, and he did it so that his children might live.

Joffrey promised mercy. Cersei and the council had arranged for Ned to be sent to the Wall. But Joffrey was his mother's son and his father's son too, in ways that Cersei had not foreseen, and the cruelty that ran in the blood of the boy born of incest could not be leashed by promises or politics. "Ser Ilyn, bring me his head," the boy king said, and Ser Ilyn Payne drew Ice—Ned's own greatsword, the blade of House Stark—and in a single stroke, Lord Eddard Stark of Winterfell lost his head on the steps of the Great Sept while his daughter Sansa screamed and the crowd roared and a girl dressed as a boy named Arry clung to a statue and could not look away.

Sansa fainted. When she woke, her father was dead and she was alone in a nest of lions. Joffrey took her to the walls and showed her the heads mounted on spikes—her father's head, Septa Mordane's head, the heads of all the men who had served and protected her—and he made her look at them. He promised that one day he would give her Robb's head too. Sansa looked at the boy she had once dreamed of marrying, this golden prince who was no prince at all, and for one wild moment she thought of pushing him from the parapet. It was the Hound, Sandor Clegane, Joffrey's sworn shield and a man whose face was half-melted by fire, who stopped her—not from violence, but from the king's notice—and gave her a rough cloth to wipe the blood from her lip where Ser Meryn had struck her. "Save yourself some pain, girl," the Hound told her. "Give him what he wants."

Far to the north, Bran dreamed. He dreamed of wolves and ravens and a three-eyed crow that called to him from the darkness. He dreamed of falling and flying. He woke to a world diminished, a lord of Winterfell at seven years old while his mother was gone and his father was gone and Robb was marching to war. The wildling woman Osha came to Winterfell as a captive and spoke of darker things—the Others stirring beyond the Wall, the dead walking, the long night returning. No one listened. They were all too busy with their wars.

Robb Stark rode south with the banners of the north behind him, a boy of fifteen commanding an army because there was no one else. His mother met him on the march, and together they reached the Twins, the great bridge-castle of House Frey, where the ancient Lord Walder Frey sat surrounded by sons and grandsons and great-grandsons, a sour, grasping, petty lord who had spent his long life being mocked as the Late Lord Frey for arriving at Robert's Rebellion only after the battle was won. Walder Frey held the only crossing of the Green Fork for miles, and his price was steep: Robb must wed one of his daughters, Arya must wed one of his sons, and a Frey must be fostered at Winterfell. Catelyn negotiated the terms with the old lord while Robb's army waited, and the terms were agreed, and the army crossed.

What followed was a campaign of startling audacity for a boy who had never seen war. Robb split his forces, sending the greater part under Lord Roose Bolton's command to meet Tywin Lannister's army at the Green Fork while he led his cavalry west through the Whispering Wood to spring a trap on Jaime Lannister's host besieging Riverrun. Bolton's men were mauled, as Robb intended, buying time with blood while the real blow fell in the west. In the dark of the Whispering Wood, Robb's northmen fell on Jaime's outriders and shattered them, and when the Kingslayer himself rode out to break the trap, Grey Wind—Robb's direwolf, grown huge and terrible—pulled armored men from their horses while the Young Wolf's cavalry closed in from every side. Jaime killed ten men trying to cut his way to Robb, they said afterward, but in the end he was pulled from his horse and put in chains, the greatest knight of the Kingsguard brought low by a boy of fifteen. The Stark banners flew over Riverrun, and the river lords swore their swords to Robb, and in the great hall of the castle the lords of the north and the Trident raised their voices and their steel and named him King in the North. The old title, the title of the Kings of Winter who had ruled from Winterfell for eight thousand years before Aegon the Conqueror came with his dragons. Winter is coming, the Starks had always said, and now winter had come south with a crown on its head.

On the Wall, Jon Snow fought a different war. He had come to the Night's Watch expecting honor and purpose and found instead a crumbling ruin manned by thieves and rapists and broken men, the dregs of the Seven Kingdoms sent to guard a frontier that the realm had largely forgotten. The Wall itself was vast—seven hundred feet of ice, stretching from the mountains to the sea—but the men who garrisoned it were few and growing fewer, and the enemy beyond it was stirring in ways that no living man could fully comprehend. Jon's uncle Benjen had gone ranging beyond the Wall and never returned, and when the bodies of two of his companions were found, they seemed merely dead—until the night they rose with bright blue eyes and cold black hands and had to be destroyed with fire.

Jon might have fled south when word came of his father's arrest. He nearly did. He rode out in the night, deserting his vow, and it was his friends—Samwell Tarly, the craven fat boy who was braver than he knew, and Pyp and Grenn, lads from the gutters and the farms—who rode after him and brought him back. The Night's Watch took no part in the wars of the realm. That was the oath. That was the ancient duty. Lord Commander Mormont, the Old Bear, knew what Jon was and what he might become, and he gave the young man Longclaw, a Valyrian steel bastard sword, and told him to remember his vows. When the Old Bear announced that the Watch would ride in force beyond the Wall to find Benjen Stark, to learn the truth of the wildling king Mance Rayder who was gathering the free folk in the Frostfangs, and to discover what power was raising the dead, Jon Snow rode with them, and the Wall disappeared behind him in the grey mist.

Across the narrow sea, in the Free Cities and the vast grasslands of the Dothraki sea, a different story was unfolding—one that began with fear and ended with fire. Daenerys Targaryen was thirteen years old when her brother Viserys sold her to Khal Drogo for the promise of an army. Viserys was the last male heir of House Targaryen, the dynasty that had ruled the Seven Kingdoms for nearly three centuries before Robert's Rebellion toppled the Mad King and drove the surviving Targaryens into exile. He was a cruel, petulant, half-mad boy who called himself king and treated his sister as chattel, and he traded her maidenhead for forty thousand Dothraki screamers who would cross the sea and win back his father's throne.

Daenerys wept on her wedding night. She was a girl far from home in a land she did not know, wed to a man whose language she could not speak, surrounded by a people whose ways were savage and strange. Drogo was huge and powerful and terrifying, and for those first nights the bedding was a thing she endured rather than desired. But Daenerys was the blood of the dragon, and the dragon does not weep forever. She learned to ride as the Dothraki rode, not sitting her horse but becoming part of it. She learned their tongue. She learned to meet Drogo not as a frightened bride but as a khaleesi, and in time she found something like love with this man who was fierce and proud and gentle in his own rough way. Ser Jorah Mormont, a disgraced knight of Westeros who served as her advisor, watched the girl transform into a queen and knew, with the mingled wonder and despair of a man falling in love with something he could never have, that there was more to Daenerys Stormborn than anyone had guessed.

Illyrio Mopatis, the fat Pentoshi merchant who had sheltered the Targaryen children, gave Daenerys three dragon eggs as a wedding gift—petrified, he said, turned to stone over the centuries, beautiful but nothing more than curiosities. Daenerys felt the warmth in them. She dreamed of fire.

Viserys grew more desperate and more dangerous as the khalasar rode east instead of west, deeper into the Dothraki sea rather than toward the ships that would carry them to Westeros. He had been promised a crown, and he meant to have it. At Vaes Dothrak, the sacred city of the horse lords where no blade might be drawn, Viserys committed the ultimate folly. Drunk and raving, he stormed into the feast hall and pressed a sword to Daenerys's swollen belly—she was carrying Drogo's child—and demanded his crown. Drogo gave him one. He melted gold in a cooking pot and poured it over Viserys's head, and the last son of the Mad King died screaming with molten gold running down his face. "He was no dragon," Daenerys said, watching her brother die without flinching. "Fire cannot kill a dragon."

The assassination attempt came later, in a market town where a wineseller tried to poison Daenerys with a cask of fine Arbor gold. It was Ser Jorah who saved her, recognizing the trap, and the fury that seized Khal Drogo when he learned that the men across the sea had tried to murder his wife and his unborn son was a terrible thing to witness. He swore then that he would cross the poison water, that he would take the iron chair, that he would give his son the Seven Kingdoms. He began raiding the Lhazareen settlements for slaves to sell for ships, and it was in one of those raids that everything came apart.

A Lhazareen godswife named Mirri Maz Duur was among the captives, and Daenerys, moved by pity, took the woman under her protection. When Drogo was wounded in a fight with one of his own riders—a festering cut that turned septic—Daenerys begged Mirri Maz Duur to save him. The godswife agreed, and her price was terrible. She needed death to pay for life, she said, and the shadows danced in the tent where she worked her blood magic, and Drogo's stallion screamed as its throat was cut, and when Daenerys stumbled into the tent despite all warnings, the cost was her unborn son. Rhaego was born dead, his body scaled and winged and monstrous, and Drogo lived—if what remained could be called living. He breathed. His eyes were open. But the man was gone, and nothing Daenerys could do would bring him back.

Mirri Maz Duur did not pretend remorse. She had watched the Dothraki burn her temple and rape her people. She had saved Daenerys from rape and received only more violation for her trouble—she had been raped three times before the khaleesi found her. "Look to your khal and see what life is worth, when all the rest is gone," she told Daenerys, and there was a savage satisfaction in her voice that Daenerys understood even as she hated it. She held a pillow over Drogo's face and wept while the last breath left him, and then she built a pyre.

The khalasar had shattered. Most of the riders had followed other khals, taking the herds and the slaves and leaving Daenerys with the old, the sick, the young, and the faithful few. Ser Jorah begged her not to do what she meant to do. He thought she meant to die, and perhaps he was right, in a way—part of her did die that night, and something else was born. She placed the three dragon eggs upon Drogo's pyre. She bound Mirri Maz Duur to the wood and lit the fire, and the godswife's screams rose with the flames and the sparks and the smoke. And then Daenerys Stormborn walked into the fire.

When dawn came over the Dothraki sea, the fire had burned down to ash and embers, and there in the center of the pyre sat Daenerys, naked and unburned, her silver-gold hair gone but her skin untouched by flame, and on her shoulder crouched a dragon, cream-and-gold, no bigger than a cat, with wings like translucent parchment. Another clung to her arm, green as jade. The third, black as midnight with eyes like smoldering coals, nuzzled against her breast. The first dragons born in over a hundred years, born of fire and blood and a girl's terrible grief. Ser Jorah went to his knees. The Dothraki who remained knelt with him, every one, and the world was changed.

Three great threads, then, woven through the first volume of a tale that would shake the foundations of a world. In King's Landing, the game of thrones was played and an honorable man paid the price for believing that truth and justice still held meaning in a court built on lies. In the north, a boy king rode to war while another boy took an oath to guard the realms of men against an enemy that most of those realms did not believe existed. And in the east, a girl who had been sold like a broodmare to a savage lord found in herself the fire that had built an empire three hundred years ago, and brought dragons back into the world.

The realm bled. Lord Tywin Lannister held the Riverlands in an iron fist, his armies ravaging the countryside, his gold buying what his steel could not take. Stannis Baratheon, Robert's younger brother, brooded on Dragonstone, preparing his own claim to the throne with the truth of Joffrey's parentage—for Ned had sent him word before the end. Renly Baratheon, the youngest brother, beloved and charismatic, gathered the chivalry of the south around him at Highgarden and declared himself king. The realm had five kings now, or would have soon—Joffrey on the Iron Throne, Robb in the north, Stannis on Dragonstone, Renly in the south, and Balon Greyjoy on the Iron Islands, whose ambitions had not yet declared themselves but were stirring in the salt spray. The War of the Five Kings, they would call it, though war is too clean a word for what was coming—a breaking of the world, a feast for crows.

And through it all, like a drumbeat beneath the noise of politics and battle, came the whisper from beyond the Wall: winter is coming. The Others were real. The dead were walking. The Long Night that the histories spoke of—eight thousand years of darkness and cold, when the White Walkers rode their dead horses through a world of ice—was coming again, and all the kings and queens and lords and ladies of the Seven Kingdoms were so busy fighting over a chair made of swords that none of them heard the warning until it was too late.

Sansa, imprisoned in silk and courtesy, learned to hide her tears and guard her tongue and survive in a court that wanted her broken. She learned that the songs she had loved were lies, that knights were not always noble and princes were not always kind, and she learned it the hardest way possible—one blow at a time, one humiliation at a time, one severed head at a time. Arya, lost in the streets of King's Landing, was found by a Night's Watch recruiter named Yoren, a hard man in black who recognized Ned Stark's daughter and cut her hair and told her she was a boy now, a boy named Arry, headed north to the Wall where no Lannister's writ ran. She clutched her thin sword Needle—Jon's parting gift, castle-forged steel made for a girl's hand—and she whispered the names of the people she would kill someday, a prayer of hatred that grew longer with every mile.

Jon Snow understood something that perhaps no one else in the Seven Kingdoms yet understood: the war that mattered was not the war for the Iron Throne. The war that mattered was the one being fought in the dark, beyond the edge of the maps, where the dead stirred in their icy graves and the cold was not just weather but a weapon wielded by something ancient and malevolent and patient beyond human reckoning. The Old Bear understood it too, which was why he was marching north with every man he could spare, leaving the Wall undermanned and the realm unwarned, because someone had to go and look the enemy in the face and find out what was coming before it was too late.

And in the smoking ruins of Drogo's pyre, Daenerys Targaryen nursed her dragons and looked west toward a throne she had never seen and a kingdom she had never known, and she was afraid and she was alone and she was the most dangerous person in the world.

The great wheel was turning. The game had begun. In the game of thrones, Cersei had warned Ned Stark, you win or you die. There is no middle ground. Ned had not believed her. Ned was dead. The living would have to learn the lesson for themselves, and the tuition would be paid in blood—oceans of it, rivers of it, enough blood to drown the world. Winter was coming, and with it the longest night, and when the snows fell and the white winds blew, the lone wolf died but the pack survived. If the pack could find each other. If the pack could hold together. If the pack could remember what it was, scattered across a thousand leagues of war-torn countryside, with enemies on every side and the cold coming down from the north like the judgment of the old gods.

The Stark words were a promise and a warning both, and neither had yet been fulfilled. The Lannister words were "Hear Me Roar," but the words they lived by were different: a Lannister always pays his debts. The Targaryens had no official words at all, only the ancient command of Aegon the Conqueror, carved in Valyrian steel: fire and blood. Before the story was done, all three houses would make good on their words, and the world would burn and freeze and bleed, and the iron throne would claim more lives than any war in the history of the Seven Kingdoms.

But that is a tale for another day, another telling. This much is certain: when the snows began to fall and the nights grew longer, the children born in the long summer would shiver and shake and wonder if the warmth would ever return. The old men would remember and the young men would learn and the dead would rise, and in the east, a girl with silver hair and three small dragons would begin the long march west, toward a destiny written in fire. The game of thrones is never truly won. It is only survived, for a time, by those ruthless enough or lucky enough or stubborn enough to outlast their enemies. And in the end, when the last king falls and the last castle burns, there is only the land, and the winter, and the wolves howling in the night.
